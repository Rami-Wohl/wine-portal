Alcoholic fermentation is the stage at which must becomes wine: yeast cells consume sugars and chiefly form alcohol and carbon dioxide, while heat and many aromatic by-products are produced. The process is natural, but reliable fermentation requires active observation and suitable conditions.
:::

:::objectives{#leerdoelen depth="foundation"}
- Describe the conversion of sugar into alcohol, carbon dioxide and heat.
- Explain the basic roles of yeast, temperature and fermentation vessel.
- Distinguish fermenting dry, deliberate arrest and an unintended stuck fermentation.
:::

:::section{#suiker-wordt-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation"}
## From sugar to alcohol

Yeasts are microorganisms that can convert grape sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide. The conversion also produces heat and a range of compounds that may contribute to aroma and texture. Escaping carbon dioxide explains the lively surface of an active fermentation; for ordinary still wine, most of that gas is allowed to leave.

As yeast consumes sugar, liquid density falls and alcohol rises. A winemaker therefore follows more than visible bubbles, measuring factors such as density or sugar concentration and temperature. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation]

:::section{#gist-en-start depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-yeast-choice"}
## Yeast and the start of fermentation

Yeast cells are already present on grapes and in the winery environment. A winemaker may allow a spontaneous population to develop or add a selected yeast culture. A selected strain offers greater predictability, but does not determine wine character on its own; must composition, temperature, nutrition and later cellar decisions still matter.

Yeast needs a suitable environment to establish a healthy population. Insufficient available nutrients, unsuitable temperature, high sugar concentration or inhibitory compounds can make that start more difficult. [@source.awri-yeast-choice]
:::

:::section{#temperatuur-en-vat depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-fermentation-temperature"}
## Temperature and fermentation vessel

Fermentation generates heat. Without cooling, a large tank can therefore become warmer than the surrounding cellar. Temperature affects yeast activity, process speed and which volatile aroma compounds are retained or formed. Cooling, or choosing a warmer fermentation, is consequently both a style and process decision within safe limits.

Stainless steel makes cooling and cleaning straightforward; concrete and wood have different thermal and oxygen properties. Vessel shape and size also influence heat loss and, in red wine, contact with the cap of skins. No material occupies the highest rung for every wine.
:::

:::section{#volgen-en-bijsturen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013"}
## Monitoring a healthy fermentation

Regular measurements show whether sugar is disappearing at an appropriate rate. Tasting and smelling help identify unusual aromas early. During skin fermentation, the winemaker also monitors temperature and the condition of the floating mass of skins.

If the rate falls unexpectedly, the cause is investigated first: temperature, nutrient conditions, alcohol, yeast health or another inhibition. Simply applying more heat or adding nutrients blindly can move a problem rather than solve it. [@source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013]
:::

:::section{#droog-stoppen-vastlopen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-fermentation-interruption source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013"}
## Fermenting dry, stopping deliberately or becoming stuck

A dry wine results when almost all fermentable sugar has been consumed. For a wine with residual sugar, a winemaker may deliberately interrupt fermentation, for example through cooling and separation from yeast followed by suitable stabilisation. That is a planned endpoint. [@source.oiv-fermentation-interruption]

A **stuck fermentation** is unintended: yeast stops while fermentable sugar remains. This increases microbiological risk and calls for diagnosis and recovery. The same measurement—sugar remains—can therefore describe either a deliberate style decision or a process failure.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
How much sugar is consumed helps determine dryness and alcohol. Yeast strain, temperature, vessel and fermentation course can influence aroma and texture, but always within the must's composition. The next lesson adds another central distinction: does the liquid ferment with or without skins?
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Fermentation is biological conversion under winery management. The winemaker does not make alcohol directly, but creates and monitors the conditions in which yeast can complete an appropriate conversion.
:::
