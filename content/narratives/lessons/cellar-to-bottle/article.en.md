A market-ready wine does not appear automatically when maturation ends. Lots are selected and perhaps combined; clarity and stability are assessed; bottling must then carry the intended wine cleanly and with as little unwanted change as possible into a sealed package.
:::

:::objectives{#leerdoelen depth="foundation"}
- Explain blending as assembly from possible grapes, parcels, vessels, moments or vintages.
- Describe the purpose of clarification, stabilisation and filtration without making every method compulsory.
- Place bottling as a critical transfer into a sealed package.
- Summarise the complete route as a branching decision model rather than one recipe.

:::section{#assemblage depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-blending-code"}
## Blending turns components into one wine

During [[concept.assemblage|blending]], a winemaker tastes possible components and decides in which proportions they form the final wine. Components may come from different grape varieties, parcels, press fractions, fermentation vessels, maturation vessels or production moments. Some wine styles can also combine vintages.

The process often begins with small trial blends. The winemaker seeks coherence in fruit, acidity, tannin, alcohol, aroma and texture, then checks whether the chosen proportions retain the same purpose at full scale. Blending can build complexity or consistency, but it cannot magically transform a weak component.
:::

:::section{#helderheid-en-klaring depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-fining-agents source.oiv-fining-practices"}
## Clarity, settling and fining

During rest, yeast and other particles may settle; racking separates clearer wine from that deposit. When natural settling is insufficient or a specific problem needs treatment, [[concept.clarification-and-fining|fining]] may be used. A processing aid binds selected components, after which the resulting material is removed.

The objective may be clarity, protein stability or reduction of excessive bitterness or astringency. Bench trials help select an appropriate agent and the lowest effective dose, because excessive fining can also remove desirable colour, aroma or structure. [@source.oiv-fining-practices]
:::

:::section{#stabilisatie-en-filtratie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-filtration-wine source.awri-pre-packaging source.awri-microbiological-stability"}
## Stabilisation and filtration address different risks

A wine can look clear yet later become hazy, form crystals, ferment again or change microbiologically. **Stabilisation** is therefore an umbrella objective: preparing wine so that foreseeable physical, chemical or microbiological changes are controlled for its intended storage.

Filtration passes wine through a medium or membrane that retains particles. Coarser filtration supports clarity; suitable membrane filtration can remove microorganisms before bottling. Not every wine needs the same filtration, but residual sugar, unstable microbiology and long distribution increase the need for assurance. [@source.oiv-filtration-wine]
:::

:::section{#bottelen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-pre-packaging source.awri-sulfur-dioxide"}
## Bottling closes the winery route

During [[concept.bottling|bottling]], finished wine passes through clean hoses and filling equipment into a clean bottle before a closure is applied. Temperature, fill height and closure need to match. Hygiene remains critical because contamination at this final stage cannot be corrected by another winery operation.

Pumping, filtration and filling can introduce oxygen. Air contact and headspace are therefore managed and the protective condition of the wine is checked around bottling. [[concept.sulfur-dioxide|Free SO₂]] can decline during the operation and needs to be assessed in relation to pH, style and storage aim. [@source.awri-sulfur-dioxide]
:::

:::section{#hele-route depth="foundation"}
## The complete route from grape to bottle

The path began with grapes that already carried sugar, acids, skins, seeds, aroma and a particular condition. Sorting, destemming, crushing and pressing determined which parts continued together. Yeast converted sugar; skin contact and extraction made white, rosé and red into distinct principal routes.

Malolactic conversion, lees contact, vessel, time and oxygen then changed the young product further. Blending and preparation carried selected components as one stable wine to the bottling line. Each stage both limited and opened choices for the next.
:::

:::comparison{#beslismodel depth="foundation"}
## One process model with branches

| Stage | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Grape and reception | What raw material enters the winery? |
| Separation and pressing | Which grape parts continue together? |
| Fermentation | How is sugar reliably converted into alcohol? |
| Skin contact | When and how are colour and structure extracted? |
| After fermentation | Which conversion, lees contact and separation follow? |
| Maturation | How do vessel, time and oxygen develop and protect the wine? |
| Finishing and bottle | Which composition and preparation can be packaged stably? |

This model is a way to organise winery decisions. It is not a recipe in which every wine follows every stage in the same way.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
The final wine carries traces of the whole route. Blending can shape balance; clarification and filtration can protect clarity and stability; careful bottling helps retain fruit, colour and texture. The glass can prompt plausible process questions, but reconstructing the route from taste alone remains uncertain.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
From grape to bottle, winemaking consists of connected decisions. Each stage works with the material left by the previous one and prepares the conditions for the next.
:::
