:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation"}
A vine experiences its environment at different scales: from the long-term climate to the air around a single bunch on a warm afternoon. Learn to distinguish these scales and choose the observations needed to answer a specific vineyard question.
:::

:::objectives{#leerdoelen depth="foundation"}
- Distinguish a climate pattern from a weather event.
- Connect heat, light, moisture and air movement with what the plant experiences.
- Choose appropriate local observations before assessing a vineyard intervention.
:::

:::section{#patroon-en-gebeurtenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wmo-climatological-normals"}
## Which time window fits your question?

**Climate** describes patterns in conditions over longer periods. A climate normal can provide a reference for usual monthly temperatures or rainfall, for example. WMO standard normals use thirty-year periods. Such an average helps place a season against a long-term reference. [@source.wmo-climatological-normals]

**Weather** concerns specific conditions at a particular time: rain during flowering, a cold night or a hot afternoon. To ask which conditions recur regularly, use a long series of observations. To ask what the vine experienced yesterday, look at that event. The [[concept.climate-weather-site-microclimate|overview of climate, weather and microclimate]] connects these time windows with different spatial scales.
:::

:::section{#wat-de-plant-ontmoet depth="foundation" source_refs="source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020"}
## Heat, light and water work together

Temperature influences the rate of many developmental processes. Light provides energy for photosynthesis and influences the exposure of buds, leaves and bunches. These conditions gain meaning alongside the [[concept.grapevine-phenology|developmental stage]]: emerging shoots, flowers and ripening berries belong to different parts of the growing season. [@source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology]

The air around a leaf matters too. Warm, dry air can create strong evaporative demand; air movement changes exchange between the leaf and its surroundings. Actual water loss also depends on the stomata, the small adjustable pores in the leaf, and on the water supply. The plant thus connects the atmosphere with the root zone. [[concept.vine-water-relations|Grapevine water relations]] explain this connection further. [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020]
:::

:::section{#twee-plekken-een-vraag depth="foundation" source_refs="source.pena-vineyard-canopy-temperature-2020"}
## Two places, the same forecast

Imagine two fictional vineyard sections. In **A**, bunches are relatively exposed, receiving direct afternoon light. In **B**, they sit behind a denser layer of leaves, in a more sheltered position. Variety and developmental stage are the same for this exercise; we know little yet about soil water or plant condition. Both sections share the same regional weather forecast.

To ask “should we remove more leaves around the bunches?” we still need important information. How long does direct light reach the grapes? What happens to temperature and moisture around the bunch? How does the plant respond during warm hours?

A measurement study in a [[grape.chardonnay|Chardonnay]] vineyard found differences between air measurements inside the canopy and at a nearby standard weather station. This supports taking local measurements too. The differences recorded in that trial do not provide a fixed temperature correction for our fictional sites. [@source.pena-vineyard-canopy-temperature-2020]
:::

:::section{#van-observatie-naar-keuze depth="foundation" source_refs="source.pena-vineyard-canopy-temperature-2020 source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020"}
## What additional information would you request?

A reasoned answer to the exercise compares observations **at the same relevant times**, using comparable sensor setups. Record whether you are measuring air in the bunch zone, free air at a station or something else. Also note exposure, leaf cover and developmental stage. This gives any difference an interpretable context. [@source.pena-vineyard-canopy-temperature-2020]

Add the vine's condition. If water supply and evaporative demand fall out of balance, stomata may close, altering both water loss and carbon dioxide uptake. Greater exposure therefore needs assessing alongside water availability and plant response. For A and B, observation is a better supported next step than removing the same amount of leaf cover everywhere. [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020]
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
To connect a difference in grape ripeness with the site, also collect information about the grapes themselves: their developmental progress, composition, health and harvest timing. The [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|reference on ripeness]] helps organise these observations. For the finished wine, [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]], [[concept.extraction|extraction]] and [[concept.elevage|cellar maturation]] enter the picture too. A warmer forecast alone is too narrow a basis for attributing a specific flavour to A or B.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Choose the scale that fits your question. Long-term data provide a reference; local observations reveal what a particular vine encounters during this season.
:::
