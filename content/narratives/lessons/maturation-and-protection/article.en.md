During maturation, a winemaker guides young wine towards bottling. Vessel, time, temperature, lees, racking, oxygen and protection work together. Maturation is therefore not a pause after winemaking, but a stage in which development is allowed and unwanted change is bounded.
:::

:::section{#elevage-als-begeleiding depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-pre-packaging"}
## Maturation guides young wine

[[concept.elevage|Maturation, or élevage]], covers the period and operations between fermentation and final bottling. Wine may rest, form sediment, be racked, blended or protected. The winemaker follows how aroma, acidity, tannin and texture develop into a coherent whole.

Duration does not follow prestige. A fresh aromatic wine may benefit from short storage in a neutral tank; a structured wine may need more time for components to integrate. Waiting too long can instead cost fruit and energy.
:::

:::section{#tank-beton-en-hout depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-oak-maturation source.wset-oak-winemaking"}
## Tank, concrete and wood serve different functions

Stainless steel seals well, is easy to clean and permits precise temperature control. Concrete has thermal mass and, depending on its finish and shape, may offer a different pattern of gas exchange and wine movement. Wooden vessels allow limited oxygen transfer and may contribute wood compounds.

Old oak generally supplies fewer recognisable oak aromas than new oak, but remains a maturation vessel. Size changes the ratio of wine volume to vessel surface: a small barrel provides more contact per litre than a large foudre. Material, age and size are therefore separate variables.
:::

## New oak, toast and contact time

New oak can release compounds associated with vanilla, spice, toast, smoke or coconut-like notes. Wood origin and species, seasoning, cooper and toast level influence what is available. The wine then determines how those compounds are taken up and perceived.

A higher proportion of new oak or longer contact is not an automatic improvement. Oak can support structure or obscure fruit. Winemakers may therefore combine new and used barrels, different sizes, or cask and tank to arrive at a suitable whole.
:::

:::section{#zuurstof-beheren depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wine-australia-oxygen-style source.awri-wine-faults"}
## Allowing and limiting oxygen

Small, controlled amounts of oxygen can support reactions that change colour and tannin structure during maturation. Racking, bâtonnage, vessel material and empty headspace affect exposure. Without control, the same oxygen can encourage accelerated ageing, browning and loss of fresh aroma.

[[concept.oxidation|Oxidation]] is therefore not simply present or absent. The winemaker decides where contact is useful, keeps vessels properly closed, tops up space created by evaporation and limits air during vulnerable transfers.
:::

:::section{#so2-en-hygiene depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-sulphiting-wine source.awri-sulfur-dioxide"}
## Sulfur dioxide, temperature and hygiene

[[concept.sulfur-dioxide|Sulfur dioxide]] helps protect wine from oxidative reactions and unwanted yeasts and bacteria. The free fraction is chiefly active; pH and wine composition affect how much protection a measured concentration provides. Values are therefore monitored and adjusted to the particular wine. [@source.awri-sulfur-dioxide]

SO₂ does not operate alone. Clean equipment, suitable temperature, full and well-sealed vessels and rapid response to unusual aroma or activity reduce the pressure on any individual protective measure.
:::

:::comparison{#vatkeuze-zonder-ladder depth="foundation"}
## Vessel choice without a quality ladder

| Option | What it can principally offer |
| --- | --- |
| Stainless steel | Neutrality, sealing and temperature control |
| Concrete | Thermal stability and its own pattern of movement and gas exchange |
| Used oak | Limited oxygen transfer with little new oak aroma |
| New oak | Oxygen transfer plus noticeable wood compounds |

The appropriate choice follows from the wine and intended style. Producers can combine materials or move wine during maturation.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
Maturation can preserve or shift primary fruit expression, add oak aroma and allow texture to integrate. Once the intended balance and stability have been reached, the final design stage follows: assembling lots and preparing the wine for bottling.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Maturation is a managed balance between development and protection. Vessel, time and oxygen gain meaning only together with the wine they contain and the controls around them.
:::
