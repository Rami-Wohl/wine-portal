:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation"}
A grower shapes a vine over several years, then adjusts its growth each season. Pruning selects the buds that remain; work on shoots, leaves and bunches changes what develops from them. Learn to distinguish these interventions and assess them through the plant, the timing and the intended result.
:::

:::objectives{#leerdoelen depth="foundation"}
- Distinguish training, dormant pruning, canopy work and cluster thinning.
- Explain why light, airflow and protection all matter around a bunch.
- Assess crop load against the vine’s capacity and identify an observation still needed.
:::

:::section{#vorm-en-vertrekpunt depth="foundation" source_refs="source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022"}
## What do you establish before leaves appear?

The trunk and any permanent arms give the [[concept.vine-as-living-system|vine]] its structure over several years. Developing and maintaining that structure is called **training**. Posts and wires support it and the new growth. A horizontal, permanent arm is a **cordon**. [@source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022]

Last summer’s shoots have become one-year-old wood by winter. During **dormant pruning**, the grower chooses which wood and buds to retain. Those buds can produce new shoots bearing inflorescences. **Spur pruning** retains short pieces of one-year-old wood; **cane pruning** retains longer one-year-old canes. Bud fruitfulness varies with factors including variety and position. The reference on [[concept.pruning-training-canopy-crop-load|pruning and canopy management]] explores these choices further. [@source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022]

Pruning therefore sets a starting point for shoots and fruit. The eventual bunch number and harvest also depend on events during the [[concept.grapevine-phenology|annual cycle]].
:::

:::figure{#werk-door-het-jaar depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.pruning-training-canopy-crop-load.annual-decision-chain"}
**Different interventions at different times**

1. **Before pruning:** the permanent structure carries one-year-old wood from the previous season.
2. **Dormant pruning:** selected wood and buds are retained for new growth.
3. **Shoot work:** new shoots are selected and distributed through the available space.
4. **Summer work:** shoots and leaves are managed; the bunches here retain some shade.
5. **Assessment:** foliage, growth and fruit are considered together to follow crop load and ripening.

The illustration explains the sequence. It gives neither cutting instructions nor an ideal bud or bunch count. The loose bunches beside the final scene do not mean that thinning is always needed.
:::

:::section{#groen-werk depth="foundation" source_refs="source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022 source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021 source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024"}
## Which part of growth are you changing?

The **canopy** consists of shoots and leaves. Shoot thinning changes shoot number; positioning distributes shoots through space. Removing leaves around bunches mainly changes their exposure and airflow. These are different ways of addressing a dense canopy. [@source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022] [@source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021]

**Cluster thinning** removes fruit. Bunch number falls, but this does not automatically provide more healthy, well-lit foliage. First establishing whether the problem concerns shoot distribution, leaf function or crop load helps identify an appropriate intervention. [@source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024]
:::

:::section{#licht-en-bescherming depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021"}
## How much openness helps a bunch?

A more open fruit zone can improve drying and light access. Leaves also protect berries against intense radiation and heat. Leaf removal changes several conditions at once. Understanding the [[concept.climate-weather-site-microclimate|microclimate around the bunch]] helps establish which change is desirable. [@source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021]

Timing matters: berries exposed gradually to light respond differently from berries suddenly uncovered after developing in shade. Late, severe leaf removal can increase sunburn risk. Consider the shaded side, the developmental stage and the weather forecast too. [@source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021]
:::

:::section{#fruit-en-draagkracht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024"}
## When is this vine carrying a lot of fruit?

**Crop load** concerns fruit relative to the vine’s capacity, including its functioning leaf area. Yield per hectare does not tell you how much well-lit foliage each vine has. Research on cluster thinning also shows varying effects on fruit composition; variety and the severity of the intervention are among the relevant factors. [@source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024]

Follow leaf function, growth and [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|ripening]] alongside bunch number. This helps investigate suspected overcropping. Removing fruit alone does not demonstrate a gain in quality.
:::

:::section{#probeer-het-zelf depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021"}
## Remove every leaf before a hot week?

In a hypothetical vineyard, bunches remain damp for a long time in a dense fruit zone. The berries have begun ripening and a hot, sunny week is forecast. Someone proposes removing all surrounding leaves on both sides. What trade-off is missing?

A reasoned answer considers both **drying and protection against sudden exposure**. First investigate shoot distribution, leaf layers, sunlight on both sides and the fruit’s current condition. Late leaf removal combined with intense exposure can favour sunburn. [@source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021] “More open” is therefore too broad a goal: the intended change needs to account for place and timing.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021 source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024"}
Canopy and crop load can alter grape exposure, health and composition. [@source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021] [@source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024] To explain a wine, follow the harvested fruit and the winemaking choices that follow. A low yield figure gains meaning alongside that information. [[concept.vine-nutrition-soil-management-vineyard-floor|Nutrition and vineyard floor management]] also help explain the plant’s growth.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Connect each intervention to what it changes: structure, buds, shoots, leaves or fruit. Then assess whether the vine and bunches show the intended change at this particular time.
:::
