:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation"}
A bunch of grapes grows on a plant with a history. As new shoots and fruit develop, roots and wood continue to support the vine. This lesson follows what returns each year, what grows afresh and how this year's crop connects with the following season.
:::

:::objectives{#leerdoelen depth="foundation"}
- Recognise the root, trunk, shoot, bud, leaf and bunch and describe their functions.
- Put the vine's main developmental stages in order.
- Explain how buds and reserves connect successive growing seasons.
:::

:::section{#blijvend-en-nieuw depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-roots source.cornell-grapevine-flowers"}
## What remains, and what grows afresh?

Start with the permanent framework. Woody roots anchor the vine and connect finer roots with the trunk. Roots and permanent wood can also store reserves. Young, fine root regions carry out much of the uptake of water and dissolved mineral nutrients. [@source.cornell-grapevine-roots]

Above ground, buds produce new **shoots**: initially green stems bearing leaves and sometimes inflorescences. An inflorescence is a group of small flowers from which grapes can develop after fruit set. The bunch therefore hangs on this season's growth. Buds capable of producing future shoots are already being formed during that growth. [@source.cornell-grapevine-flowers]
:::

:::figure{#plant-in-beeld depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.vine-as-living-system.vine-anatomy-cutaway"}
**Reading the vine**

1. **Fine roots:** contact with soil water and dissolved nutrients.
2. **Woody roots:** anchorage, connection and storage.
3. **Trunk and permanent wood:** the framework that lasts for several years.
4. **Shoot:** new growth bearing leaves and reproductive organs.
5. **Bud:** developing structures for later growth.
6. **Leaf:** intercepts light and exchanges gases.
7. **Inflorescence:** small flowers, before their development into grapes.
8. **Bunch:** berries that need supplies as they develop.

The illustration combines different developmental moments. Flowers and ripe grapes appear together to help identify organs; the plate is neither a snapshot nor a measurement of rooting depth.
:::

:::section{#groei-vraagt-aanvoer depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-roots source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology"}
## How does new growth obtain materials?

A growing plant needs water, mineral nutrients and carbon compounds. Leaves use light energy to incorporate carbon dioxide from the air into organic substances, including sugars. This process is called **photosynthesis**. The plant uses the resulting material for growth and maintenance and can store some of it. Early shoot growth draws partly on reserves while the new canopy is still small. [@source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology]

Water from the root zone reaches above-ground tissues through the **xylem**, the water-conducting tissue. Evaporation from leaves helps drive that flow. Roots, leaves and bunches therefore function as parts of one [[concept.vine-as-living-system|living system]]: growth needs both uptake from the soil and production by the leaves. [@source.cornell-grapevine-roots]
:::

:::section{#van-knop-tot-oogst depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-grapevine-growth-stages"}
## From budburst to ripe grapes

[[concept.grapevine-phenology|Phenology]] describes recognisable developmental stages. Read the sequence below as an order of changes; their dates and duration vary with variety, place and season.

1. **Budburst:** a winter bud opens and the first leaf tips become visible.
2. **Shoot growth:** new leaves unfold and inflorescences become recognisable.
3. **Flowering and fruit set:** flowers open; subsequently, berries that have set develop further.
4. **Green berry growth:** berries enlarge and are initially hard and green.
5. **Véraison:** berries begin to soften and change colour. This marks the start of ripening.
6. **Ripening and harvest:** grapes develop further until the chosen harvest time.

These stages overlap within a vine and vineyard. Shoots may still be growing while berries develop, for example. Véraison and harvest ripeness are separate steps; [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|berry development and ripeness]] explore this distinction further. [@source.awri-grapevine-growth-stages]
:::

:::section{#volgend-jaar-begint-nu depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-flowers source.pellegrino-vine-carbohydrate-status-2014 source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology"}
## Why harvest does not end the vine's year

While the current bunches grow, buds develop for the following season. Structures for future inflorescences can already begin to form inside them. A crop therefore has a history extending across two growing seasons. What eventually bursts, flowers and sets fruit also depends on subsequent conditions. [@source.cornell-grapevine-flowers]

Carbon reserves change through the season too. Healthy, active leaves can still contribute to replenishing reserves after harvest, while conditions allow photosynthesis. Later, leaves age and fall, while lignified shoots and winter buds become part of the dormant vine. The amount of reserve replenishment depends on factors including leaf function, water availability and the plant's demands. [@source.pellegrino-vine-carbohydrate-status-2014]
:::

:::section{#probeer-het-zelf depth="foundation"}
## What would you ask about early leaf loss?

Imagine two otherwise comparable vines, both just harvested. One still has active green leaves; the other has lost its leaves early. What information would you collect to understand the start of next season?

A useful answer begins with **the timing, cause and duration of leaf loss**, and the plant's condition beforehand. The first vine still has an opportunity to photosynthesise; the second lacks that leaf function. This makes reserve replenishment a question to investigate. Neither the illustration nor a single observation tells you the size of the reserves or how many grapes will grow next year.
:::

The vine's season provides the background to the grapes arriving at the cellar. Faced with a question about their ripeness or composition, you can now first investigate how leaf function, water supply and developmental stage interacted. Then follow harvest timing and winemaking to understand how that raw material became wine. An image of a healthy vine alone does not explain the flavour of the finished wine.

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Each year the vine builds new growth on a permanent framework. Buds and reserves connect this year's crop with growth in the following season.
:::
