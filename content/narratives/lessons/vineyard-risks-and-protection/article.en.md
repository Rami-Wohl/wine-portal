:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation"}
A dried shoot, yellow leaf or damaged berry is an observation. To protect a vineyard effectively, you also need to understand what caused the damage and how the risk develops. Learn to organise possible causes and move from an initial signal to a well-supported next step.
:::

:::objectives{#leerdoelen depth="foundation"}
- Distinguish weather and physical damage, infectious disease, pests and physiological disorders.
- Gather appropriate information before treating a damage pattern as a diagnosis.
- Connect prevention, a targeted measure and reassessment with the intended fruit.
:::

## Which kinds of causes should you investigate?

An initial grouping helps you ask different questions:

- **Weather and physical damage:** for example frost, heat or a broken shoot. The event and developmental stage help explain which organ was affected.
- **Infectious disease:** a pathogen, such as a fungus or bacterium, infects the plant. Conditions and susceptibility influence its course.
- **Pests:** a harmful animal may damage tissue, for example. Some insects also transmit a pathogen; such a carrier is called a **vector**.
- **Physiological disorders:** plant function is disrupted, for example through limited oxygen around roots or a nutritional problem.

This grouping follows the connections between plant, environment and possible harmful organisms. [@source.oiv-plant-protection-good-practices-2018] [@source.uc-ipm-pierces-disease] [@source.awri-waterlogged-vineyards] [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024] Causes can also occur together. The [[concept.vineyard-hazards-diseases-pests-disorders|vineyard risks reference]] explores them further.
:::

## What do you need to know beyond the damaged leaf?

A **symptom** is a visible response, such as discolouration or wilting. Different causes can produce similar symptoms. UC IPM describes, for example, how symptoms of Pierce’s disease can be confused with other problems and how laboratory testing can help when the cause is uncertain. [@source.uc-ipm-pierces-disease]

Record which organ is affected, when it began and where it occurs: one vine, scattered patches or a continuous area? Compare healthy plants and investigate recent weather and management. A suspected [[concept.vine-nutrition-soil-management-vineyard-floor|nutrient deficiency]] needs that context too. [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024] Then form a **hypothesis**, a possible explanation that still needs testing.
:::

:::section{#beschermen-en-controleren depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-plant-protection-good-practices-2018 source.inrae-ephytia-phylloxera"}
## How does protection become a targeted choice?

Integrated management combines prevention, observation and appropriate measures. Healthy planting material and suitable vineyard design provide a starting point; field observations, weather and developmental stage then help track risk. Choose a measure suited to the likely cause and reassess afterwards. The OIV guide also connects these choices with limiting impacts on people and the environment. [@source.oiv-plant-protection-good-practices-2018]

A [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]] shows how protection can begin before planting. Grafting European vines onto resistant rootstocks provided an important solution to root damage caused by [[concept.phylloxera|phylloxera]]. [@source.inrae-ephytia-phylloxera] That targeted protection explains a choice of plant material; it does not make the vine immune to every other problem.

For an actual intervention, local expertise and, where applicable, current authorisations and labels belong to implementation. Here the focus is the decision process: what do you want to prevent or limit, why should this measure help and how will you observe the result?
:::

:::section{#probeer-het-zelf depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-waterlogged-vineyards source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024"}
## Yellow leaves in a wet hollow: feed immediately?

In a hypothetical block, yellow leaves appear in a low area after a prolonged wet period. Elsewhere, the vines grow normally. Someone proposes adding nutrients there. Which question comes first?

Investigate **the root environment and the explanation for the pattern**. How long does the soil remain saturated, how does drainage work and what condition are the roots in? Prolonged saturation can limit oxygen supply and root function. [@source.awri-waterlogged-vineyards] Leaf colour and location make this a question to investigate, not a certain diagnosis. Combine plant and soil investigation where needed; colour alone does not justify fertilisation. [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024] The [[concept.vine-water-relations|water relations reference]] helps explain the mechanism further.
:::

:::section{#fruit-en-wijndoel depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vaczy-noble-grey-rot-2024"}
## Why does the intended fruit also matter?

With [[concept.botrytis|botrytis]], the same fungal species can cause unwanted grey rot or, under specific conditions, noble rot. Noble rot can be useful for certain [[concept.sweet-wines|sweet wines]]; persistently unfavourable conditions can instead lead to fruit loss. [@source.vaczy-noble-grey-rot-2024] The intended product and the berries’ actual condition therefore help determine how you assess the outcome.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vaczy-noble-grey-rot-2024"}
Fruit condition connects the vineyard with sorting and processing. The distinction between grey and noble rot shows how much the nature of the damage matters. [@source.vaczy-noble-grey-rot-2024] Follow the affected organ, fruit condition and winery decision before naming a consequence for wine. An unusual flavour afterwards does not establish a field diagnosis on its own.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Protection starts with focused observation. Organise possible causes, test your explanation, choose an appropriate next step and check what changes.
:::
