White, rosé and red wine differ by more than colour. Their main route is determined by when juice and skins meet and separate again. That contact provides the foundation on which time, temperature, alcohol and movement further manage extraction.
:::

:::section{#drie-hoofdroutes depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-maceration source.awri-skin-contact"}
## When juice and skins remain together

For most white wine, grapes are pressed before the must ferments. Skins are therefore separated early, although a short period of skin contact before pressing may be used deliberately. Rosé begins with black grapes: limited contact releases colour and other skin components before the pink juice continues fermentation without skins.

For red wine, black skins remain present during alcoholic fermentation. Alcohol develops while juice and skins are together and helps make different components available than aqueous juice alone. After the intended contact period, free-run wine and [[concept.pressing|press wine]] are separated from the solid mass.

## Maceration and extraction

[[concept.maceration|Maceration]] describes contact between liquid and solid grape material. [[concept.extraction|Extraction]] is what passes across during that contact, including colour compounds, tannins and aromatic or flavour-active substances.

The terms are connected but not identical. Long contact does not guarantee a fixed amount of extraction, and not every substance moves at the same rate. Berry ripeness and damage, temperature, alcohol, time and movement change what becomes available from skins and seeds.
:::

:::section{#tijd-temperatuur-alcohol depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-fermentation-temperature source.awri-extended-maceration-2019"}
## Time, temperature and alcohol work together

Warmer liquid can accelerate extraction, while longer contact provides more opportunity for transfer and reaction. During red fermentation, the solvent also changes: alcohol rises as sugar falls. The same skins therefore do not sit in the same chemical environment each day.

More is not automatically better. A light red may be pressed early to retain supple fruit; a concentrated wine may sustain longer contact when grapes are ripe and sound. Extended contact after fermentation calls for continued tasting because texture may keep developing. [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019]
:::

:::section{#schillenkoek-beheren depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wre-cap-management"}
## Managing the floating cap

Carbon dioxide pushes skins upward during red fermentation to form a floating **cap**. Without renewed contact, the top can dry and extraction becomes uneven. The winemaker therefore brings liquid and skins together regularly.

During **pumping over**, or *remontage*, fermenting wine is drawn from the bottom of the tank and returned over the skins. During **punching down**, or *pigeage*, the cap is pushed into the liquid. Frequency and force are adjusted to fruit, vessel, temperature and desired structure.
:::

:::comparison{#vergelijking-routes depth="foundation"}
## The decisive point of separation

| Route | Skin contact | Usual pressing point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White | None or brief contact before fermentation | Before fermentation |
| Rosé | Brief contact with black skins | Before fermentation, after colour extraction |
| Red | Contact during alcoholic fermentation | After or near the end of skin fermentation |

The table gives the main model. Orange wine, direct pressing of black grapes and other deliberate variants show how winemakers can alter the same moment of separation.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
The timing and management of skin contact help explain colour, tannin structure, bitterness, aroma and mouthfeel. After pressing, however, young wine is not finished: acids, yeast sediment and microbiological stability create a new set of choices.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Wine colour is the visible result of a deeper process distinction: which grape parts remain together at which moment, and how is their contact managed?
:::
