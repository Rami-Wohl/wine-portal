A berry can become sweeter while its acids, skin and aromas develop differently. When is it ripe enough to harvest? Learn to combine measurements with tasting, recognise variation within a vineyard and weigh harvest timing against the intended fruit and the risk of waiting.
:::

:::objectives{#leerdoelen depth="foundation"}
- Explain why ripeness involves more than a sugar reading.
- Distinguish a single measurement from a representatively monitored trend.
- Support a harvest decision with the wine goal, fruit condition and uncertainty.
:::

:::section{#wat-verandert-in-de-bes depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-berry-ripening"}
## Ripening has several dimensions

A grape berry contains juice, skin and usually seeds. Around **véraison**, the onset of ripening, it softens and changes in colour and composition. Black grapes generally turn red to purple; white grapes need not do so. Sugars then increase, acid composition changes and aromas and compounds in skins and seeds develop further. These processes do not move in step. [@source.awri-berry-ripening]

**Ripeness** therefore describes several properties. Sugar and acidity help assess composition; tasting adds information about aroma, skin texture and seeds, for example. In black grapes, pigments and [[concept.tannin|tannins]] also matter. The reference on [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|berry development and ripeness]] explores these processes in more detail.
:::

:::figure{#rijpheid-in-beeld depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.berry-development-ripeness.ripeness-variables"}
**Seven windows onto the same fruit**

1. **Soluble solids:** a refractometer gives an indication of sugar concentration.
2. **Acid balance:** pH and titratable acidity reveal different aspects of the juice.
3. **Skin:** pigments, tannins and texture may matter to the wine goal.
4. **Seeds:** colour, texture and flavour provide additional observations.
5. **Aroma:** smelling and tasting help follow aromatic development.
6. **Water:** changes in berry water affect concentrations.
7. **Health and conditions:** damage, shrivelling and weather also count.

## What question does your measurement answer?

A refractometer measures how juice bends light. The usual **°Brix** reading estimates soluble solids, mainly sugars. It helps estimate alcohol potential, but is neither a complete sugar analysis nor a prediction of the finished wine. [@source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023]

**pH** describes acid activity in the juice; **titratable acidity** measures how much base is needed to reach a specified endpoint. They are not interchangeable. The reference on [[concept.acidity|acidity]] explains the distinction further. Taste the fruit too: a sugar reading does not tell you how the skin or aroma has developed. [@source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023] [@source.awri-berry-ripening]
:::

## From one berry to a useful trend

A **sample** is a selection intended to tell you something about a larger lot. Picking only the most attractive berries distorts that picture. Variation exists between vines, between bunches and within a bunch, including differences between shaded and exposed berries. Suitable sampling takes this into account. [@source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity; Berry sampling for maturity assessment]

Record origin and date, and compare successive samples collected and prepared in comparable ways. A change of method can create an apparent trend. Where zones differ clearly, monitoring them separately can preserve information lost in an overall average. A single reading then becomes part of a **time series**: several observations that help assess direction and variation. [@source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity] [@source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023]
:::

:::section{#oogsten-onder-onzekerheid depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-berry-ripening source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity"}
## What might waiting gain, and what might it risk?

Start with the intended fruit. Retaining acidity may carry considerable weight for a fresh wine; skin and tannin properties also matter for a red wine made with skin contact. Waiting can allow further development, but an increasing concentration may also result from water loss. “More sugar” therefore does not automatically mean that all other properties have ripened further. [@source.awri-berry-ripening]

Assess fruit health and the weather outlook too. Increasing rot risk can reduce the room to wait. [@source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity] Then ask the practical question: can this fruit be picked, transported and received in time? A harvest decision connects desired composition with what is feasible in this [[concept.vintage|growing season]]. It is a judgement under uncertainty, not a universal threshold.
:::

:::section{#probeer-het-zelf depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-berry-ripening source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity"}
## Sugar is rising: is the harvest decision settled?

In a hypothetical vineyard intended for a fresh white wine, the sugar reading is rising. Some berries are shrivelling and wet weather is forecast. Someone suggests waiting for an even higher reading. What information is missing?

Ask about **acid trends, aroma, health and a representative sample**. Investigate whether water loss partly explains the rise and whether the observation applies across the vineyard. [@source.awri-berry-ripening] [@source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity] Then weigh the possible benefit of waiting against losing desired fruit and the feasibility of picking. Without that information, this case cannot yield a fixed harvest day.
:::
