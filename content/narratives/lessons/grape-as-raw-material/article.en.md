A grape looks small and simple, yet it enters the winery as a complete package: juice, sugar, acids, skin, seeds and its own aromatic starting point. A winemaker can separate, combine and protect that material before fermentation; the possibilities begin with what the harvest already contains.
:::

:::section{#de-bes-als-pakket depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-wine-composition"}
## Juice, skins, seeds and stems

The pulp consists mainly of water, dissolved sugars and acids. During [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]], yeast converts much of that sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide. Sugar concentration therefore provides an important starting point for potential alcohol, while [[concept.acidity|acids]] help shape freshness and the chemical environment of juice and wine.

The skin encloses more than juicy flesh. In black grapes, it contains pigments, tannins and many compounds that can contribute to aroma. The seeds contain [[concept.tannin|tannins]] too. Stems, if harvested and retained, add another form of plant material. The winery decision to keep juice, skins, seeds and sometimes stems together—or to separate them—later determines which components have an opportunity to enter the wine. [@source.wset-wine-composition]

## Sugar, acidity and phenolic maturity

As grapes ripen, sugars increase, acid composition changes and skins, seeds and aromatic compounds continue to develop. Those changes run alongside one another, each at its own pace. A sugar reading alone therefore cannot describe the maturity of the whole grape. [@source.awri-berry-ripening]

Winemakers taste as well as measure. They may consider acidity, aroma, skin texture, and the flavour and colour of seeds. In black grapes, **phenolic maturity** is a collective term for the development of the colour- and tannin-bearing parts of the berry. The desired balance depends on the intended wine: grapes for a light, fresh style require a different harvest window from fruit intended for a concentrated red.
:::

:::section{#oogsten-is-kiezen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-berry-ripening"}
## How harvest timing shapes the raw material

More time on the vine may bring additional sugar and further aromatic and phenolic development, while acids and freshness continue to evolve as well. Earlier picking will often retain a firmer acid balance and lower potential alcohol. The winemaker looks for a moment when the relevant properties suit the intended style.

That moment occurs within a real growing season. Heat, rain and the course of ripening can produce different proportions in each [[concept.vintage|vintage]]. Bunches and sections of a vineyard do not always ripen at the same time either. Harvest may consequently be spread across several days or selective passes.
:::

## Why healthy grapes matter

Intact, healthy berries give the winery a controlled starting point. Damage, fungal growth or sour rot can expose juice to oxygen, microorganisms and unwanted transformations before the intended fermentation. Effects vary with the condition and proportion of affected fruit, so bunches are assessed during harvest and reception and sorted where necessary. [@source.awri-managing-botrytis-fruit-2023] [@source.penn-state-grape-sour-rot]

[[concept.botrytis|Botrytis]] shows why context matters. Under highly specific conditions, noble rot can create desirable raw material for sweet wine. Within a learning path about ordinary still dry wine, affected fruit primarily calls for recognition, selection and an adjusted winery plan.

## What the grape brings and the winery can shape

| The grape brings | The winery can then |
| --- | --- |
| Water, sugar and acids | Protect, press and ferment |
| Skins, seeds and sometimes stems | Separate components or deliberately maintain contact |
| Pigments, tannins and aromatic compounds | Manage extraction, temperature and time |
| A particular maturity and state of health | Sort and adapt the production route |

Winemaking is therefore not the correction of an empty base. It is the purposeful guidance of material that already contains structure, opportunities and constraints.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
The harvested grape helps explain why a wine starts with a particular acid and alcohol potential, tannin structure and aromatic direction. What you ultimately taste emerges from the complete route: grape variety, growing season, harvest timing and health meet fermentation, extraction, maturation and every choice in between.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Winemaking begins with raw material that already points in certain directions. A sound winery decision responds to the composition, maturity and health of the grapes that actually arrive.
:::
