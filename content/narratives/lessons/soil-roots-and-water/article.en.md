:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation"}
Water in a vineyard soil must travel a route before it helps the plant grow. It needs to remain where roots can reach it, be taken up and move through the vine to the leaves. Learn to distinguish storage, accessibility and plant response before judging water management.
:::

:::objectives{#leerdoelen depth="foundation"}
- Distinguish water in the soil from water the vine can use.
- Follow the water pathway from a soil pore through root and leaf to the air.
- Combine soil, weather and plant observations before choosing water supply or drainage measures.
:::

:::section{#voorraad-en-bereik depth="foundation" source_refs="source.nrcs-available-water-capacity source.cornell-grapevine-roots"}
## Where is the water, and can roots reach it?

Soil contains solid particles and **pores**, the spaces between them that can hold water and air. Large pores let water drain relatively easily; smaller ones retain it more strongly. Some of the remaining water is held so tightly that plants find it difficult to take up. High water storage and high water availability are therefore different properties. [@source.nrcs-available-water-capacity]

The rooted space matters too. Soil depth, structure and layers that restrict root growth determine the volume a vine can explore. Compaction can limit both pores and rooting. The name “clay” or “sand” therefore offers an initial clue, followed by investigation of the actual soil profile. [[concept.vineyard-soils|Vineyard soils]] explain these properties further. [@source.nrcs-available-water-capacity]

Within that profile, young, fine root regions carry out much of the uptake. Their location and activity determine where the vine makes contact with soil water. A deep woody root in an illustration, by itself, proves little about uptake on a particular day. [@source.cornell-grapevine-roots]
:::

:::figure{#waterroute-in-beeld depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.vine-water-relations.soil-plant-atmosphere-pathway"}
**Follow the water pathway**

1. **Supply:** rain and a drip line represent possible sources of water.
2. **Soil pores:** water enters the soil and is distributed, retained or drained.
3. **Fine roots:** take up water where they are in contact with an available supply.
4. **Xylem:** water-conducting tissue connects roots with shoots and leaves.
5. **Leaf:** water vapour leaves the plant; carbon dioxide can enter for photosynthesis.
6. **Atmosphere:** light, heat, humidity and wind influence evaporative demand.

The arrows simplify a branching, changing system. Scale and rooting depth are schematic; the drip line represents a possible supply, rather than advice for every vineyard.
:::

:::section{#van-wortel-naar-lucht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-roots source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020"}
## What happens when demand and supply diverge?

Evaporation from the leaf is called **transpiration**. It helps pull water upwards through the xylem. In the leaf, **stomata**, small pores, regulate exchange with the air. The [[concept.vine-as-living-system|vine]] thus connects soil and atmosphere. [@source.cornell-grapevine-roots]

When supply falls short of demand, the plant can close its stomata further. This limits water loss but also carbon dioxide entry for photosynthesis. Growth can consequently slow. The response depends on factors including the duration and severity of the deficit, plant material and environmental conditions. Severe, prolonged drought can damage leaves and water transport. [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020]
:::

:::section{#veel-water-weinig-lucht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-waterlogged-vineyards"}
## Why can roots be restricted in wet soil too?

Roots need oxygen for respiration. If the root zone stays saturated for a long time, water replaces soil air. The oxygen supply becomes restricted and root function can decline; prolonged saturation can kill roots. [@source.awri-waterlogged-vineyards]

A wet soil surface therefore tells only part of the story. You also need to know how long water persists, where it collects in the profile and how it drains. Rain, groundwater and a poorly permeable layer can combine to create a different situation from a single shower on freely draining soil. [@source.awri-waterlogged-vineyards]
:::

:::section{#eerst-onderzoeken depth="foundation"}
## Which observations belong together?

Investigate the water pathway in three steps:

1. **Soil and roots:** where is the moisture, how far do active roots extend, and are there compacted or persistently saturated layers?
2. **Environment and timing:** what were the rainfall, heat and humidity, and which growth stage has the vine reached? The [[concept.climate-weather-site-microclimate|environmental reference]] helps choose the appropriate scale.
3. **Plant:** how are shoot growth and leaf function progressing, and what do repeated observations or suitable water-status measurements show?

Together, these data help locate a possible restriction. A decision about irrigation or drainage then needs a specific goal, local possibilities and expert assessment. The [[concept.vine-water-relations|water relations reference]] develops those considerations further.
:::

:::section{#probeer-het-zelf depth="foundation"}
## What does moisture below a compacted layer tell you?

In a fictional vineyard, the topsoil looks dry. Below a compacted layer you still measure moisture; many fine roots are found above it. Is that deeper storage sufficient evidence that the vine has enough water?

A reasoned answer first asks **how many active roots actually reach the moisture**, how water moves through that layer and how the plant responds. Deep storage may increase the reserve, but its usefulness depends on the connection with roots. The observation prompts investigation of that connection; the appropriate intervention follows assessment of the whole profile and plant condition.
:::

Water availability can influence growth, berry development and ripening. The outcome depends on factors including the timing and severity of a restriction. The soil name thus takes its place in a chain of processes, rather than serving directly as a flavour description.

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Follow water through the whole system: storage in the soil, access for active roots and the plant's response to current demand. That connection guides water management.
:::
