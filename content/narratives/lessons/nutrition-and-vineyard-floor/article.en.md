A vine shares its root environment with other plants and soil organisms. Nutrients must become available there and be taken up before they support growth. Learn to distinguish soil reserves, plant uptake and a laboratory result, and consider vegetation through its function in the vineyard.
:::

- Interpret a tissue measurement alongside growth stage, sampling and plant growth.
- Weigh soil protection against competition for water and nutrients.

:::section{#van-voorraad-naar-opname depth="foundation" source_refs="source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020 source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024"}
## How do nutrients reach the plant?

**Nutrients** are elements the plant uses, such as nitrogen for building proteins. In the soil, they may be dissolved in water, bound to particles or incorporated into plant residues and organisms. Soil organisms transform residues and can release elements or hold them temporarily. The nutrient supply therefore moves between different forms. [@source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020]

The [[concept.vine-as-living-system|vine]] takes up suitable dissolved forms through its roots. Conditions in the root zone influence this process: moisture, oxygen and soil chemistry are among the factors that determine the plant’s opportunities. Soil and plant tests therefore answer different questions. The [[concept.vine-nutrition-soil-management-vineyard-floor|nutrition reference]] explores availability and uptake further. [@source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020] [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024]
:::

:::figure{#voeding-in-beeld depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.vine-nutrition-soil-management-vineyard-floor.root-zone-nutrient-cycle"}
**Follow nutrients through an open cycle**

1. **Reserves:** soil particles, organic matter and residues contain elements.
2. **Transformation:** chemical and biological processes can release or retain elements.
3. **Solution and binding:** a changing proportion is dissolved in soil water; another remains bound.
4. **Uptake:** fine roots take up suitable forms. Fungal threads can act as partners.
5. **Allocation:** the plant distributes absorbed elements among organs and storage.
6. **Return and removal:** residues can return; harvest and losses remove elements.

Dots, arrows and enlarged soil organisms are schematic. The fungal threads show one possible partnership; they do not guarantee uptake. The illustration shows a process, not a direct route from rock to wine flavour.
:::

:::section{#wat-meet-je depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024"}
## What does the number on a tissue report mean?

A laboratory can analyse a leaf blade or its stalk, the petiole. The report usually gives a **concentration**: the amount of an element per amount of sampled tissue. This differs from the total amount the whole vine has absorbed. Rapid growth can distribute an element through more tissue mass, lowering its concentration. [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024]

A useful comparison therefore requires the same tissue type and a comparable developmental stage, alongside information about growth and management. Keep areas with unusual and normal growth separate during a targeted investigation. Combine analysis with observations of the plant, [[concept.vineyard-soils|soil]] and [[concept.vine-water-relations|water relations]]. Leaf colour alone does not establish a nutritional diagnosis either. [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024]
:::

:::section{#vloer-met-meerdere-doelen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.abad-cover-crops-viticulture-soil-2021 source.abad-cover-crops-agronomic-performance-2021"}
## Why grow plants between the rows?

The **vineyard floor** is the managed soil space between and beneath vines. Vegetation can protect soil against erosion and support its structure and water infiltration. Infiltration means water entering the soil. This lets you assess vegetation through what it does, alongside its appearance. [@source.abad-cover-crops-viticulture-soil-2021]

Living vegetation also uses water and nutrients. Competition can help restrain vigorous vines, but may become an unwanted burden where water reserves are limited. Species, season, location beneath or between rows, and management all change the outcome. A temporary cover and permanently growing vegetation therefore raise different questions. [@source.abad-cover-crops-agronomic-performance-2021]
:::

:::section{#kiezen-en-hermeten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024 source.abad-cover-crops-agronomic-performance-2021"}
## How can you make the goal testable?

Start with a concrete question: do you want to limit erosion, restrain excessive growth or investigate a suspected uptake problem? Then record what you will observe before and after the change. A nutrition question needs growth observations and comparable samples; floor management also calls for observations of cover, water availability and vine response. [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024] [@source.abad-cover-crops-agronomic-performance-2021]

This separates three steps: **what was measured, which process might explain it and what change do you expect?** Appropriate implementation follows from that connection and assessment of local conditions.
:::

:::section{#probeer-het-zelf depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024"}
## Lower tissue nitrogen: has the vine absorbed less?

In a hypothetical block, shoots are growing faster this year. A tissue report shows a lower nitrogen concentration than last year. Does that prove the plant has taken up less nitrogen?

A reasoned answer is **no: concentration differs from total amount**. Faster growth can distribute the amount through more mass. Also check whether the same organ was sampled at the same stage and how the vines continue to develop. [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024] The report needs interpretation; on its own, it is not an instruction to add fertiliser.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020"}
Nutrition and floor management can influence growth and grape composition through available water and nutrients. [@source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020] Follow that plant response before naming a consequence for wine, and include harvest and processing. A soil organism, a green alley or a single analytical result does not yet explain the finished wine’s flavour.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Nutrient management connects soil, uptake and plant growth. Read measurements in that context, and assess vegetation through its purpose and its use of the same space, water and nutrients.
:::
