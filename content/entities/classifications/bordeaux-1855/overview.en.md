:::summary{#wat-is-1855 depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
The **1855 classification** is a historical ranking of a select group of [[concept.cru|growths]], or wine estates, from [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. It recorded an existing commercial hierarchy in 1855 and subsequently became one of the region's best-known points of reference. It is neither an appellation — a legally protected designation of origin — nor a ranking of every Bordeaux wine. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]
:::

:::section{#oorsprong depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
## History — a trade list made for Paris

For the Paris Universal Exhibition, Napoleon III requested a presentation of the Gironde's wines. The Bordeaux Chamber of Commerce entrusted the ranking to the local association of wine brokers. Their list was published on 18 April 1855. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]

The result was not a modern blind tasting. The classification recorded which estates the contemporary trade regarded as its leading red and sweet growths, and the order in which they stood.
:::

:::detail{#handelsrangorde parent="oorsprong" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
### Price as a trace of reputation

The [[concept.courtier|courtiers]] — wine brokers with close knowledge of the market and its estates — did not begin with a blank sheet. Rankings already circulated before 1855, and leading estates fell into recognisable classes according to the prices they achieved and the reputation they had sustained. The Paris commission turned that fluid commercial knowledge into an official snapshot. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]

Price served as evidence of the standing a growth had accumulated over several years. That is not proof that every bottle from a higher class tastes better than every bottle from a lower one.
:::

:::detail{#een-lijst-die-bevroor parent="oorsprong" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
### Two official changes

The list gained its extraordinary historical weight precisely because it was scarcely revised. Only two official changes have been made since April 1855: Château Cantemerle was added to the fifth class (Cinquième Cru) on 16 September 1855, and [[producer.chateau-mouton-rothschild|Château Mouton Rothschild]] was promoted from the second to the first class in June 1973. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]

Estates, ownership, performance and market prices continued to change in the meantime. The fixed list and the living wine market therefore run alongside one another, not always in the same order.
:::

:::section{#opbouw depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
## Structure — separate ladders for red and sweet wine

The classification consists of two separate rankings. The **61 red-wine growths** are divided into five classes: Premier, Deuxième, Troisième, Quatrième and Cinquième Cru. The **27 sweet-wine growths** occupy three levels: one Premier Cru Supérieur, eleven Premiers Crus and fifteen Deuxièmes Crus. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]

The French terms literally mean first, second, third, fourth and fifth growth. They state a position within this historical list; they do not turn a wine into a different appellation.
:::

:::detail{#aantallen-en-gebieden parent="opbouw" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
### A narrow slice of greater Bordeaux

The red list lies almost entirely in the [[region.medoc|Médoc]]: [[appellation.saint-estephe|Saint-Estèphe]], [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]], [[appellation.saint-julien|Saint-Julien]], [[appellation.margaux|Margaux]] and [[appellation.haut-medoc|Haut-Médoc]]. The sweet-wine list belongs to [[appellation.sauternes|Sauternes]] and [[appellation.barsac|Barsac]]. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]

Large parts of Bordeaux therefore sit outside 1855. Absence from the list says nothing by itself about an estate's legal origin or quality; other areas use other classifications, or no comparable system at all.
:::

:::detail{#haut-brion-als-uitzondering parent="opbouw" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
### Haut-Brion beyond the Médoc

The red growths include one famous geographical exception: Château Haut-Brion, then treated as part of [[appellation.graves|Graves]] and now situated within [[appellation.pessac-leognan|Pessac-Léognan]]. It was included because its commercial standing was comparable to that of the highest-valued Médoc growths. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]

That single château does not extend the list into a classification of all Graves. Instead, it highlights just how historical and selective its boundaries are.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-betekenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
## Label and meaning — what the rank really tells you

“Grand Cru Classé en 1855” tells you that the [[concept.chateau|château]], here meaning the wine estate, belongs to this historical group. A term such as Premier or Cinquième Cru then states its class. That is valuable context: it immediately connects the estate to a particular heritage and reputation.

Keep reading, however. The appellation tells you where the wine comes from, the producer whose decisions shaped it, and the [[concept.vintage|vintage]] in which its grapes grew. The classification replaces none of that information.
:::

:::detail{#rang-en-werkelijkheid parent="etiket-en-betekenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
### Rank and present reality

The official rank is nearly fixed, but the reality in the bottle is not. Producers invest, change ownership or style, and encounter stronger and weaker vintages; prices can also move above or below the historical order. Even the body representing the classified growths describes this continuing reassessment by the market. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]

Use 1855 as a historical and institutional compass, not as the automatic result of a present-day tasting.
:::

:::detail{#classificatie-is-geen-appellation parent="etiket-en-betekenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
### Two systems, two questions

An appellation primarily answers a question of origin: from which delimited area does the wine come, and which production rules must it meet? The 1855 classification answers a different question: which selected estates appeared on the historical list, and which rank did they receive?

Classified and unclassified producers can therefore share the same appellation. The appellation is not a lower rung of 1855, and 1855 is not a higher appellation.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Read Bordeaux 1855 as an almost frozen map of historical commercial reputation: influential and informative, but narrower than Bordeaux and never the whole story of a bottle.
:::

:::caveat{#geen-smaakgarantie depth="advanced" variant="simplification" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
A higher class is not a timeless guarantee of taste. Rank, appellation, producer, wine, vintage and bottle development each supply different information; only together do they form a useful picture.
:::
