Francs Côtes de Bordeaux is the smallest geographical centre in the Côtes family: three communes on high, rolling ground at the eastern edge of the Libournais. Merlot-led red sits beside a rarer white repertoire ranging from dry to liquoreux.

That combination makes Francs more than a small neighbour of Saint-Émilion. A cooler eastern climate, clay-limestone slopes and the possibility of sweet white give the appellation its own teachable profile.

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Location and boundaries — three communes on eastern heights

The legal zone covers Francs, Saint-Cibard and Tayac, east of Castillon and close to the Dordogne departmental border. It belongs to [[region.libournais|the Libournais]] and lies far inland from the estuary.

Francs is a geographical denomination within [[appellation.cotes-de-bordeaux|Côtes de Bordeaux]]. The name protects red, dry white and sweet white from officially delimited parcels.
:::

:::detail{#klein-is-niet-eenvoudig parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Small is not simple

Three communes make the map manageable, but not the vineyard. Valleys, slopes and plateaus alter drainage, frost exposure and sunlight. Small scale makes parcel context more important, not redundant.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## History — an old wine district with a young name

Viticulture in Bordeaux's eastern hills developed around villages and trading routes towards the Dordogne and Libourne. The modern origin was recognised in 1967 as Bordeaux-Côtes de Francs and later amended.

Since 2009 Francs has sat as a geographical denomination under Côtes de Bordeaux. The shared name brought market visibility while red, white and the three-commune core retained their local identity. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-francs-cotes-de-bordeaux"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — clay, limestone and an eastern rhythm

Clay-limestone dominates many slopes, with marl, sand, gravel and local iron-rich deposits in the mosaic. Clay can retain water; limestone-rich and higher positions can affect drainage and rooting depth.

Atlantic influence reaches Francs, but its eastern position makes continental variation more apparent. Spring frost, summer drought and the course of autumn can consequently differ sharply between years.
:::

:::detail{#blauwe-klei-zonder-magie parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.civb-francs-cotes-de-bordeaux source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Blue clay without magic

Local communication identifies blue clay as a distinctive element. Colour or geological terminology cannot explain wine by itself. Meaning runs through structure, water, roots and parcel management; flavour remains the outcome of more factors.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Grape varieties — red and white in one small origin

For red, [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] are principal varieties. Merlot often supplies the core; Cabernet Franc is valuable for fragrance and freshness in an eastern climate.

White chiefly uses [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], Sauvignon Gris, [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and Muscadelle. The same varieties may be made dry or harvested riper and more concentrated for liquoreux.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — three routes from the same hills

Red follows skin fermentation, malolactic conversion, blending and maturation. Dry white is pressed and made for freshness and texture. For liquoreux, overripe grapes concentrated through noble rot and/or drying on the vine must be hand-picked in successive passes.

That last point belongs at foundation level: it explains why sweet Francs is not a simply sweetened dry wine but a labour-intensive, weather-dependent style.
:::

:::detail{#edelrot-en-passerillage parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Noble rot and passerillage

Noble rot can remove water from berries and concentrate aroma and sugar; *passerillage* is drying on the vine without noble rot necessarily dominating. Selective picking prevents sound, concentrated and compromised grapes from being brought in indiscriminately.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Appellation rules — red, dry white and liquoreux

Francs protects three main forms: still red, dry white and sweet white designated liquoreux. The specification sets separate ripeness, yield, sugar, harvest and maturation conditions.

The colours share origin but not exactly the same production rules or release dates. The label must therefore be read together with colour and sweetness.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Current thresholds

Minimum planting density is 4,500 vines per hectare. Base yield is 52 hectolitres per hectare for red, 62 for dry white and 37 for liquoreux. Liquoreux requires at least 238 grams of sugar per litre of must, no enrichment and maturation until at least 15 March after harvest. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-francs-cotes-de-bordeaux"}
## Classification and producers — small without an official hierarchy

Francs has no official château classification. Limited scale makes individual estates visible, but fame, bottle weight or proximity to Saint-Émilion supplies no legal rank.

Compare producers by colour, cuvée, parcel and method. For white, dry or liquoreux is especially important; one producer cannot stand as a benchmark for all three styles.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-francs-cotes-de-bordeaux"}
## Wine style and bottle development — three voices from a small appellation

Red can combine cherry, plum, blackberry and spice with rounded to firm tannin. Dry white can pair citrus, flowers and white fruit with tension and texture. Liquoreux may show honey, candied citrus and ripe stone fruit supported by sufficient acidity.

Serve red around 16–18 °C, dry white cooler and sweet white around 8–10 °C. Ageing capacity varies strongly by cuvée; sugar alone does not make a sweet wine balanced or long-lived.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — colour and sweetness belong with the name

Read “Francs Côtes de Bordeaux” as the complete origin. Then determine whether the bottle is red, dry white or liquoreux. Older labels may say Bordeaux-Côtes de Francs.

Producer, vintage and bottler remain essential. *Côtes* places Francs in the shared family, not on an informal quality rung above or below Castillon.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Contemporary developments — scale as opportunity and vulnerability

A small appellation can explain parcels and makers personally but has less volume and visibility. Red, dry white and liquoreux provide distinction if the story is not reduced to “small Saint-Émilion”.

Climate change can aid ripening while increasing frost, drought and unstable autumn weather. Sweet white in particular remains dependent on suitable conditions and selective labour.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Francs Côtes de Bordeaux is a small eastern appellation with an unusually broad range: red, dry white and liquoreux. Clay-limestone, vintage and a deliberately chosen harvest and cellar route matter more than its size suggests.
:::
