:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-overview source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
Barsac shares with [[appellation.sauternes|Sauternes]] the world of golden white wines concentrated by [[concept.botrytis|noble rot]], yet it has a place and name of its own. The appellation lies entirely within the commune of Barsac, north of the Ciron and close to the Garonne. Wine from its delimited parcels may be sold as Barsac or—under the overlapping rules—as Sauternes. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

Beneath that dual identity is a distinctive landscape: an almost level limestone plateau with a thin reddish topsoil, alongside warmer gravel deposits towards the river. Sémillon usually leads the wine, selective hand-picking yields little concentrated juice, and fresh acidity must lift the sweetness. Barsac is not inherently a lighter copy, but its own perspective on the same great sweet-wine tradition.

## Location and boundaries — one commune, two possible names

Barsac lies on the left bank of the Garonne in [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. The Ciron separates it to the southeast from the other communes of Sauternes; the Saint-Cricq stream marks the transition towards [[appellation.graves|Graves]] in the northwest. Unlike Sauternes, whose geographical area covers five communes, Barsac's geographical area belongs to a single commune. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

That municipal simplicity does not mean every piece of land may produce the wine. The production parcels are officially delimited. Commune, appellation area and authorised vineyard are three different scales; a future Atlas view must show them as such.
:::

:::detail{#twee-namen-een-wijngaard parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Barsac or Sauternes is a choice within overlapping rights

Barsac is both an AOC in its own right and one of the five communes in the geographical area of Sauternes. Local growers retained the right in 1936 to use their commune's name while their wine could also qualify as Sauternes; the official history records that both usages predate the decree. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

The name on the label therefore does not change the origin. It may say something about how a producer presents its identity, range and market position. It is not an automatic quality rank, nor proof that two cuvées bearing different names contain the same wine.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — wine, stone and barrels along the Garonne

Viticulture in the Sauternais has been documented since the Middle Ages. Barsac also had a practical route to the wider world: its Garonne port carried goods and wine barrels by *gabarre* towards Bordeaux. By the thirteenth century it held the title of royal prévôté, an administrative and commercial role older than the modern appellation. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

From the late sixteenth, and especially the seventeenth century, trade with Dutch buyers helped steer production towards sweet white wine. Local growers simultaneously developed their choice of varieties, late harvesting and successive selections. Trade created demand; the vineyard and its skills determined what could be made to meet it.
:::

:::detail{#haven-handel-en-zoete-wijn parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
### A costly harvest method needed an accessible market

Botrytised grapes yield little juice, and repeated picking rounds demand much labour. Proximity to the Garonne and the Bordeaux trade made it possible to sell such costly wines beyond the region. During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, individual crus consequently gained increasingly distinct reputations.

Local building stone was exported too. Historical quarrying of the durable *pierre de Barsac* left small plots and stone walls in the landscape. The history of trade survives not only in documents and bottles, but in the port, buildings and pattern of the land.
:::

:::detail{#van-prevote-naar-aoc parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Old independence acquired two modern forms

In 1855, brokers included ten Barsac crus in the classification of sweet wines: two premiers crus and eight deuxièmes crus. This ranked estates according to their historical commercial reputation; it did not yet protect the commune name as a production rule. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]

The Barsac AOC was recognised on 11 September 1936. It tied the name to delimited land and practices such as overripe, botrytised fruit and manual *tries successives*. Classification ranks named crus; the appellation protects the origin and production method of every wine bearing its name.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026 source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — a low plateau over limestone

Barsac lies north of the Ciron and is notably level. The plateau reaches roughly twenty metres in elevation and slopes towards the Garonne to about five metres beside younger alluvial land. In so-called Haut-Barsac, only some forty to sixty centimetres of sandy, reddish topsoil covers heavily fissured limestone. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

Towards the east, warmer, freely draining gravel deposits overlie the same broader limestone base. Barsac therefore contains at least two clear soil environments. “Limestone-rich” is an important starting point, not a description of every parcel.
:::

:::detail{#kalkplateau-en-grind parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Soil differences act through water, roots and growth

On the plateau, roots can follow fissures in the limestone to reach deeper water. The thin topsoil and limestone subsoil provide a different water reserve from the gravel zone, where surplus water drains faster and the surface warms more readily. This affects vigour, ripening and the condition in which grapes enter a damp autumn.

It is tempting to turn this into a fixed flavour distinction, but producer, variety, picking date, botrytis and cellar choices all still matter. Soil shapes conditions; it does not dictate a tasting note.
:::

:::detail{#water-mist-en-muren parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Mist arises from a larger water system

The cool Ciron meets the warmer Garonne near Barsac. Condensation can create morning mist, followed by sunny, ventilated afternoons that dry the bunches again. This alternation favours noble rot but does not guarantee it: persistently wet weather can bring grey rot, while a dry autumn can delay concentration.

Stone walls around small parcels are not merely decorative [[concept.terroir|terroir symbols]]. The specification connects them with historical limestone quarrying. Their exact microclimatic effect must be studied site by site rather than invented from their presence.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
## Grape varieties — Sémillon forms the core

[[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] usually forms the basis of Barsac. It can bring breadth and texture and is particularly well suited here to concentration by noble rot. [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]] can add freshness and citrus tension; [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]] can contribute a more floral accent.

There is no legally fixed recipe for the proportions. Parcel, ripeness, botrytis and producer determine which lots come together. A successful [[concept.assemblage|blend]] uses the varieties to balance sweetness, aroma, acidity and texture.
:::

:::detail{#vier-rassen-geen-vast-recept parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Permitted does not mean dominant

The current rules name four varieties: sémillon, sauvignon blanc, [[grape.sauvignon-gris|sauvignon gris]] and muscadelle. Sauvignon gris is a separate permitted variety. The text prescribes no fixed percentages in the bottle. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

Varieties and parcels may reach ideal botrytis at different times. The eventual blend therefore begins with separate picking and vinification: every lot preserves information about time, place and selection.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — pick when the berry, not the parcel, is ready

Barsac must be made from overripe grapes affected by noble rot, hand-picked in successive selective passes. Sound, ideally shrivelled and unusable berries can sit beside one another in a single bunch. Pickers take only what is suitable at that moment and return later. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

These *tries successives* make the harvest slow, costly and uncertain. Shrivelled berries provide little juice; rain or drought between passes can alter the result. Low volume is not a marketing device but a direct consequence of concentration and selection.
:::

:::detail{#tries-als-lezen-van-de-wijngaard parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Botrytis demands discrimination, not simply patience

Noble rot makes the skin porous, allowing water to evaporate and concentrating sugar, acidity and aroma. With too much moisture, the same fungus can become destructive. The grower is therefore not waiting for maximum infection, but seeking healthy concentration before the berry is lost.

The highly sugared must ferments slowly and may stop while sugar remains. Acidity, alcohol, botrytis aromas and texture must prevent the wine from becoming heavy. The cellar preserves what the harvest selected; it cannot turn poor fruit back into noble rot.
:::

:::detail{#kelderkeuzes parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Barrel is customary, not a uniform prescription

Fermentation and [[concept.elevage|maturation]] may take place partly in oak. Producers differ in press fractions, barrel selection, proportion of new wood, duration and final blend. The official specification describes barrel fermentation as common, not as an exclusive château recipe.

A fermentation stopping because of high sugar and rising alcohol belongs to the historical explanation, but modern stability requires temperature control, analysis and careful bottling. “Naturally sweet” means residual grape sugar, not the absence of cellar technique.
:::

## Appellation rules — the same sweet core, a protected name of its own

Barsac is reserved for still white wine with residual sugar from officially delimited parcels. Noble rot, over-ripeness and manual successive tries form part of its legal core. The appellation thus protects both place and an exceptional harvest method. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

Its production rules are substantively the same as those of Sauternes. According to the official text, the distinction rests chiefly on Barsac's soils and landscape, together with its historical right to the commune name. That makes Barsac independent without inventing an artificially different cellar recipe.
:::

:::detail{#dezelfde-kernregels-andere-naam parent="appellationregels" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026 source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
### A choice of name does not change the minimum standard

Whether an eligible wine is declared as Barsac or Sauternes, the core conditions for grapes, harvest and wine remain the same. A producer may choose one name for identity or range, but “Barsac” is legally neither a lower nor a higher tier of Sauternes.

The rules also specifically prohibit anti-botrytis treatment and total chemical weed control. That does not automatically make the wine organic; it limits particular practices within a broader approach to vineyard management.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### The figures behind the category

The 2026 specification requires at least 6,500 vines per hectare, a minimum must sugar level of 221 grams per litre, a base yield of 25 hectolitres per hectare and at least 45 grams of fermentable sugar per litre in the finished wine. Minimum maturation runs until 15 March following the harvest. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

These are legal thresholds, not an ideal tasting note. Perceived sweetness also depends on acidity, alcohol, texture and serving temperature; producers may exceed the minima considerably.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026 source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
## Classification and producers — ten crus in the 1855 list

Barsac supplied ten of the 27 classified sweet-wine crus of 1855: two premiers crus and eight deuxièmes crus. [[producer.chateau-climens|Château Climens]] and [[producer.chateau-coutet|Château Coutet]] are the two premiers. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

That strong presence explains Barsac's historical visibility, but 1855 is not an annual reassessment. The system ranks named estates, not every parcel, every [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]] or every producer in the appellation.
:::

:::detail{#twee-premiers-acht-deuxiemes parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### The complete Barsac group

- **Premiers crus:** Château Climens and Château Coutet.
- **Deuxièmes crus:** [[producer.chateau-doisy-daene|Château Doisy Daëne]], [[producer.chateau-doisy-dubroca|Château Doisy-Dubroca]], [[producer.chateau-doisy-vedrines|Château Doisy-Védrines]], [[producer.chateau-broustet|Château Broustet]], [[producer.chateau-nairac|Château Nairac]], [[producer.chateau-caillou|Château Caillou]], [[producer.chateau-de-myrat|Château de Myrat]] and [[producer.chateau-suau-barsac|Château Suau]].

The list is a navigation map for producers and history, not buying advice. The three Doisy names are separate estates, not three spellings of one château.
:::

:::detail{#rang-en-huidige-wijn parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
### Historical rank and present performance remain different questions

Ownership, vineyard area, style and reputation have changed since 1855. Unclassified producers sit outside the list without automatically making lesser wine; an old rank likewise offers no protection against a weak bottle or poor storage.

Read classification together with exact wine name, producer, [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and condition. A château may also make dry wine or other cuvées to which the Barsac classification cannot simply be attached.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-overview source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
## Wine style and bottle development — sweet, lively and not uniform

Young Barsac may be golden and smell of citrus, apricot, peach, flowers, honey and candied peel. The wine is sweet and often full in texture, while fresh acidity and sometimes a fine bitterness provide direction. Botrytis can add saffron-like, spicy or waxy nuances. [@source.inao-barsac-overview]

Barsac is often described as livelier or finer than Sauternes. That can be a useful point of comparison, but not an appellation guarantee. Plateau and gravel zone, varietal proportions, selection, vintage and producer create considerable range.
:::

:::detail{#barsac-is-geen-smaakgarantie parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026 source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022"}
### “Limestone freshness” does not explain everything

The official specification associates Barsac with fruit, liveliness and a texture sometimes described as mineral. This is a collective style description, not proof that limestone arrives as a literal aroma in the glass. Soil acts through water, roots, temperature and growth.

Taste for proportion instead: how do acidity, sugar, alcohol, texture and aromatic length support one another? A powerful Barsac can be convincing, and a subtle bottle need not be light.
:::

:::detail{#tijd-in-de-fles parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Gold can turn amber, fresh fruit can deepen

With age, citrus and stone fruit may shift towards marmalade, dried fruit, honey, nuts, wax and spice. Strong bottles can develop for decades, but concentration, acidity, closure and storage determine the actual course of their lives.

Half bottles generally mature faster than larger formats. Age alone is no guarantee of quality: heat, light, seepage or a weak cork can tire even a celebrated cru.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — Barsac on the front, sometimes Sauternes

Start with the exact appellation name, then producer, cuvée, [[concept.vintage|vintage]], bottle size and bottler. If the label says Barsac, it is sweet white wine from the commune made under the protected rules. If a wine from a Barsac estate says Sauternes, that may be legally correct too. [@source.inao-barsac-specification-2026]

A classification term must belong to the exact château. Gold decoration, “[[concept.grand-vin|grand vin]]” or a picture of a château proves no rank. With older bottles, provenance and condition matter greatly.
:::

:::detail{#barsac-of-sauternes parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Compare both appellation and cuvée first

The choice between Barsac and Sauternes can differ by producer and wine. Do not assume that two labels bearing the same château name are identical, or that one name is systematically sweeter, dearer or better. Check the precise cuvée and vintage.

Dry white wine from a Barsac estate falls outside the sweet Barsac rules and carries another permitted origin. Estate name and appellation remain separate layers of information.
:::

:::detail{#serveren-en-bewaren parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-barsac-overview"}
### Serve cool, with enough room for aroma

Around 10–12°C is a useful starting point: cool enough for tension, not so cold that aroma disappears. A small white-wine glass offers more space than a liqueur glass. Blue cheese, salty or spicy dishes and fruit desserts that are not excessively sweet can work, but pairings are not laws.

An opened young bottle can often remain engaging for several days when refrigerated; acidity and sugar help, but oxidation does not stop. For long ageing, stable coolness, darkness and a reliable closure matter more than the prestige of the label.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
## Contemporary developments — an old name, a newly risky autumn every year

Barsac remains dependent on ripe, healthy grapes, favourable botrytis and enough pickers for repeated passes. Heat may accelerate ripening, but can also lower acidity or cause drought stress before noble rot arrives; sustained rain may instead encourage grey rot.

At the same time, sweet wine is seeking its place at the table anew. Producers work with smaller formats, new serving contexts, stricter selection and sometimes dry companion wines. The challenge is to remain relevant without hollowing out the promise of botrytis and origin.
:::

:::detail{#klimaat-arbeid-en-keuze parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-barsac-specification-2026"}
### Climate adaptation is also a question of labour and yield

Canopy management, soil care, water availability, picking date and selection strictness interact. An extra trie may preserve quality but costs labour; waiting may increase concentration or lose the fruit. Vines on limestone plateau and gravel do not respond identically in dry years.

Choosing Barsac or Sauternes for the label may carry commercial meaning, but it does not change the physical risks of harvest. Current figures for area, sales and naming choices therefore belong in dated analysis, not as timeless facts on this overview page.
:::
