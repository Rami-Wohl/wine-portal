:::summary{#pomerol-in-het-kort depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.civb-pomerol-overview"}
**Pomerol** is a small appellation devoted solely to red wine, immediately north-east of Libourne on the Right Bank of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. The landscape is almost understated: low vines, scattered farm buildings and cellars, a church tower and few of the theatrical châteaux that shape the image of the [[region.medoc|Médoc]]. Yet this quiet plateau produces some of the world’s most celebrated and expensive wines.

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]] takes the leading role. In soils where clay, gravel and sand change proportion over short distances, it can combine generous fruit and a velvety texture with freshness and firm structure. [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] is often its most important partner. There is no single Pomerol recipe, however: parcel, year, harvest timing, selection, extraction and maturation all shift the result. Pomerol also has no official classification of its châteaux. Names such as [[producer.petrus|Petrus]], [[producer.le-pin|Le Pin]], [[producer.vieux-chateau-certan|Vieux Château Certan]] and [[producer.chateau-lafleur|Château Lafleur]] command enormous reputations, but no statutory cru rank. That distinction between appellation, reputation and market price is essential to understanding Pomerol. [@source.inao-pomerol-overview] [@source.civb-pomerol-overview]
:::

## Location and boundaries — a small plateau beside Libourne

Pomerol lies in the [[region.libournais|Libournais]], on the Right Bank of the Dordogne. Libourne is its urban neighbour to the south and west; [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]] lies to the east and [[appellation.lalande-de-pomerol|Lalande-de-Pomerol]] begins to the north, beyond the Barbanne. The name identifies both a commune and a protected wine origin, but their outlines are not exactly the same.

The PDO is small and entirely focused on still red wine. Its core lies in the commune of Pomerol and a historically defined part of Libourne. Only parcels within the official delimitation may supply grapes for Pomerol; a postal address, château gate or view across the plateau proves nothing by itself. [@source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021]
:::

:::detail{#pomerol-libourne-en-lalande parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021"}
### Pomerol, Libourne and Lalande are not synonyms

The town of Libourne is fundamental to Pomerol’s trade and history, but not all of Libourne belongs to the production area. Conversely, the legal story does not end neatly at every municipal sign: historic judgements and the current specification recognise precisely named sections and parcels.

Lalande-de-Pomerol is an appellation in its own right, not a northern district, second tier or automatically lighter version of Pomerol. They share a climate, grape varieties and a common boundary, but their names protect different origins. On the label, the hyphens carry meaning rather than mere typography.
:::

:::detail{#grens-is-geen-stijl parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021 source.legifrance-pomerol-order-2021"}
### The legal boundary is more precise than the landscape appears

Alongside the core in Pomerol and Libourne, the specification approved in 2021 describes particular parcels and separate zones where grapes may be harvested or cellar work may take place. Such exceptions are legal and historical; they do not suggest that soil suddenly changes character at a road. [@source.legifrance-pomerol-order-2021]

This page therefore draws no boundary of its own. A dependable parcel view must eventually come from official geometry and a controlled Atlas layer. Landscape helps us picture the place; only the current specification determines the right to use its name.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.pomerol-syndicate-history"}
## History — old viticulture, young global fame

Pomerol’s winegrowing past reaches back for centuries, but its present lustre is surprisingly modern. From the twelfth century, the Hospitallers of Saint John of Jerusalem became important here. Their hospice and land stood on a route used by pilgrims travelling to Santiago de Compostela. War and depopulation interrupted the landscape; vineyards were re-established in later centuries and wine gradually became the dominant activity. [@source.pomerol-syndicate-history]

In the nineteenth century, Libourne’s merchants and the railway to Paris opened new markets. [[concept.phylloxera|Phylloxera]] then destroyed the vineyard, which had to be rebuilt on grafted vines. Pomerol was recognised as an AOC on 8 December 1936. Its truly international cult status gathered pace chiefly after the Second World War. [@source.inao-pomerol-overview]
:::

:::detail{#hospitaliers-handel-en-spoor parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.pomerol-syndicate-history"}
### From pilgrimage route to trading network

The Hospitallers do not magically explain the modern wine, but they connect agriculture, movement and hospitality with this place at an early date. Much later, Pomerol’s position beside Libourne made another network decisive. Merchants could send barrels to northern France, Belgium and beyond; the railway connection with Paris accelerated that movement.

From the late nineteenth century, and again between the world wars and after 1945, families from the Corrèze and Belgium, among other places, settled in the Libournais. Some combined estate ownership with wine trading. Pomerol’s reputation therefore grew not only from soil and cellar, but also from migration, credit, distribution and persistent family enterprise. [@source.pomerol-syndicate-history]
:::

:::detail{#van-laatkomer-tot-wereldnaam parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.civb-pomerol-overview"}
### Prestige without a nineteenth-century ranking

When the [[region.medoc|Médoc]] received its famous commercial classification in 1855, Pomerol did not yet hold the same international market position. This helps explain why its present hierarchy does not descend from that document. During the second half of the twentieth century, a handful of small estates attracted mounting attention; scarce production and global demand amplified prices.

That ascent should not be retold as an ancient inevitability. Pomerol has a long winegrowing history, but the worldwide status of its most expensive names is younger. This combination—old landscape, late breakthrough—distinguishes it from the monumental self-presentation of many Left Bank crus.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021 source.pomerol-syndicate-terroir"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — subtle slopes with serious consequences

The Pomerol plateau descends in terraces towards the valley of the Isle. Its changes in elevation are small enough to escape a hurried visitor, yet they affect drainage, exposure and the distribution of sediments. Gravel, sand and clay occur at the surface in changing mixtures; beneath them are clay-rich layers and, in places, iron compounds. [@source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021]

The temperate oceanic climate brings both moderation and variability. Rain around flowering or harvest, summer heat and the soil’s water reserves change the task each year. [[concept.terroir|Terroir]] here is not a list of soil names but the interaction of relief, water, climate, roots and human management.
:::

:::detail{#plateau-terrassen-en-water parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021 source.pomerol-syndicate-terroir"}
### Gravel drains, clay holds water

Gravelly topsoil generally lets water pass more readily and warms easily. Clay can retain more water and provide roots with a buffer during dry periods. Sandier sections respond differently again. None of these textures acts alone: a shallow layer of gravel over clay creates a different root environment from deep gravel or predominantly sandy ground.

This water balance is critical for Merlot. Too much water may prolong growth and ripening; prolonged drought can close stomata and halt development. The celebrated words clay and gravel are therefore only the beginning of an explanation. Profile depth, compaction, organic matter, rooting and the weather of the [[concept.vintage|vintage]] complete the story.
:::

:::detail{#crasse-de-fer-zonder-mystiek parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021 source.pomerol-syndicate-terroir"}
### *Crasse de fer* without mysticism

In Pomerol, *crasse de fer* refers to iron-rich deposits or hardened zones in the subsoil. It is not a uniform carpet beneath every celebrated parcel, nor a mineral that travels directly into the glass as a taste of “iron” or “blood”. The term describes one part of a complex soil profile. [@source.pomerol-syndicate-terroir]

Iron compounds may relate to drainage history and soil formation; hardened layers may also affect rooting and water movement. A soil component is not an aroma, however. Attributing every velvety or truffle-scented Pomerol to *crasse de fer* skips over the grape, year, microbiology and winemaking.
:::

## Grape varieties — Merlot leads, Cabernet Franc answers

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]] occupies by far the largest share of Pomerol’s vineyard. It generally ripens earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon and, in this oceanic climate, can build ripe plum- and cherry-like fruit, colour, alcohol and an enveloping texture. Clay-bearing soils may retain enough water for ripening to continue through dry spells.

[[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] is its principal counterpoint. It can contribute aroma, freshness, line and tannic structure. Some estates use little; others give it a substantial voice. The appellation prescribes no fixed blend: Pomerol is Merlot-dominant, not necessarily one hundred per cent Merlot. [@source.pomerol-syndicate-terroir]
:::

:::detail{#merlot-en-cabernet-franc parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.pomerol-syndicate-terroir"}
### Two ripening rhythms in one wine

Merlot’s earlier ripening can help growers harvest before autumn rain, but in hot years it can also accumulate sugar quickly and lose freshness. Cabernet Franc often needs more time and a well-chosen site; fully ripe, it can add perfume and tension, while insufficiently ripe fruit may show a firmer green edge.

The [[concept.assemblage|blend]] therefore begins with planting and parcel choice. Clay, gravel, exposure, vine age and harvest date determine which lots become available. The final proportion is not a regional formula, but an estate’s answer to its land and that particular year.
:::

:::detail{#de-volledige-wettelijke-set parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021"}
### The complete legal set is larger

Alongside Merlot and Cabernet Franc, the specification permits [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]], [[grape.malbec|Côt—also called Malbec or Pressac]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]]. [@source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021] They play much smaller roles in the present vineyard, but their inclusion prevents tradition from being reduced to a two-grape story.

Cabernet Sauvignon can contribute structure and blackcurrant-like fruit but ripens later. Malbec may add colour and flesh; Petit Verdot needs considerable warmth and can bring spice and tannin. These are possible functions, not guaranteed flavours or proof that every variety appears in every bottle.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — precision on small parcels

Pomerol is often described as a collection of small estates. This can support close parcel work and separate vinification, but scale does not guarantee quality. Pruning, canopy, yield, soil care, disease control and harvest timing first determine which grapes reach the cellar.

There, black grapes are generally destemmed and fermented with their skins. Lots may remain separate by parcel, variety and picking date. After fermentation and tasting comes the composition of the wine; it then develops further in barrel, tank or a combination. The appellation provides the framework, not a single cellar recipe.
:::

:::detail{#oogst-extractie-en-assemblage parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021"}
### Picking ripe fruit without counting sugar alone

In Merlot, sugar and soft fruit can rise quickly while skin, seed, acidity and aroma each follow their own rhythm. Later picking may give richness, but may also raise alcohol or reduce freshness; early picking does not automatically preserve elegance if tannin and flavour remain unripe.

During [[concept.maceration|maceration]], temperature, time and movement steer the [[concept.extraction|extraction]] of colour, aroma and [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. Blending then connects lots with different individual qualities. Selection may be strict, but “less wine” is not in itself proof of better wine.
:::

:::detail{#opvoeding-zonder-pomerol-recept parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021"}
### Maturation without a Pomerol recipe

Many ambitious producers use oak barrels, sometimes with a high proportion of new wood. Others favour older oak, larger vessels or additional tanks. [[concept.elevage|Maturation]] can soften tannin, manage oxygen exchange and add aroma, but may also cover fruit and place when oak or extraction falls out of balance.

Techniques such as temperature control, cold soaking or micro-oxygenation are producer choices as well, not part of Pomerol’s definition. A famous method from one estate must never be presented as a general appellation rule.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021 source.legifrance-pomerol-order-2021"}
## Appellation rules — protected origin, not a quality formula

Pomerol is a [[concept.aop|protected designation of origin]] for still red wine. Its rules establish, among other things, where grapes may come from, which varieties are permitted, how vineyards are laid out and which production and analytical requirements the wine must meet. The name is therefore more than a stylistic term.

This control supplies a baseline for origin and method. It does not determine how much Merlot is in an individual bottle, which vessels were used, how severely lots were selected or how long the wine will age gracefully. PDO compliance and exceptional quality are different questions.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021 source.legifrance-pomerol-order-2021"}
### A few current thresholds, and their limits

The specification approved in 2021 requires at least 5,500 vines per hectare, sets a base yield of 49 hectolitres per hectare and an absolute ceiling of 60. Marketable wine may contain no more than 2 grams per litre of glucose plus fructose and 0.30 grams of malic acid per litre; the latter means that [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|malolactic conversion]] must be practically complete. [@source.inao-pomerol-specification-2021] [@source.legifrance-pomerol-order-2021]

These figures protect a production framework, but they do not form a tasting note. A lower actual yield may result from strict selection, but also from hail, disease or drought. High planting density says little without root space, crop per vine and careful management.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.civb-pomerol-overview"}
## Classification and producers — famous names without an official ladder

Pomerol has never established an official classification of its crus. There are therefore no premiers crus, grands crus classés or periodically revised tiers of the kind found elsewhere in Bordeaux. “[[concept.grand-vin|Grand vin]]” on a label, a high price or a celebrated owner does not change this. [@source.civb-pomerol-overview]

The appellation is hardly without hierarchy, however. Tasters, merchants and collectors distinguish sharply between producers on reputation, consistency and scarcity. That informal order can be influential, but it remains changeable and commercial. It does not carry the same authority or scope as a statutory or official classification system.
:::

:::detail{#reputatie-is-geen-rang parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.civb-pomerol-overview"}
### Reputation is not rank

[[producer.petrus|Petrus]] is often treated as Pomerol’s icon; [[producer.le-pin|Le Pin]], with its tiny production, became another symbol of scarcity. Their market status must not be translated backwards into an official category that does not exist. Nor do their prices represent the daily reality of every Pomerol producer.

The absence of classification does not make comparison impossible, but it shifts the responsibility. Consider producer, parcel holdings, winemaking history, vintage and bottle condition. A fixed reputation may lag behind changes in ownership, management or style.
:::

:::detail{#namen-als-orientatie parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview"}
### Names for orientation, not a homemade classification

Alongside Petrus and Le Pin, [[producer.vieux-chateau-certan|Vieux Château Certan]] and [[producer.chateau-lafleur|Château Lafleur]] help explore the highest ambition and different varietal balances. [[producer.chateau-la-conseillante|Château La Conseillante]] and [[producer.chateau-levangile|Château L’Évangile]] lie towards Saint-Émilion; [[producer.chateau-trotanoy|Château Trotanoy]], [[producer.chateau-clinet|Château Clinet]] and [[producer.chateau-gazin|Château Gazin]] broaden the picture further.

This selection is a learning route, not a complete list or ranking. Pomerol also includes less familiar family estates. An overview that names only icons mistakes visibility for the whole area.
:::

:::detail{#producent-le-pin parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.le-pin-francois-thienpont"}
### Le Pin — small estate, large contrast

[[producer.le-pin|Le Pin]] shows how recently Pomerol’s map of reputations could still change. Jacques Thienpont bought the first parcel in 1979; the estate grew to seven parcels covering about two hectares. It is planted entirely with Merlot on a mixture of gravel, clay and sand. Production is tiny and the wine, according to the producer, matures for eighteen months in new barrels. [@source.le-pin-francois-thienpont]

That combination made Le Pin the model of a young cult estate, but “small” and “expensive” are not tasting notes. As a learning reference it contrasts above all with older, larger properties: concentrated Merlot, parcel precision and scarcity created a global reputation without a grand château history or official rank.
:::

:::detail{#producent-vieux-chateau-certan parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.vieux-chateau-certan-estate source.vieux-chateau-certan-vineyard"}
### Vieux Château Certan — three varieties in one historic block

[[producer.vieux-chateau-certan|Vieux Château Certan]] is among Pomerol’s earliest documented estates. The present house took shape under Charles de Bousquet after 1858; wine merchant Georges Thienpont bought the property in 1924. That began a family chapter which continues a century later. [@source.vieux-chateau-certan-estate]

Its contiguous fourteen hectares are divided into 23 parcels. The current planting — about 70% Merlot, 25% Cabernet Franc and 5% Cabernet Sauvignon — is unusually plural for Pomerol. [@source.vieux-chateau-certan-vineyard] Merlot can supply roundness while the Cabernets add aroma, freshness and structure. The exact blend changes with the year; that interplay makes Vieux Château Certan a useful counterweight to the idea that great Pomerol must always be almost pure Merlot.
:::

:::detail{#producent-chateau-lafleur parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.lafleur-official source.decanter-lafleur-vin-de-france-2026"}
### Château Lafleur — Pomerol history with a present-day break

[[producer.chateau-lafleur|Château Lafleur]] is a very small Guinaudeau family property, historically associated with an individual balance of Merlot and Cabernet Franc. The latter can give the wine a taut, aromatic and long-lived profile that differs markedly from the cliché of merely soft Merlot. [@source.lafleur-official]

From the 2025 harvest, the estate releases Lafleur as **Vin de France**, not AOC Pomerol. The family cites the room needed to adapt to climate change. [@source.decanter-lafleur-vin-de-france-2026] Lafleur remains geographically and historically essential to Pomerol, but new bottles no longer legally carry the appellation. This is exactly why a producer, a place and an appellation must remain separate entities.
:::

:::detail{#producent-chateau-la-conseillante parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.la-conseillante-history"}
### Château La Conseillante — family continuity on the eastern edge

[[producer.chateau-la-conseillante|Château La Conseillante]] lies towards Saint-Émilion and takes its name from Catherine Conseillan, who expanded the property to twelve hectares in the eighteenth century. The Nicolas family bought it in 1871. Louis Nicolas helped found Pomerol’s growers’ union in 1900 and gave the bottle and label their recognisable violet and silver details. [@source.la-conseillante-history]

The fifth family generation manages the estate today. Since 2012, a vat room designed for separate parcel vinification has allowed finer selection. [@source.la-conseillante-history] La Conseillante is therefore more than a famous name: it shows how a stable surface area, family continuity and increasingly precise parcel work can coexist within one estate.
:::

:::detail{#producent-chateau-levangile parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.lafite-levangile"}
### Château L’Évangile — between Petrus and Saint-Émilion

[[producer.chateau-levangile|Château L’Évangile]] lies in south-eastern Pomerol: Petrus borders it to the north, while a road separates the estate from Saint-Émilion to the south. Its present 22 hectares contain 43 parcels across clay, gravel and sand; Merlot dominates alongside Cabernet Franc and a little Cabernet Sauvignon. [@source.lafite-levangile]

The Léglise family established the foundations in the eighteenth century. After the Chaperon and Ducasse families, Domaines Barons de Rothschild Lafite acquired the estate in 1990. Organic farming, biodiversity and adaptation to heat and water stress are now explicit parts of management. [@source.lafite-levangile] L’Évangile thus connects a historic border position with a contemporary search for resilience.
:::

:::detail{#producent-chateau-trotanoy parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.moueix-trotanoy"}
### Château Trotanoy — powerful Merlot on heavy clay

[[producer.chateau-trotanoy|Château Trotanoy]] was acquired by Jean-Pierre Moueix in 1953. The present estate covers about 7.2 hectares and is planted with Merlot. Gravel over clay and, in places, dark clay above iron-rich layers create different water and root environments within the property. [@source.moueix-trotanoy]

The producer describes Trotanoy as dense, powerful and intended for long bottle ageing. That style image is useful as long as it is not treated as a guarantee for every bottle: vintage, selection and storage still matter. Within this collection, Trotanoy represents the compact, structured face of Merlot and shows that “velvety Pomerol” can also mean considerable tannin and patience.
:::

:::detail{#producent-chateau-clinet parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-clinet-history"}
### Château Clinet — Merlot with Cabernet Sauvignon

[[producer.chateau-clinet|Château Clinet]] stands on the plateau near Pomerol’s church. According to the estate, the Clinet name has been attached to the vineyard since at least 1595. The Laborde family has owned it since 1999 and Ronan Laborde has led it since 2004. [@source.chateau-clinet-history]

Its 11.5 hectares are currently planted to 75% Merlot and 25% Cabernet Sauvignon. The latter is striking in an appellation where Cabernet Franc is usually Merlot’s principal partner. [@source.chateau-clinet-history] Clinet therefore broadens the varietal story: warmth and flesh can be complemented by Cabernet Sauvignon’s later ripening and structure.
:::

:::detail{#producent-chateau-gazin parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-gazin-estate"}
### Château Gazin — scale on the Pomerol plateau

[[producer.chateau-gazin|Château Gazin]] covers about 26 contiguous hectares on Pomerol’s high terrace. [@source.chateau-gazin-estate] That makes it large in an appellation so often described through tiny estates. Its position and extent provide an important counterexample: Pomerol is not a collection of micro-properties alone.

The estate connects its history with land once associated with the Hospitallers and is managed by descendants of Louis Soualle. Such continuity matters, but is not proof of quality by itself. For this learning route, scale is the principal lesson: a larger contiguous property offers more internal variation in soil and selection than the simple image of one château, one parcel and one style suggests.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.civb-pomerol-overview"}
## Wine style and bottle development — enveloping, but not shapeless

Pomerol is often recognised by ripe red and dark fruit—plum, cherry and blackberry—alongside violets, spice and a full, soft texture. Good examples also retain acidity, length and [[concept.tannin|tannin]]: velvety does not mean sweet, slack or without backbone.

Cabernet Franc can increase perfume and tension; clay and gravel profiles, harvest timing and cellar choices alter the weight. Some wines are seductive when young, while others remain closed for years. This is a range with which to taste, not an aroma checklist that every bottle must complete. [@source.civb-pomerol-overview]
:::

:::detail{#waar-verschillen-ontstaan parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.pomerol-syndicate-terroir"}
### Richness can take different forms

Merlot from a clay-rich profile may seem dense and slow to unfold; sandier ground or a larger share of Cabernet Franc may give a lighter, more fragrant impression. Such patterns are not parcel laws. Vine age, yield, picking date, extraction and oak can reinforce, soften or obscure a soil effect.

Producer ideals differ as well. One seeks maximum ripeness and volume, another earlier-picked freshness and quieter oak. “Typical Pomerol” therefore says less than a combination of place, maker and year.
:::

:::detail{#tijd-in-de-fles parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.civb-pomerol-overview"}
### From fruit towards earth, tobacco and truffle

With bottle age, fresh plum may shift towards dried fruit, leather, tobacco, forest floor and sometimes a truffle-like scent; tannin may soften and the wine gain savoury cohesion. That development is chemical and gradual. It does not occur because truffles grow beside the vines or iron from the soil enters the aroma.

Not every Pomerol needs a long wait. Concentration, acidity, tannin, winemaking, closure, bottle size and storage jointly determine the pace. A modest wine may give its greatest pleasure in youth; a great, well-stored bottle can change for decades without every old vintage automatically being better.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.inao-pomerol-overview"}
## Labels and buying — read the name, not the myth

Look first for the exact appellation name **Pomerol**, then producer, [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]], [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and bottler. A reference to [[concept.estate-bottling|estate bottling]] tells you something about the production chain, not a classification. “[[concept.grand-vin|Grand vin]] de Bordeaux” is not a Pomerol rank either. [@source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025]

For older or expensive bottles, provenance of the bottle—where and how it was stored—is at least as important as the label. Current prices and recommended harvest years age too quickly for this overview. Do not buy a reputation without identifying the actual wine as well.
:::

:::detail{#pomerol-is-niet-lalande parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview"}
### Pomerol is not Lalande-de-Pomerol

Both names are protected and both may produce Merlot-dominant red wine. Yet “Lalande-de-Pomerol” cannot be shortened to Pomerol, just as a producer name cannot replace the appellation. Read the whole line and do not let price or graphic design hide the hyphens.

Nor does the word *château* guarantee a monumental building, while Le Pin and Petrus need no such prefix to be recognisable producers. The legally meaningful elements are origin, bottler and compulsory information; prestige resides partly in reputation beyond those rules.
:::

:::detail{#serveren-en-bewaren parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-pomerol-overview"}
### Serve for age and structure

Serve red Pomerol cool rather than warm, in a generous glass. A young, compact wine may benefit from air or careful decanting; an old bottle instead needs rest, time upright for sediment and gentle pouring. Age alone does not decide the approach—taste first when possible.

Store bottles in darkness, without vibration and at as stable and cool a temperature as possible. At the table, classic pairings with poultry, beef, lamb and mushrooms work, but so do simpler dishes with enough savoury depth. The wine need not be “justified” by heavy food.
:::

## Contemporary developments — a famous plateau remains farmland

High prices can obscure that Pomerol is first a working agricultural area. Vines age, diseases change, soils need protection and labour must be available for short harvest windows. Small family enterprises and internationally financed estates do not have the same capacity to invest.

At the same time, producers renew cellars, follow parcels more closely and reconsider tillage, canopy, selection and oak. Modernisation does not automatically erase tradition; nor does tradition prove that every old habit remains agronomically or stylistically ideal.
:::

:::detail{#warmte-water-en-merlot parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation"}
### Heat makes ripeness easier and timing harder

Warmer years can ripen Merlot reliably, but may also drive sugar upwards quickly as acidity falls. Longer drought puts extra pressure on sandy or freely draining sections; clay can buffer water, but is not an inexhaustible reservoir. Heavy rain at the wrong moment remains another side of the same volatile climate. [@source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation]

Adaptation consists of connected choices: pruning date, canopy, ground cover, organic matter, [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]], harvest timing and the place of Cabernet Franc. No measure works identically every year. Pomerol’s future will be tasted not simply in greater ripeness, but in the ability to manage tension and water.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Pomerol does not derive its identity from château architecture or an official ranking. It is a small, Merlot-dominant landscape in which subtle differences of soil and water, family history, trade and producer choices create remarkably varied red wines.
:::
