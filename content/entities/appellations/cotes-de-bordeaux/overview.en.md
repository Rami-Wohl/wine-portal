:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-cotes-de-bordeaux"}
Côtes de Bordeaux is not one continuous area around a single town, but a shared AOP for five groups of hillside vineyards along the Gironde, Garonne and Dordogne. The umbrella name on its own applies only to red wine; [[appellation.blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux|Blaye]], [[appellation.cadillac-cotes-de-bordeaux|Cadillac]], [[appellation.castillon-cotes-de-bordeaux|Castillon]], [[appellation.francs-cotes-de-bordeaux|Francs]] and [[appellation.sainte-foy-cotes-de-bordeaux|Sainte-Foy]] may be added as more precise geographical denominations.

That shared surname makes the areas recognisable as a family without erasing their differences. From the estuary at Blaye to easterly Sainte-Foy, river, soil, climate and permitted wine colour change appreciably.
:::

## Location and boundaries — five islands of slopes

The AOP is dispersed across 110 communes in [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. Its five historic centres lie around Blaye on the Gironde, Cadillac on the Garonne, Castillon and Francs north of the Dordogne, and Sainte-Foy south of it. Only officially delimited or identified parcels within eligible communes qualify.

The word *Côtes* evokes slopes and plateaus above the river valleys. It signals a landscape relationship, not one continuous boundary or one uniform wine.
:::

:::detail{#naam-en-plaatsnaam parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Name and place name

“Côtes de Bordeaux” can appear alone on red wine. “Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux” or “Castillon Côtes de Bordeaux” adds a smaller origin and further conditions. The place name is not a quality rank: it identifies where the grapes grew and which local rules apply.

The five denominations do not share exactly the same colours. Cadillac and Castillon are red; Blaye makes red and dry white; Francs adds dry and sweet white; Sainte-Foy covers red, dry white, moelleux and liquoreux.
:::

## History — separate ports, one collective name

The rivers served as arteries for centuries. Wine from towns, bastides and hillside villages travelled down the Garonne, Dordogne and Gironde to Bordeaux and then to Atlantic markets. Related trading cultures consequently developed in places that are far apart on the ground.

The five centres received separate protected names during the twentieth century. They began formal cooperation in 1975, and Côtes de Bordeaux was recognised as a collective AOC in 2009. Sainte-Foy joined in 2016. Older bottles and books may therefore name Premières Côtes de Blaye, Premières Côtes de Bordeaux, Côtes de Castillon, Bordeaux-Côtes de Francs or Sainte-Foy-Bordeaux. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::detail{#samenwerken-zonder-gelijktrekken parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Cooperation without uniformity

The union combined protection and recognition while retaining the historic sectors in one specification. Legally they are supplementary geographical denominations; editorially each deserves its own page because location, colours and local history differ materially.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — rivers below limestone-rich hills

Slopes, plateaus and valley floors form the recurring pattern. Clay and limestone are widespread, while sand, silt and gravel mark terraces and lower ground. Drainage, aspect and water storage therefore vary both within and between the five centres.

Atlantic influence and large bodies of water moderate the climate. Farther east, around Francs and Sainte-Foy, continental influence strengthens and vintage variation is often more pronounced. River mist can help create conditions for noble rot at Sainte-Foy, but does not guarantee it each year.
:::

:::detail{#een-familie-geen-terroirformule parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### One family is no terroir formula

Clay-limestone can support Merlot with water in dry periods; gravel can drain and warm more quickly. These are possibilities, not fixed aromas. Soil depth, roots, site, season and decisions in vineyard and cellar remain necessary to understand a wine.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot at the centre, more colours at the edges

For red, [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] are principal varieties. Merlot is often the pivot; the others can add fragrance, freshness, colour and tannin. [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] are accessory varieties.

Where white is permitted, [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.sauvignon-gris|Sauvignon Gris]], [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]] form the principal group. Colombard and Ugni Blanc are limited accessories.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — blending as an answer to place and year

Red ferments with skins and undergoes malolactic conversion. Producers adjust extraction, [[concept.assemblage|blending]] and maturation to ripeness and intended structure; oak is an option, not a compulsory style badge.

Dry white is pressed and generally fermented cooler. In Francs and Sainte-Foy, sweet white can come from overripe grapes affected by noble rot and/or drying on the vine, hand-picked in successive passes. This is a separate production route, not merely dry wine with residual sugar.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Appellation rules — one specification with local layers

The current specification governs area, parcels, grapes, pruning, yield, ripeness and cellar practice. Côtes de Bordeaux without a place name protects still red wine. The five geographical denominations add their own area and sometimes other colours or stricter values.

These rules provide controllable origin and production discipline, not an automatic ranking of the five names or a guarantee of producer quality.
:::

:::detail{#gedeelde-en-afwijkende-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Shared and differing thresholds

The base yield is 55 hectolitres per hectare for red Côtes de Bordeaux and 52 for red with a geographical denomination. Minimum planting density is generally 4,500 vines per hectare; Castillon requires 5,000. Reds mature at least until 15 March after harvest and reach consumers from 31 March. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]

Transitional provisions and analytical limits make the specification more complex than this learning overview. Production decisions always require the current official text.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-cotes-de-bordeaux source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Classification and producers — an origin family, not a ranking

Côtes de Bordeaux has no collective official château classification. The name unites independent estates, merchants and cooperative structures. A château, “grand vin” or heavy bottle supplies no legal rank within this AOP.

Read producer and cuvée beside origin. The denomination pages can later group representative producers without turning every château into a standalone monograph.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-cotes-de-bordeaux"}
## Wine style and bottle development — approachable red to serious cellar wine

Red ranges from juicy and early-drinking to fuller and firmer, often around plum, red and dark fruit, spice and soft to distinct tannin. More extraction, riper Cabernet or Malbec and longer maturation can increase structure and ageing potential, but producer and vintage weigh heavily.

White styles range further: taut and dry in Blaye, Francs and Sainte-Foy, with concentrated sweet wines also possible in the latter two. Reading “Côtes de Bordeaux” as one style therefore misses the most important local information.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — read from family to detail

Begin with the complete name. Côtes de Bordeaux alone means red from the wider collective area. Blaye, Cadillac, Castillon, Francs or Sainte-Foy points to a smaller area and its own colour possibilities.

Then read colour or sweetness, producer, cuvée, vintage and bottler. Do not confuse Côtes de Bordeaux with [[appellation.cotes-de-bourg|Côtes de Bourg]]: that neighbouring name belongs to a separate AOP.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Contemporary developments — recognisable without losing difference

The collective name makes five less straightforward origins easier to recognise internationally. The challenge is also to show why Castillon is not Cadillac and why the whites of Francs and Sainte-Foy are not footnotes to red.

Heat, drought, intense rain and disease pressure demand different responses in canopy, soil, picking and grape mix on each slope. The family shares the problem; the answer remains local.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Côtes de Bordeaux is a shared AOP for red wine and the legal roof over five geographical denominations. Slopes, rivers and one specification connect the family; the place name on the label identifies which local world speaks within it.
:::
