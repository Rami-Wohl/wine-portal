Montagne-Saint-Émilion occupies hills north of the Barbanne, opposite the plateau of [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]]. It is an independent red appellation in the commune of Montagne, not an outer district or lower tier of Saint-Émilion.

Limestone plateaus, clay-limestone slopes and western sandy-gravel terraces give Merlot and the Cabernets different places to ripen.
:::

## Location and boundaries — hills above the Barbanne

The legal zone follows the commune of Montagne, about ten kilometres northeast of Libourne. Within it, the villages of Montagne, Parsac and Saint-Georges occupy rises above the Barbanne valley.

Not all this land carries the same wine name: [[appellation.saint-georges-saint-emilion|Saint-Georges-Saint-Émilion]] has its own boundary inside the modern commune. Parcel maps, not merely a postal address, determine the appellation.
:::

:::detail{#satelliet-zonder-ondergeschiktheid parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-montagne-saint-emilion-overview"}
### “Satellite” is an orientation word

The customary collective term identifies four appellations around Saint-Émilion. It does not make Montagne-Saint-Émilion legally subordinate to Saint-Émilion or subject to the same classification.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-montagne-saint-emilion-overview source.boagri-montagne-saint-emilion-specification-2019"}
## History — the commune won the right to Saint-Émilion

Roman remains and medieval religious holdings testify to a long agricultural history. Trade through Libourne and the Dordogne connected the wine with markets beyond the district.

After legal disputes over origin, Montagne was allowed to append “Saint-Émilion” to its commune name in 1921. The Montagne-Saint-Émilion AOC was recognised in 1936. The double name preserves a hard-won historic usage, not geographic inclusion in the old Jurade.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-montagne-saint-emilion-specification-2019 source.inao-montagne-saint-emilion-overview"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — three faces of Montagne

Oligocene limestone forms the plateaus; incised slopes carry clay-limestone and molasse. The Isle left sand and gravel in the west, while lower positions contain sandy clay and silt. Delimitation favours soils and positions with sufficient natural drainage.

The Atlantic climate gains warmer summers and cooler nights inland. The Barbanne and relief create local differences in frost, wind and water. “Montagne” means mountain, but this is rolling hill country rather than high-altitude viticulture.
:::

:::detail{#plateau-helling-terras parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-montagne-saint-emilion-specification-2019"}
### Plateau, slope and terrace ask for different work

Clay-limestone can supply Merlot through dry spells; gravel drains and warms earlier; footslopes can be cooler and wetter. Rooting, aspect and harvest date therefore vary by parcel even within one commune.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-montagne-saint-emilion-specification-2019"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot first, four principal grapes permitted

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] are principal grapes; Carménère and Petit Verdot are accessory. Merlot dominates in practice and suits cool clay-limestone ground.

Cabernet Franc can bring freshness and fragrance, Cabernet Sauvignon structure on warmer soil and Malbec colour and spice. Principal grapes make up at least seventy per cent of the final blend.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-montagne-saint-emilion-specification-2019"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — leaf area for complete ripeness

At least 5,500 vines per hectare, limited buds and compulsory trellising aim for well-spaced bunches and enough active leaf. In the cellar, skin contact, extraction pace and the choice among tank, concrete and oak shape texture more than the commune name alone.

Wine completes malolactic conversion and matures until at least 15 March after harvest. Oak is possible but not compulsory.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-montagne-saint-emilion-specification-2019"}
## Appellation rules — red from Montagne

Montagne-Saint-Émilion protects still red wine from delimited parcels in Montagne. Base yield is 53 hectolitres per hectare and minimum natural alcohol is 11%. Consumer release begins no earlier than 31 March after harvest. [@source.boagri-montagne-saint-emilion-specification-2019]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-montagne-saint-emilion-overview"}
## Classification and producers — no Grand Cru Classé system

The Saint-Émilion Grand Cru classification does not apply here. Montagne-Saint-Émilion has no official château ranking. Its many family estates and varied parcels can nevertheless create clear producer differences.

A château or cuvée name is not a classification. Look for concrete information about site, blend and maturation.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-montagne-saint-emilion-overview"}
## Wine style and bottle development — roundness with a limestone backbone

Typical examples combine plum and red fruit with spice and medium to firm tannin. Merlot can give early roundness; Cabernet and careful extraction may increase freshness and ageing capacity. Leather, tobacco and earth can emerge with bottle age.

This remains a range. Serve around 16–18°C and adjust air and cellaring to producer and vintage.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-montagne-saint-emilion-specification-2019"}
## Labels and buying — Montagne belongs to the name

A bottle from this PDO states Montagne-Saint-Émilion in full. Do not let the larger words “Saint-Émilion” conceal the communal origin. “Grand Cru” and the Saint-Émilion classification do not follow automatically from the name.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation"}
## Contemporary developments — reassessing ripeness and water

Warmer years help late-ripening Cabernet but accelerate sugar accumulation in Merlot. Ground cover, shade and water infiltration must therefore be adjusted across plateau, slope and terrace. Intense rain keeps erosion control high on the agenda.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Montagne-Saint-Émilion is an independent red appellation on limestone plateaus, slopes and terraces north of the Barbanne. Its shared name links it historically with Saint-Émilion, but boundary, classification and wine remain its own.
:::
