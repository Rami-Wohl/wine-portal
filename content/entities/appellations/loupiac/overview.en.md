:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-loupiac-overview source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
**Loupiac** is a small sweet-white appellation contained within one commune on the Right Bank of the Garonne. Limestone-rich slopes, autumn mist and sunny, airy periods can support concentration of ripe grapes here. Sémillon usually carries the wine; successive hand selections determine which grapes are actually picked.

Loupiac lies between Cadillac and Sainte-Croix-du-Mont. It shares their broad sweet-wine logic, but not their name, exact boundary or every legal threshold.
:::

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## Location and boundaries — one commune above the Garonne

The appellation lies about forty kilometres southeast of Bordeaux and is geographically limited to the commune of Loupiac. Even there, not every piece of land qualifies automatically: wine must come from officially delimited parcels. Harvest, vinification, production and maturation normally take place within the commune, with a defined nearby area as a practical exception. [@source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026]

The landscape adjoins [[appellation.cadillac|Cadillac]] to the north and [[appellation.sainte-croix-du-mont|Sainte-Croix-du-Mont]] to the south. That proximity makes comparison useful, but does not turn three appellations into one origin name.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## History — a trade wine on an ancient inhabited slope

A Gallo-Roman villa testifies to an old agricultural landscape around Loupiac, but does not by itself prove that today's wine style existed then. The sweet-wine tradition took shape much later through Bordeaux's trade with England and especially Holland. During the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, sweet white wines, keeping and transport became increasingly important.

After phylloxera, vineyards were replanted with Sémillon, Sauvignon and Muscadelle. Loupiac received its AOC in 1936 within commune boundaries that had previously been established in law. [@source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026]
:::

:::detail{#handel-en-erkenning parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
### Reputation without a modern ranking

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century writers compared Loupiac with famous sweet origins, while prosperous Bordelais built houses on the slopes. Such historic reputation language shows how the wine was positioned then; it is not a current classification or evidence that every producer reaches the same level.

Its durable identity therefore lies not in a château ranking, but in delimited origin, grape varieties, selective harvest and collective rules.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — limestone, slope, wooded valley

Loupiac climbs from the foot of the coteau to a limestone plateau that can exceed one hundred metres. South- and southwest-facing slopes look towards the Garonne; small stream valleys cut into the plateau. Vineyards alternate with old deciduous woodland and cooler wooded hollows. [@source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026]

Beneath the vines are combinations of limestone with sandy-gravelly clay, molasse, calcareous brown soils and, lower on the slope, silt, sandy clay and old gravel. Poorly drained bottoms and modern river deposits are excluded from the appellation's vineyard selection.
:::

:::detail{#water-en-mist parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
### A useful autumn sits between wet and dry

Mist and moisture from the Garonne may encourage [[concept.botrytis|Botrytis cinerea]] in the morning. Sun, wind and freely draining slopes then help remove moisture. When that alternation works, berries shrivel and sugars and aromas concentrate.

If wet conditions persist, harmful rot becomes more likely; if grapes dry without botrytis, passerillage on the vine may carry the concentration. The appellation accepts both routes, but always requires overripe selected fruit.
:::

## Grape varieties — four white varieties, no fixed blend

[[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] is usually the principal grape and suits concentration on the vine. [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]] and [[grape.sauvignon-gris|Sauvignon Gris]] can add freshness and aromatic tension; [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]] may contribute a floral, spicy nuance.

All four are legally permitted, but their actual proportions vary by estate and year. The difficulty of botrytis selection makes a fixed blending recipe even less plausible.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — several passes through the same row

Grapes must be overripe, botrytised and/or shrivelled on the vine. Pickers harvest exclusively by hand in successive tries. A parcel can therefore be partly picked while other bunches remain. [@source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026]

After careful pressing, the sugar-rich juice ferments slowly. Final balance depends on concentration, acidity, where fermentation ends and choices of barrel, tank and maturation. None of those cellar choices can repair unhealthy fruit.
:::

:::detail{#tries-zonder-zekerheid parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
### Selection makes output variable

Every extra pass costs labour and exposes the remaining grapes to new weather. Volume can therefore vary sharply, and a producer may decide to use fruit for another permitted origin. Sweet wine here is not a by-product after dry wine, but a deliberate, risky selection.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## Appellation rules — the name protects place and method

Loupiac is reserved for still white wine with residual sugar. Besides commune and parcels, the rules cover varieties, planting, overripeness, hand picking by tries, yield and minimum maturation. They make the category recognisable, but do not automatically guarantee flavour, condition or ageing potential.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
### The current figures behind the name

The 2026 specification requires at least 5,000 vines per hectare. Must must contain at least 238 grams of sugar per litre for Sémillon and 229 grams for other varieties. Base yield is 40 hectolitres per hectare; wine contains at least 45 grams of fermentable sugar per litre at bottling and matures at least until 15 January after harvest. [@source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026]

A provision for slopes above 33 percent permits slightly wider row spacing. This confirms locally steep terrain, but does not turn all Loupiac into a steep wall.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## Classification and producers — no official ranking

Loupiac has no official château classification. Producers nevertheless differ in sites, strictness of their tries, cellar choices and consistency. Those differences deserve judgement by estate and vintage, not a ranking invented by this platform.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-loupiac-overview source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## Wine style and bottle development — sweetness with nerve

Loupiac can combine ripe white and yellow fruit, citrus, flowers, honey and preserved notes with a full texture. Fresh acidity and sometimes a slight bitter or spicy edge provide direction. Origin alone dictates no aroma: botrytis, passerillage, barrel and vintage alter the wine markedly.

Bottle age may deepen the fruit towards dried apricot, marmalade, wax, spice and nuts. Ageing time is never a fixed appellation promise; concentration, acidity, producer, closure and storage all matter.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — its own name, not a small Sauternes

Loupiac on the label means sweet white appellation wine from the delimited commune. Then read producer, vintage, bottler and volume. [[appellation.sauternes|Sauternes]] across the river is more famous, but Loupiac is neither its subzone nor a quality tier within it.

The useful comparison is how place, soils, harvest selection and rules differ—not which name should rank higher.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.boagri-loupiac-specification-2026"}
## Contemporary developments — pressure on the harvest window

Faster ripening can raise sugar early while acidity and healthy skins follow another rhythm. At the same time, drought, heat or heavy autumn rain may shorten the window for noble rot. Selection, ground cover, canopy and harvest planning therefore matter increasingly.

Labour and demand for sweet wine also shape how much Loupiac is actually made. The appellation remains credible when producers bottle only what meets the selection threshold, not when they chase the same volume every year.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Loupiac is a one-commune appellation where limestone-rich Right Bank slopes, autumn weather and successive hand selections make sweet white wine possible. Comparison with its neighbours is illuminating; its boundary and name remain distinct.
:::
