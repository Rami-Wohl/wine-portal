Sainte-Foy Côtes de Bordeaux lies at Bordeaux's far eastern edge, around the bastide of Sainte-Foy-la-Grande and the Left Bank of the Dordogne. No other geographical denomination within Côtes de Bordeaux has such a broad palette: red, dry white, moelleux and liquoreux.

The river is more than scenery. It connected the area to trade, cut slopes and terraces, and can bring late-summer moisture and mist that matter for sweet white wine.

## Location and boundaries — Bordeaux at the border

Nineteen communes form the zone around Sainte-Foy-la-Grande near the Dordogne and Lot-et-Garonne departments. Vineyards occupy valley ground, slopes and higher rolling land on the Left Bank of the Dordogne within [[region.entre-deux-mers|Entre-deux-Mers]].

Sainte-Foy is a geographical denomination within [[appellation.cotes-de-bordeaux|Côtes de Bordeaux]]. Parcels are identified under official criteria; a commune boundary alone does not confer automatic entitlement.
:::

:::detail{#een-grensstreek-met-eigen-richting parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### A border district with its own outlook

Through the Dordogne, Sainte-Foy looks towards both Bordeaux and Bergerac. The position explains a landscape and wine culture that do not fit a simple Left- or Right-Bank picture. Its legal identity remains Bordeaux; its geographical outlook is broader.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.sainte-foy-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — a bastide that sent wine travelling

Sainte-Foy-la-Grande was founded as a bastide on the Dordogne in 1255 and became a trading centre where wine could be assembled and shipped. The river gave surrounding villages access to the Atlantic commercial world.

A court judgment distinguished Sainte-Foy-Bordeaux in 1928; AOC recognition followed in 1937. Red increased after the 1956 frost, but dry and sweet white survived. In 2016 Sainte-Foy became the fifth geographical denomination within Côtes de Bordeaux. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::detail{#rood-werd-groter-wit-bleef parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Red grew; white remained

The post-war shift towards more black grapes changed the production balance, not the whole identity. By protecting four styles, current rules preserve a white tradition easily lost in broad accounts of Bordeaux.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.sainte-foy-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — from gravel valley to clay-limestone heights

Lower ground contains gravel, sand and river deposits; clay-gravel and clay-limestone occur on slopes and heights. They differ in drainage, warming and water reserve. Stream valleys dissect the plateau and create local changes in air movement and frost.

The eastern position brings more continental influence than the estuary. The Dordogne can also produce humid, mild conditions and nocturnal mist in late summer. This can encourage noble rot, but dry wind and sound fruit remain necessary if rot is not to become destructive.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Grape varieties — two palettes for four styles

Red chiefly uses [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and Malbec. Merlot often supplies the core; Cabernets can add freshness, fragrance and structure.

White rests on [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], Sauvignon Gris, [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and Muscadelle. Sémillon is particularly suited to concentrated sweet white; Sauvignon helps retain freshness. Grape alone does not decide style: harvest timing and selection separate dry, moelleux and liquoreux.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — from skin fermentation to successive picking

Red ferments on skins, undergoes malolactic conversion and is then blended and matured. Dry white is picked earlier, pressed and made for freshness and texture.

Moelleux processes riper grapes while retaining noticeable sugar. Liquoreux requires overripe fruit concentrated by noble rot and/or drying on the vine, with manual *tries successives*: repeated passes taking only suitable bunches or berries.
:::

:::detail{#zoet-is-geen-enkele-schaal parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Sweetness is not one sliding scale

Moelleux and liquoreux differ in more than “how sweet”. Rules require different must weights, sugar bands, harvest choices and maturation. Acidity, concentration and botrytis character then determine whether sweetness feels vivid or heavy.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Appellation rules — four styles explicitly protected

Sainte-Foy Côtes de Bordeaux protects still red, dry white, white moelleux and white liquoreux. *Moelleux* is compulsory when fermentable sugar lies between 17 and 45 grams per litre; liquoreux starts at 51 grams per litre.

Rules for parcels, grapes, ripeness, yield, harvesting and maturation differ by type. “White Sainte-Foy” without a sweetness category is therefore incomplete information.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Current thresholds

Minimum planting density is 4,500 vines per hectare. Base yields are 52 hectolitres per hectare for red, 62 for dry white, 45 for moelleux and 37 for liquoreux. Moelleux matures at least until 15 December of the harvest year; liquoreux until 15 March following it. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.sainte-foy-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview"}
## Classification and producers — diversity without château class

Sainte-Foy has no official château classification. Small estates and divergent wine styles also make a simple hierarchy unhelpful: a producer strong in red does not automatically represent dry and sweet white.

A future collection should therefore orient by style and practice without presenting its selection as a quality classification.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.sainte-foy-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview"}
## Wine style and bottle development — from cherry to honey

Red can pair cherry, plum and spice with a fresh opening and firmer finish. Dry white moves around citrus, white flowers and juicy fruit. Moelleux is softer and rounder; liquoreux can develop honey, apricot, candied citrus and botrytis spice.

Serve red around 16–18 °C and white around 8–11 °C. Dry white is often most vivid young; concentrated sweet white may develop longer when acidity, sugar and extract are balanced.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — find the style beside the name

Read the full origin Sainte-Foy Côtes de Bordeaux and then check colour and sweetness. *Moelleux* is a regulated term; liquoreux is the sweeter category. Ignoring those words on a white label can lead to a very different bottle from the one expected.

Older bottles may say Sainte-Foy-Bordeaux. Producer, vintage and bottler remain necessary for navigating the broad stylistic choice.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Contemporary developments — four styles in a less predictable climate

Sainte-Foy can distinguish itself through diversity, but four styles also require four clear stories. Moelleux and liquoreux in particular deserve explanation as historic, labour-intensive wines rather than a residual category.

Warmer, drier summers and unstable autumns change ripening and botrytis pressure. Some years help concentration; others increase drought stress or unwanted rot. Flexible harvesting and soil care therefore matter even more.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Sainte-Foy Côtes de Bordeaux is the eastern border appellation around the Dordogne and the most stylistically diverse Côtes denomination. Red, dry white, moelleux and liquoreux share place but each requires its own reading of grape, harvest and cellar.
:::
