**Port is a fortified wine from Portugal's demarcated Douro region.** Grape spirit is added during fermentation, retaining some of the grape sugar. Ageing then plays a central role in determining the wine in the glass: fruit-focused ruby, wood-matured tawny, or a white or rosé expression. [@source.ivdp-port-introduction] [@source.ivdp-port-vinification]
:::

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-introduction source.ivdp-douro-characteristics"}
## Location and boundaries

The grapes come from the Douro region in northern Portugal, along the river and its tributaries. The name Port is connected with the harbour city of Porto, while the vineyards lie inland. The demarcated region is divided into Baixo Corgo, Cima Corgo and Douro Superior. [@source.ivdp-douro-characteristics]

Porto is the protected wine name; Port is its familiar English form. The same region also produces wine under the name Douro. A shared regional origin therefore does not make every Douro wine Port. For Port, origin, fortification and ageing belong together. [@source.ivdp-port-introduction] [@source.ivdp-douro-characteristics]
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-history"}
## History

The export trade connected the Douro vineyards with Porto and Vila Nova de Gaia. When commercial interests and confidence in origin came under pressure, the Companhia Geral da Agricultura das Vinhas do Alto Douro was established in 1756. Demarcation and supervision were intended to protect origin and trade. Today's wine reflects the subsequent development of winemaking, commerce and cellar ageing. [@source.ivdp-port-history]
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-douro-characteristics"}
## Landscape, climate and soils

Deep valleys, steep slopes and predominantly schist-derived soils characterise the Douro. Mountains limit the arrival of moist Atlantic air. Summers are hot and dry, and rainfall generally decreases eastwards. Altitude and slope orientation introduce considerable local variation. Terraces and other vineyard layouts make cultivation on the hillsides possible. [@source.ivdp-douro-characteristics]
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-douro-grapes"}
## Grape varieties

Red Port is often a blend of several varieties. Touriga Franca, Touriga Nacional and Tinta Roriz are important Douro grapes; Tinta Roriz is the local name for Tempranillo. Tinta Barroca also contributes, among others. A variety's contribution depends on the site and intended blend: there is no fixed recipe for every Port. White Port uses white varieties, including Malvasia Fina, Rabigato and Viosinho. [@source.ivdp-douro-grapes]
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-vinification"}
## Viticulture and winemaking

For red Port, colour and other skin components are extracted during a relatively short fermentation. The maker then adds grape spirit while sugar remains. The increase in alcohol stops fermentation. **The timing of addition helps determine how much sugar is retained.** This is an application of [[concept.fortification|fortification]]. [@source.ivdp-port-vinification]

Wines are subsequently selected, blended and aged. Large vessels shape development differently from extended ageing in smaller wooden casks. Vessel choice, time and subsequent bottle ageing help explain the categories. [@source.ivdp-port-vinification]

## Appellation rules

Port is a regulated origin name supervised by the IVDP, the Instituto dos Vinhos do Douro e do Porto. Special categories such as Vintage, LBV and colheita have their own requirements for harvest year, ageing and certification. A year on the bottle therefore becomes meaningful when read alongside its category. [@source.ivdp-port-introduction] [@source.ivdp-port-categories]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-categories source.ivdp-port-history"}
## Classification and producers

A Port category primarily tells you what product and ageing course you are buying. A producer selects grapes and wines and develops a house style within those conditions. Historical shipping houses and vineyard businesses form part of that development. Compare category, producer and individual bottle separately: a familiar name does not explain the ageing route. [@source.ivdp-port-categories] [@source.ivdp-port-history]
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-introduction source.ivdp-port-categories"}
## Wine style and bottle development

**Ruby** seeks to preserve red colour, fruit and structure. **Tawny** develops more amber-brown hues and dried-fruit and nut aromas through wood ageing. The extent of development varies by category. White Port also varies in sweetness and ageing; rosé acquires its colour through limited skin contact and is generally consumed young. [@source.ivdp-port-introduction]

Vintage can develop for a long time after bottling. A tawny with an age indication has already undergone its characteristic development mainly in wood. “Older” therefore calls for another question: aged in which vessel, or in which bottle? [@source.ivdp-port-categories]
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-categories"}
## Labels and buying

Three names help distinguish bottles carrying a harvest year:

- **Vintage:** one harvest, relatively early bottling, with substantial further bottle development.
- **Late Bottled Vintage (LBV):** one harvest, later bottling. Look for filtering information too; unfiltered LBV can develop sediment and continue to evolve.
- **Colheita:** one harvest, with at least seven years in wood. The bottling year helps explain the length of that ageing.

A tawny labelled, for example, **20 years** is a blended maturation profile with a regulated age indication. The number is not a harvest year. [@source.ivdp-port-categories]
:::

:::detail{#bottelmoment parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-categories"}
### The bottling point behind the name

Vintage is certified by the IVDP in the second year after harvest and may be bottled until 30 July of the third year. For LBV, certification falls in the fourth year and bottling may take place until the end of the sixth year. A different bottling point therefore gives the wine a different history before bottle ageing begins. [@source.ivdp-port-categories]
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-categories"}
## Contemporary developments

Current IVDP categories also include age indications of fifty and eighty years, and Very Very Old (VVO) for more than eighty years of wood ageing. These names make long-maintained cellar stocks visible. Reading the category remains important: a very old wood-aged Port and an old Vintage have followed different maturation routes. [@source.ivdp-port-categories]
:::
