Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux comes from the Right Bank of the Gironde, around Blaye's citadel and the limestone and clay slopes behind it. The appellation makes chiefly Merlot-led red wine, but also dry white.

The full name matters. Legally, *Blaye* is a supplementary geographical denomination within [[appellation.cotes-de-bordeaux|Côtes de Bordeaux]] AOP. It is not the whole [[region.blayais-bourgeais|Blayais and Bourgeais region]], nor [[appellation.cotes-de-bourg|Côtes de Bourg]].
:::

## Location and boundaries — forty communes on the Right Bank

The eligible area covers forty communes around Blaye, north of Bourg and opposite the Médoc across the estuary. Only parcels officially delimited for the appellation within those communes may carry the name.

Vineyards run from river-facing slopes to inland plateaus and valleys. Blaye is not one simple strip beside the water. Elevation, aspect and soil change over short distances.
:::

:::detail{#een-benaming-binnen-een-aop parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### A denomination within a shared AOP

Since 2009, Côtes de Bordeaux has united several geographical denominations in one specification: Blaye, Cadillac, Castillon, Francs and Sainte-Foy. They share a legal framework but retain their own boundaries, colours and additional rules.

A bottle labelled “Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux” is therefore more specific than “Côtes de Bordeaux”. The words form the origin together; *Blaye* is no separate château rank or cuvée name.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — from port wine to the collective Côtes

Blaye occupies a strategic point on the Gironde. Trade, defence and shipping connected the town to Bordeaux and Atlantic markets for centuries. Vineyards on surrounding slopes supplied wine to that current.

Premières Côtes de Blaye was recognised as an AOC in 1936. From the late twentieth century, several *Côtes* areas sought a clearer shared profile; in 2009 this produced Côtes de Bordeaux AOP with Blaye as a geographical denomination. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::detail{#een-naamsverandering-met-inhoud parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### A name change with substance

The move to Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux was more than new typography. Production conditions entered a shared specification while Blaye retained specific provisions for red and white. Older labels and books may therefore use a different name for a substantially related origin.

Historical bottles require vintage and then-current law to be read together. The current name should not be projected carelessly backward.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.gironde-landscape-blayais-libournais"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — slopes above the Gironde

Limestone and clay-limestone soils matter around Blaye, alongside sandy and gravelly deposits. Slopes provide drainage and different aspects; plateaus catch wind and clay can retain water in dry periods.

The estuary moderates temperatures, but the Atlantic climate brings humidity and rain risk. Healthy fruit therefore requires open canopies, suitable soil management and a fast response to disease pressure.
:::

:::detail{#bodem-is-geen-aromastempel parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Soil is not an aroma stamp

Merlot can find even water supply on clay, while Cabernet may ripen later on warmer, well-drained sites. Sauvignon and Sémillon can join freshness to texture on limestone and clay. These are growth relationships, not guaranteed flavours.

Yield, picking, fermentation and maturation can strengthen or blur differences between nearby parcels. A soil label without producer context remains incomplete.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot in red, Sauvignon and Sémillon in white

Red uses [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] and [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] as principal varieties, with [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]] accessory. Merlot generally ripens early and gives rounded fruit; Cabernets and Malbec can add freshness, fragrance and structure.

Dry white relies chiefly on [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.sauvignon-gris|Sauvignon Gris]], [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]]. Sauvignon preserves tension, Sémillon can add breadth and Muscadelle a floral accent.
:::

:::detail{#assemblage-volgt-plek-en-jaar parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Blending follows place and year

A producer can combine Merlot from cooler clay with Cabernet from a warmer slope. Early-ripening Merlot can reduce risk in a wet year; Cabernet gets more time in a long dry autumn. [[concept.assemblage|Blending]] distributes both character and seasonal risk.

For white, a Sauvignon-led cuvée can be taut and citrus-driven, whereas more Sémillon or lees contact brings texture. Neither route defines the entire appellation alone.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — two colours, two rhythms

Red ferments with skins to extract colour and tannin. The winemaker controls pumping over or cap immersion, usually follows with malolactic conversion and chooses tank, concrete or wood for maturation. The best extraction suits the fruit's ripeness.

For white, grapes are pressed and oxidation restrained. Cool fermentation can preserve citrus and flowers; lees or cask can add roundness. Freshness remains the dry style's backbone.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Appellation rules — Blaye adds precision

The specification determines forty communes, parcel delimitation, grapes, ripeness, yield, planting density and cellar practice. Blaye is permitted for still red and dry white. Red also has compulsory minimum maturation.

Rules make the name controllable but do not standardise château style. A fruit-led tank cuvée and a longer cask-aged selection can both be legitimate.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Current thresholds

Planting density is at least 4,500 vines per hectare. Base yield is 52 hectolitres per hectare for red and 62 for white; ceilings are 65 and 72. Minimum natural potential alcohol is 11.5 and 10.5 per cent respectively. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]

Red matures until at least 15 March after harvest and may be marketed from 31 March. These values belong to the current specification and are no tasting rank.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux"}
## Classification and producers — reputation without official rank

Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux has no current official château classification. Size, architecture or “grand vin” on a label supplies no legal rank. Family estates, larger businesses and cooperative structures coexist.

For quality, examine producer, cuvée, year and transparency about origin and maturation. A future producer overview can show representative styles without turning every château into a full independent page.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Wine style and bottle development — generous red and bright white

Red can combine plum, blackberry, violets and spice with round to firm tannin. Many examples are approachable young; more serious selection can move toward cedar, tobacco and earth in bottle.

White is dry and often citrus-led and floral, sometimes with yellow fruit and greater texture. Sauvignon-led wine generally drinks fresh when young, while Sémillon, lees contact or subtle oak can carry some development.
:::

:::detail{#serveren-en-bewaren parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux"}
### Serving and keeping

Red can be served around 16–18 °C with poultry, lamb, mushrooms or beans; white around 8–11 °C with fish, goat's cheese and green herbs. Let white warm slightly in the glass for texture.

Ageing ambition differs by cuvée. Concentration, acidity, tannin maturity and producer advice are better guides than appellation alone.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — four words form one origin

Read “Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux” as the complete appellation presentation. Then check red or white, producer, cuvée, vintage and bottler. “Blaye” alone can occur in historical or other legal contexts and needs attention.

Do not confuse the bottle with Côtes de Bourg despite their proximity. “Côtes de Bordeaux” without Blaye is also broader and intended for still red; the supplementary name supplies more precise origin.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Contemporary developments — individuality within a shared name

The collective Côtes de Bordeaux name aids recognition, but Blaye must keep its own landscape and style visible. Dry white, wine tourism and a clear producer story can give it more expression alongside red.

Heat can accelerate ripening; drought and downpours put soils and slopes under pressure. Ground cover, erosion control, pruning and picking time therefore demand ever greater precision.
:::
