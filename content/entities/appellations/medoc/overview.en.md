The Médoc AOP covers the northern part of the peninsula bearing the same name, where the Gironde flows past vineyards, small ports and low rises of gravel and clay. For centuries, the name on a bottle referred almost exclusively to red wine: usually an [[concept.assemblage|assemblage]] in which [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] can bring supple fruit and roundness while [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] contributes structure and freshness. Since a change approved in 2025, dry white wine may carry the appellation too. [@source.legifrance-medoc-order-2025]

That does not suddenly make Médoc a different place. It reveals something long obscured by the fame of red: white grapes also have a history here. To understand the appellation, look beyond the idea of “affordable red Bordeaux.” Médoc is extensive and varied, and soil, water, varieties and producer together decide whether a wine is light-footed and immediate or firm and patient.

## Location and boundaries — the northern appellation

The [[region.medoc|Médoc region]] is the long tongue of land between the Atlantic Ocean and Gironde estuary, northwest of Bordeaux. The Médoc AOP occupies its northern half, broadly beyond the southern boundary of [[appellation.haut-medoc|Haut-Médoc]] and continuing towards the estuary mouth. Villages such as Lesparre-Médoc, Bégadan, Saint-Christoly-Médoc and Saint-Yzans-de-Médoc sit in this landscape of vines, pasture, drainage channels and woodland.

The name on a bottle is more precise than the regional name in a travel guide. Only grapes from officially delimited parcels, handled under the current specification, can become Médoc AOP. Not every field within an eligible commune is appellation vineyard land.
:::

:::detail{#een-naam-twee-schalen parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### One name identifies both a region and an appellation

In everyday language, “the Médoc” can mean the whole peninsula and its eight appellations. On a label, “Médoc” identifies one particular AOP. Pauillac, Margaux and Saint-Estèphe therefore lie in the Médoc region, but their wines are not automatically Médoc AOP; they normally carry their own communal appellations.

Nor is Haut-Médoc a superior step to which a Médoc wine can be promoted. It is a neighbouring AOP with separate boundaries. That spatial logic matters more than the tempting assumption that *haut* simply means “higher quality.”
:::

## History — wine country shaped by water and trade

Romans grew vines in the wider Médoc, but today's landscape did not descend from antiquity in a straight line. Large areas were wet, difficult to reach or used for mixed farming. From the early modern period, embankments, ditches and drainage changed what the land could support. Small Gironde ports then offered a route to Bordeaux and onwards to northern Europe.

Those trading links made viticulture attractive, but did not distribute wealth evenly. Famous estates emerged in the south on the most accessible and highly valued gravel terraces. Further north, holdings remained smaller and more mixed. The Médoc AOC, recognised on 14 November 1936, gave this northern origin a protected collective name. [@source.inao-medoc-specification-2025]
:::

:::detail{#water-handel-en-wijngaarden parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.medoc-marsh-drainage-1957 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
### Vineyards follow more than a natural map

Drainage made wet ground usable, but not every drained plot became a vineyard. Ownership, capital, access to a port and demand from merchants helped determine where vines paid. The familiar pattern of château, vineyard and estuary was shaped as much by economics as by geology.

In the nineteenth century, vine diseases, powdery and downy mildew and finally phylloxera caused severe damage. Replanting on American rootstocks changed the plant material; cooperation, mechanisation and larger operating scales later changed the work. Behind the tranquil façades lies a history of repeated reconstruction.
:::

:::detail{#van-rode-reputatie-naar-witte-erkenning parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025 source.legifrance-medoc-order-2025 source.eurlex-medoc-modification-2026"}
### White Médoc is new in law, not new to the landscape

Records cited by the official specification document white production in Médoc from at least the early eighteenth century. As red wine came to dominate its international reputation, white production contracted and was left outside the Médoc AOC created in 1936. Around the turn of the millennium, more estates again made local white cuvées, but these had to use another geographical designation.

The specification approved in 2025 added still white wine to the AOP; the amendment was published at European level in 2026. This did not invent a tradition from nothing. It gave a legal framework to a surviving but long-secondary practice, including specific provisions for varieties, yields, vinification and ageing. [@source.legifrance-medoc-order-2025] [@source.eurlex-medoc-modification-2026]
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025 source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — low rises between two waters

The AOP occupies low, gently rolling land between the Gironde to the east and pine forest towards the Atlantic to the west. The estuary moderates temperature swings; the ocean brings a mild but humid climate. Wind and sun can dry vines after rain, while a wet spring or autumn encourages disease and dilution.

Gravel is famous in Médoc, but it does not form a universal blanket in the north. Gravel rises alternate with clay, calcareous subsoil, sand and younger deposits. The useful question is therefore not “which soil tastes best?” but how a parcel retains warmth, drains or stores water and regulates vine growth.
:::

:::detail{#meer-dan-grind parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025 source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022"}
### Gravel and clay create different possibilities

Coarse gravel can warm quickly and let excess water drain. On suitable sites, that helps the later-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon. Clay stores more water and stays cooler; Merlot can ripen reliably there, especially when dry summers draw on the soil's reserves. Limestone, sand and mixed soils make the real pattern finer still.

These are growing conditions, not fixed aroma recipes. A clay-rich parcel does not automatically produce “soft plum wine,” nor does gravel put blackcurrant scent into a grape. Pruning, yield, ripeness, harvest date, fermentation and ageing all remain part of the eventual glass.
:::

:::detail{#jalles-en-waterbeheer parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025 source.medoc-marsh-drainage-1957"}
### Drainage decides what the land can carry

Narrow streams — often called *jalles* locally — carry water through flat land towards the estuary. Ditches, channels and pumps maintain that drainage. Over a distance of only a few metres, a slight change in elevation can separate rootable soil from ground that remains wet for too long.

Historical drainage is not a finished chapter. Heavier downpours require outlets, while longer dry periods make water storage and organic matter increasingly important. Modern soil management therefore seeks not maximum dryness but a resilient relationship between infiltration, storage and drainage.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Grape varieties — a red and a white palette

For red wine, the traditional palette comprises [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]], [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]], [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]]. Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot usually carry most weight in practice. The former can bring structure, blackcurrant and freshness; the latter generally offers earlier-ripening fruit and a rounder texture.

For white, the base varieties are [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]], [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.sauvignon-gris|Sauvignon Gris]] and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]]. Sauvignon can give aromatic tension and acidity, Sémillon breadth and waxy texture, and Muscadelle a floral accent. None of these roles is a guaranteed tasting note or compulsory blend percentage.
:::

:::detail{#twee-paletten parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Blending distributes both risk and character

Varieties do not ripen together and react differently to soil and season. Merlot may be harvested before autumn rain, while Cabernet Sauvignon needs longer for skins and seeds to ripen. In a favourable year, Petit Verdot can add colour, tannin and spice, but it is not a compulsory signature.

In white wine, a fresher Sauvignon component may lift a broader Sémillon lot. [[concept.assemblage|Assemblage]] — composing a wine from different lots — is therefore not an attempt to manufacture exactly the same wine every year. It brings variation between variety, parcel, picking date and vessel into a coherent style.
:::

:::detail{#aanpassingsrassen parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### New varieties receive a bounded trial space

Under a formal agreement, the current specification also allows varieties intended for climate adaptation. For red they are [[grape.arinarnoa|Arinarnoa]], [[grape.castets|Castets]], [[grape.marselan|Marselan]] and [[grape.touriga-nacional|Touriga Nacional]]; for white, [[grape.alvarinho|Alvarinho]], [[grape.floreal|Floréal]], [[grape.liliorila|Liliorila]], [[grape.sauvignac|Sauvignac]] and [[grape.souvignier-gris|Souvignier Gris]]. They offer options around heat, drought or disease sensitivity without immediately replacing the traditional palette.

The allowance is deliberately small: together, these varieties may occupy no more than five per cent of an estate's planted area and ten per cent of a blend. Permission does not mean every estate plants them or that their names will appear on every bottle. [@source.inao-medoc-specification-2025]
:::

## Viticulture and winemaking — steering ripeness, not copying a style

In the vineyard, much depends on an open, healthy canopy and a yield the vines can ripen. Bunches need to dry after rain, yet leaves also protect against sunburn. Harvest decisions balance sugar, acid, aroma, skin and seed maturity against weather risk.

Red grapes ferment with their skins to extract colour and [[concept.tannin|tannin]]; white grapes are generally pressed and fermented without skins. No automatic château formula follows. Steel, concrete, oak, fermentation temperature, oxygen and the length of [[concept.elevage|ageing]] leave producers considerable room.
:::

:::detail{#rode-wijn-bouwen parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Extraction must suit the fruit

During red fermentation, a winemaker may pump juice over or submerge the floating cap to take up colour, aroma and tannin. Too little extraction can leave a thin wine; force applied to unripe skins can create hardness. After alcoholic fermentation, [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|malolactic conversion]] softens sharper malic acid.

Oak ageing can dose oxygen gradually and add spicy or toasted tones. New oak is not synonymous with quality: vessel size, age and duration must suit the wine's concentration. Assemblage eventually connects parcels, varieties and barrels.
:::

:::detail{#witte-wijn-bewaren parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Freshness begins before fermentation

For white wine, sound fruit, controlled pressing and protection from unwanted oxidation are crucial. Cooling and temperature control help preserve volatile aromas. The specification also requires at least thirty per cent of a white lot to have spent three months in contact with wood; time fermenting in wood may count towards that period. [@source.inao-medoc-specification-2025]

This does not automatically make the wine heavy or vanilla-scented. Large, used or subtly employed vessels can build texture without masking fruit. The challenge is to gain breadth while keeping Sauvignon freshness and natural [[concept.acidity|acidity]] visible.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Appellation rules — a baseline, not a château recipe

The specification determines where grapes may originate, which varieties are permitted and what minimum provisions govern planting, yield, ripeness, vinification and ageing. It protects “Médoc” as a collective origin. It does not require every red to contain the same share of Cabernet or every white to show the same oak profile.

Red and white share a place name but follow partly different technical routes. Both must be aged at least until 31 March after harvest and may be released to consumers from 15 April. Such dates secure minimum development time; ambitious producers can wait longer. [@source.inao-medoc-specification-2025]
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### The current figures behind the name

Minimum planting density is 5,000 vines per hectare, with no more than two metres between rows. The base yield is 55 hectolitres per hectare and the annual ceiling 65; grapes must reach at least 11 per cent potential natural alcohol. Red wine must complete malolactic conversion and meet additional analytical limits.

White Médoc must be bottled in glass between 1 April and 31 December following harvest. These figures are legal thresholds, not a quality ranking. Lower yield or longer barrel ageing can be deliberate, but neither proves by itself that a wine tastes better.
:::

:::detail{#milieuregels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Soil care is part of the specification

A physical and chemical soil analysis is required before new planting. Total chemical weed control of a parcel and chemical control on headlands are prohibited. The specification also sets provisions for cover, erosion management and adaptation varieties.

These rules do not make a bottle automatically organic or regenerative. They establish a collective baseline above which estates can choose different certifications and practices. An official certification and a broad sustainability claim remain separate information for the reader.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.crus-bourgeois-medoc-classification-2025"}
## Classification and producers — beyond the shadow of 1855

The celebrated [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]] dominates the image of the Médoc region, but its red classed growths lie in the more southerly Haut-Médoc appellations. For Médoc AOP, [[classification.crus-bourgeois-du-medoc|Crus Bourgeois du Médoc]] and [[classification.crus-artisans-du-medoc|Crus Artisans du Médoc]] offer more relevant routes through its estates.

These systems classify or recognise producers according to their own criteria; they do not change a wine's appellation. A Cru Bourgeois from Médoc remains Médoc AOP, and an unclassified château can still make serious wine.
:::

:::detail{#crus-bourgeois-2025 parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.crus-bourgeois-medoc-classification-2025"}
### A broad group with three levels

The classification announced in 2025 and applying to vintages 2023–2027 includes 170 châteaux across the Médoc appellations. Ninety-one are in Médoc AOP. Three local estates — Château La Cardonne, Château Castera and Château Laujac — carry the highest *Cru Bourgeois Exceptionnel* designation in this edition. [@source.crus-bourgeois-medoc-classification-2025]

Other local listings fall under *Cru Bourgeois Supérieur* and *Cru Bourgeois*. These numbers provide scale and navigation, not an eternal league table. A new edition can change which estates participate or which level they hold.
:::

:::detail{#geen-rang-is-geen-oordeel parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.crus-bourgeois-medoc-classification-2025"}
### Classification, brand and performance are not the same

A classification assesses within a defined process and period; it is neither appellation control nor a blind tasting of every bottle. Ownership, vineyard, selection and market position can change. Read a rank alongside producer, exact [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]], [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and storage condition.

Producer coverage should keep the same proportion. A limited number of guiding estates merit compact profiles; a complete series of separate château pages would overwhelm the appellation. The classification page can later become a durable, filterable collection.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Wine style and bottle development — from dark fruit to fresh white tension

Red Médoc can combine blackcurrant, plum, cedar, dried herbs and earthy notes with noticeable tannin. Merlot-rich or early-drinking examples can be juicy and round; Cabernet-led, rigorously selected wines may need more time. The appellation therefore promises no single weight.

White Médoc is normally dry. Sauvignon can bring citrus, gooseberry or a grassy, herbal accent; Sémillon can add yellow fruit, flowers and texture. Oak may make it rounder, but freshness should preserve its line. Because the AOP category is young, a narrow “typical” style still has to emerge in practice.
:::

:::detail{#rood-in-ontwikkeling parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview"}
### Time can soften, but cannot repair

In a well-concentrated red, primary fruit can move towards cedar, tobacco, leather, dried leaf and earth. Tannin may feel finer as molecules rearrange and precipitate. This requires enough fruit, acidity and balance at the outset.

Simpler or riper reds may be most attractive when young. Cork failure, heat or oxygen damage can accelerate development into fatigue. “Médoc can age” is therefore a possibility for an individual wine, not an instruction to cellar every bottle.
:::

:::detail{#wit-in-ontwikkeling parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### White development depends strongly on composition

A Sauvignon-led wine may initially centre on citrus, herbs and tension. More Sémillon, lees contact or subtle oak ageing can provide texture and room for honeyed, waxy or nutty development. The official style description also mentions tropical fruit, acacia and apricot as possible accents.

That is a collective profile, not a tasting guarantee. The first AOP vintages also still have to show how different cuvées develop over longer periods. Producer, closure and storage are firmer guides than one categorical lifespan.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.eurlex-medoc-modification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — read the precise origin first

Begin with “Médoc” or “Haut-Médoc,” then colour, producer, cuvée, vintage and bottler. A château image, “[[concept.grand-vin|grand vin]]” or heavy bottle is not a classification. If a Cru Bourgeois designation appears, check which edition and exact wine it belongs to.

Under the current rules, the larger geographical unit may appear as “Vin de Bordeaux — Médoc” or “Grand Vin de Bordeaux — Médoc.” Bordeaux supplies context, but Médoc remains the protected appellation name. [@source.eurlex-medoc-modification-2026]
:::

:::detail{#medoc-is-niet-haut-medoc parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Two neighbouring names, not a simple ladder

Do not buy Médoc AOP as a cheap imitation of Haut-Médoc, nor assume Haut-Médoc is always better. The names identify different delimited areas. Style and quality in both depend on parcel, selection, vintage and producer.

For older red wine, fill level, leakage, colour and storage history matter greatly. With the new white category, the exact cuvée is especially useful: some estates gained experience under a broader Bordeaux designation, while others are only starting with the new AOP.
:::

:::detail{#serveren-en-bewaren parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview"}
### Temperature gives the wine room

Serve red at roughly 16–18 °C as a starting point; very young, fruity red can be slightly cooler. Air can disperse reduction or open firm tannins, but prolonged decanting may exhaust an old wine. White Médoc can begin around 9–12 °C and warm in the glass.

Store bottles in stable cool, dark, vibration-free conditions. Once open, a white may remain useful for a day or several days under refrigeration; red may respond similarly depending on age and structure. Tasting is more reliable than a universal clock.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Contemporary developments — broadening without losing identity

Warmer seasons can help ripening, but heat, drought and heavy rainfall bring new risks. Early sugar accumulation can run ahead of aroma and tannin maturity; drought stress can halt growth; humidity raises disease pressure. Soil life, canopy, rootstock, grape variety and picking date are increasingly considered together.

The appellation has no single miracle answer. It combines closer attention to soil care, limited space for adaptation varieties and a white category that gives producers more options. The outcome will differ by estate and season.
:::

:::detail{#wit-als-aanpassing-en-ambitie parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-specification-2025 source.legifrance-medoc-order-2025"}
### White wine is more than a climate measure

White wine can broaden a range and give grapes on suitable cooler or clay-limestone sites their own destination. Earlier picking may also distribute risk differently from late-ripening red. Yet it is too simple to describe white Médoc only as a response to warming: historical production, local expertise and commercial curiosity also played a part.

The coming years will therefore be a period of style formation. The best test is not whether white “tastes like red Médoc,” but whether producers connect origin, freshness, texture and oak in a convincing new local language.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Médoc is both old wine country and an appellation in motion. Understand the precise place and name first, then read soil, grapes, producer and vintage — and remember that since 2025 both red and dry white can belong to this protected origin.
:::
