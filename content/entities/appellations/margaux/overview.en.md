:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-overview source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
Margaux is the southernmost of the Médoc's celebrated communal appellations. Low gravel rises sit between woodland, drainage channels and the Gironde across four municipalities. Cabernet Sauvignon commonly takes the lead, supported by Merlot and other red [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] varieties. Together they produce red wine only: often aromatic and precise, sometimes seductive when young, yet in the finest cases built for long development in bottle.

The name carries exceptional prestige through [[producer.chateau-margaux|Château Margaux]] and twenty other classed growths from 1855. The appellation is nevertheless much more than its most famous labels. To understand Margaux is to see a landscape shaped by water management, differences between gravel terraces and lower ground, and the choices through which each producer must find ripeness, structure and finesse anew.
:::

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-overview source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
## Location and boundaries — the gateway to communal Médoc

Travelling from Bordeaux, Margaux is the first of the four great communal appellations along the Gironde. It lies within the [[region.medoc|Médoc region]], north of the city and on the eastern side of the peninsula. Its vineyards are divided among Arsac, Labarde, Soussans and Margaux-Cantenac. The last municipality was formed in 2017 by merging Margaux and Cantenac, so older books may suggest five place names instead.

The vineyards meet [[appellation.haut-medoc|Haut-Médoc]] to the south and west. Their transition is not always visible in the landscape. A drainage channel, road or cadastral line may matter more in law than any difference seen from a bicycle. Margaux is therefore not a vaguely edged flavour zone but a protected origin with a precise parcel map.
:::

:::detail{#vier-gemeenten-en-een-perceelgrens parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### Four municipalities do not mean four uniform terroirs

The official geographical area names the four municipalities in which grapes may be grown and wine made. Within them, only separately approved parcels qualify for the appellation. A vineyard inside the municipal boundary is not automatically Margaux, and an estate may own parcels with different legal origins.

That precision prevents another confusion: the former village of Margaux, today's municipality of Margaux-Cantenac, the Margaux appellation and Château Margaux are four different things. On a label, “Margaux” without *Château* before it is first of all the appellation. The producer is identified elsewhere.
:::

## History — from wet ground to a name in trade

Margaux owes its shape not only to ancient river deposits but to people who drained water, consolidated properties and sold wine to distant markets. From the sixteenth century, *bourdieux* appeared: agricultural holdings combining vines, a house and farm buildings. In the seventeenth century Dutch hydraulic engineers helped reclaim marshy parts of Médoc. Ditches and channels made land more workable and accessible gravel rises more attractive for vines.

The port of Bordeaux connected these estates with England, the Low Countries and, later, markets farther afield. By the middle of the eighteenth century the modern vineyard landscape had broadly taken shape. Its rising reputation reflected not just a new kind of wine but recognisable estate names, selection and a trade increasingly attentive to precise origin.
:::

:::detail{#van-bourdieu-naar-chateau parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
### The château became an economic and cultural centre

Where land, capital and access to trade converged, bourdieux developed into larger wine estates. The word *château* did not refer only to an imposing building. It also became the name of a property, a wine and eventually a brand. That model helped buyers distinguish wines from the same village that did not share the same price, reputation or ambition.

The nineteenth century brought prestige and disruption alike. The 1855 trade classification confirmed Margaux as a core left-bank district. Oidium, downy mildew and [[concept.phylloxera|phylloxera]] subsequently forced growers to treat, replant and adopt new [[concept.rootstock|rootstocks]]. What looks timeless has in fact been rebuilt technically and economically more than once.
:::

:::detail{#grens-en-aoc parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### A familiar name acquired a legal boundary in stages

“Margaux” was used as a wine origin in the eighteenth century, well before the modern appellation system. As reputation also attracted misuse, the name needed legal definition. A Bordeaux civil court ruling set an initial contour on 3 December 1923. The appellation d'origine contrôlée was subsequently recognised on 10 August 1954.

Today's boundary is not an untouched line inherited from 1954. Parcel delimitation and production rules were later examined and revised; the current specification refers, among others, to decisions in 2007 and 2016. An AOC is thus not a historical museum piece but a collective system seeking to combine continuity with accountable updating.

## Landscape, climate and soils — gravel rises in a land of water

Margaux appears almost flat, yet small changes in height are decisive. Old Garonne and Gironde terraces form long, rounded rises—locally called *croupes*—of gravel, pebbles and sand. Rain can drain more readily and the stony surface warms quickly. These are favourable conditions for late-ripening [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]].

Streams and man-made ditches carry water between the rises. Lower zones contain more clay, sand or younger river deposits; beneath the gravel, clay and limestone can alter water availability. The Gironde and Atlantic moderate frost and heat but also bring humidity. Rain around flowering or harvest can leave a strong mark on a [[concept.vintage|vintage]].
:::

:::detail{#terrassen-en-water parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### Drainage operates above and below ground

The officially described terraces stand roughly six to thirty-three metres above sea level. That seems modest, but a gentle rise may keep roots above persistently wet ground. Coarse gravel lets water pass; sand and clay beneath determine how much remains available during a dry summer. Ditches and small streams carry excess water towards the estuary.

“Well drained” therefore does not mean “as dry as possible.” A vine needs water to support leaves and fruit. The most useful sites combine release after rain with sufficient reserves for warmer weeks. Cultivation, plant cover between rows and organic matter also influence that balance.
:::

:::detail{#geen-enkele-margauxbodem parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023 source.margaux-winegrowers-overview"}
### There is no single recipe called “Margaux soil”

The famous deep gravels matter, but they are not equally deep, coarse or old everywhere. The appellation includes several terrace levels, sandier western zones and lower ground where clay or recent deposits gain weight. Even within one estate, water supply and ripening can differ from parcel to parcel.

This variation explains why variety and harvest date are parcel decisions. It does not justify simplistic flavour determinism: gravel does not automatically make violet aromas, nor clay soft wine. [[concept.terroir|Terroir]] describes the interaction between place, plant, season and people—not an aroma locked inside a stone.
:::

## Grape varieties — Cabernet as frame, assemblage as language

Cabernet Sauvignon is the recognisable foundation of many Margaux wines. On warm, free-draining gravel rises it can combine blackcurrant, floral and savoury nuances with fresh acidity and fine but evident [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] generally ripens earlier and may add plum-like fruit, roundness and volume. [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] often play smaller but meaningful roles.

The official palette also includes [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]]. Not every château plants every variety, and no fixed proportion defines the appellation. [[concept.assemblage|Assemblage]]—composing wine from separate lots—allows producers to respond to soil, ripeness and year.
:::

:::detail{#druif-per-plek parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023 source.margaux-winegrowers-overview"}
### Variety follows parcel and season

Cabernet Sauvignon needs a long growing season. On cooler or wetter soils it may struggle to ripen fully; on warm gravel rises it often retains tension while skins and seeds mature. Merlot suits many clay- or sand-bearing sites and can be picked before late autumn weather. Cabernet Franc may bring fragrance and line; Petit Verdot colour, tannin and spice when the season is long enough.

These roles are not guaranteed tasting notes. A warm year, old vines, yield, picking date and cellar selection shift the result. Blending spreads risk, but it is also a stylistic choice: not every producer seeks the same balance between perfume, power and early accessibility.
:::

:::detail{#castets-als-aanpassingsras parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### Castets receives a small, controlled trial space

Alongside the traditional grapes, the current specification names [[grape.castets|Castets]] as an adaptation variety. Its use requires a formal agreement and is deliberately limited: such vines may occupy at most five per cent of an estate's area and contribute no more than ten per cent of the final blend.

Permission does not mean Castets is already a common part of Margaux. It is a legal tool for gaining experience under pressures including climate change without abruptly replacing the historic palette. Its real value must emerge from long observation in vineyard and wine.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — finesse begins with ripeness

In a humid maritime climate, growers need enough leaf for photosynthesis and protection while air around bunches helps reduce fungal pressure. Pruning, training, soil management and yield steer ripening. Harvest decisions consider not just sugar and acidity but aroma, skin colour, seed maturity and the weather forecast.

Red grapes undergo [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]] with their skins, releasing colour, aroma compounds and tannin. Afterwards, [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|malolactic conversion]] softens sharper malic acid. Lots from different varieties and parcels are tasted, selected, assembled and given further [[concept.elevage|ageing]].
:::

:::detail{#selectie-extractie-en-opvoeding parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### Softness is not created by omitting structure

During fermentation, the intensity of [[concept.extraction|extraction]] determines how much material is taken from skins and seeds. Ripe, concentrated fruit can sustain a different approach from a lighter lot. Force applied to unripe skins does not make classical finesse but dryness; too little extraction may leave a wine without a middle.

Barrel ageing is common, but new oak is neither an appellation rule nor proof of quality. Oak species, toast, vessel age, oxygen and time must suit the fruit. Selection for the [[concept.grand-vin|grand vin]] can be strict; other suitable lots may enter a [[concept.second-wine|second wine]] or another [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]]. House style is built upon differences of season and parcel.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-overview source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
## Appellation rules — a shared baseline, not a style script

Margaux protects still red wine only. Its specification defines geographical origin, permitted grapes, vineyard layout, ripeness, yield, analysis and minimum ageing. These rules make the name accountable; they do not prescribe a blend percentage, château character or minimum share of new oak.

Two legally compliant Margaux wines may therefore differ greatly in concentration, price and lifespan. The appellation forms the base of the pyramid. Parcel quality, vineyard work, selection, cellar decisions and producer ambition build above it.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### The figures behind the origin

New plantings require at least 7,000 vines per hectare. Base yield is 57 hectolitres per hectare; the general ceiling is 63. At harvest, Merlot must reach at least 189 grams of sugar per litre of must and the other traditional grapes 180 grams. Minimum natural alcoholic strength is 11 per cent.

After malolactic conversion, no more than 0.30 grams of malic acid per litre may remain, and analytical limits apply to fermentable sugar among other measures. The wine is aged at least until 1 June after harvest and may be released to consumers from 1 September. These are current legal thresholds, not a recipe for greatness or a substitute for the full specification.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
## Classification and producers — an appellation across all five ranks

Margaux contains 21 red classed growths in the [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]], more than any other Médoc communal appellation. They range from first to fifth growth. [[producer.chateau-margaux|Château Margaux]] stands as the local premier cru, followed by a remarkably broad group of historic estates.

This wealth makes 1855 integral to Margaux's identity, but the classification is not a map of all quality. It ranks named properties in the context of nineteenth-century trade. Unclassified estates work under the same appellation rules and can make excellent wine; present performance also varies within every historic rank.
:::

:::detail{#eenentwintig-crus-vijf-rangen parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### The breadth matters more than a long list of names

The 21 growths occupy all five red ranks: one first growth, five seconds, ten thirds, three fourths and two fifths. Margaux is consequently both a place and a cross-section through the historic Médoc hierarchy. A shared rank does not, however, guarantee a common style, scale or market price.

A complete producer list belongs in the classification or appellation index rather than this narrative. The lesson here is relational: rank belongs to a château, appellation to the wine's origin and vintage to a single growing year. All three layers can appear on the same label, but they do not mean the same thing.
:::

:::detail{#chateau-margaux-in-context parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.chateau-margaux-history"}
### Château Margaux is a landmark, not the definition

The estate carrying the Château Margaux name is a first growth whose wine acquired an international reputation early. Its present neoclassical buildings date from the early nineteenth century; the property itself has a much longer history. That development deserves a separate producer monograph.

The distinction matters more on this page: Château Margaux lies in Margaux, but Margaux is not the estate. Reading every other château only as an approximation of the premier cru obscures differences between municipalities, terraces, business styles and price levels that make the appellation instructive.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-overview source.inao-margaux-specification-2023 source.margaux-winegrowers-overview"}
## Wine style and bottle development — perfume with a backbone

Margaux is often associated with finesse, fragrance and a silky texture. In the glass this may mean blackcurrant, plum, violets, cedar, graphite, tobacco and spice, carried by fresh acidity and tannin that can be evident yet fine-grained. Good examples combine movement and length with enough structure to avoid becoming slight.

This profile is a range, not a guarantee. A Cabernet-led wine from deep gravel may begin stern and closed; more Merlot, a warm year or an accessible selection may offer earlier roundness. Producer, parcel, vintage and age predict more than the word “elegant” alone.
:::

:::detail{#finesse-is-geen-recept parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023 source.margaux-winegrowers-overview"}
### Bottle age shifts the balance

In young wine, fruit, flowers, oak and tannin may still stand apart. With time, primary aromas can move towards cedar, tobacco, dried flowers, leather and forest floor as tannins combine and feel softer. Ageing works only when fruit, acidity and structure were balanced to begin with. Time cannot repair a thin or dried-out wine.

A straightforward Margaux may be most attractive on its fruit, while a concentrated cru from a strong year can develop for decades. Decanting may open a young bottle but exhaust an old one too quickly. Tasting, condition and producer information matter more than a universal drinking window.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.inao-margaux-overview"}
## Labels and buying — read the origin before the hierarchy

On the label, find “Margaux” first as the protected origin, then producer, exact wine, [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and bottler. “Grand vin” may identify an estate's principal wine but is not an official quality rank. “Grand cru classé en 1855” does refer to a historic classification when used for the relevant château and wine.

Price follows reputation, scarcity, vintage and market so strongly that it cannot summarise the appellation cleanly. For older bottles, fill level, seepage, provenance and storage are essential. For young wine, grape composition, selection and ageing often provide more guidance than the façade pictured on the label.
:::

:::detail{#margaux-is-niet-altijd-chateau-margaux parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
### One word can be a place name and part of a brand

On a bottle of Château Margaux, “Margaux” appears twice with different functions: once in the producer's name and once as the appellation. On another estate's bottle, only the latter meaning applies. Read the full name and the line *Appellation Margaux Protégée* or *Contrôlée*.

A serving temperature around 16–18 °C is a useful starting point; very young wine may be slightly cooler. A firm young example may benefit from air. Old Margaux should be poured gently because of sediment and fragile aromas. Store bottles in cool, dark and stable conditions—a famous name offers no protection against poor storage.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
## Contemporary developments — earlier harvests, a longer task

Warmer seasons can help Cabernet ripen more reliably, but also bring drought stress, faster sugar accumulation, lower acidity and sunburn risk. Margaux remains maritime at the same time: humidity, mildew and rain near harvest do not disappear. The challenge is not simply “more heat,” but wider swings and new combinations of risk.

Producers respond with plant cover, organic matter, adjusted pruning and canopy management, more precise harvest timing and research into plant material. A tightly limited adaptation variety is one route among many. No measure guarantees the preservation of style; together they form a process of learning by parcel and season.
:::

:::detail{#aanpassen-zonder-stijlgarantie parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### Retaining water becomes as important as removing it

The historic success of gravel rises depended partly on drainage in a humid climate. In hotter, drier summers, retaining available water can become equally important. Plant cover, soil compaction, rooting depth and organic matter influence how much rain infiltrates and how long that reserve lasts.

Adaptation therefore requires precision. A cooler clay parcel and a dry gravel crest do not need the same intervention; later pruning may shift the growth cycle but also affects labour and disease management. The appellation protects a collective identity while effective answers are tested at a smaller scale.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
Margaux is not a synonym for one château or one silky taste. It is a precisely delimited, historically constructed landscape across four municipalities, where gravel, water, Cabernet and producer decisions create a broad family of red wines.
:::
