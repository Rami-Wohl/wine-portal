:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-bordeaux-aop"}
Bordeaux AOP is the broad, versatile designation shared across the Bordeaux wine region. The same name can appear on red, rosé and white wine: from deeply coloured red blends to pale dry whites, and from dark-pink clairet to white wine with some residual sugar. Bordeaux is therefore not one flavour, but a shared origin and a common set of rules.

The appellation extends across much of the Gironde department, but not every vineyard—and certainly not every piece of land—automatically qualifies. Only delimited parcels, permitted grapes and compliant production methods can carry the name. Understanding this broad AOP also makes it easier to see what Bordeaux's smaller appellations communicate more precisely.

## Location and boundaries — the great shared origin

Bordeaux AOP extends across nearly the full wine landscape of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]], from the estuary and Atlantic side to the Dordogne and Garonne valleys. It overlaps geographically with many more specific appellations. A parcel may lie inside the zone of a communal or regional designation while its wine is released as Bordeaux for commercial, technical or stylistic reasons.

That breadth makes Bordeaux the region's connecting designation. It is not simply the bottom rung of a fixed quality ladder. A narrower appellation supplies more precise geographical information and its own rules, but a carefully made Bordeaux may be more rewarding than an unremarkable bottle bearing a more famous place-name.
:::

:::detail{#breed-betekent-niet-grenzeloos parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### A large area remains an exact legal origin

The specification names a long list of communes in which grapes may be grown and wine made. Within those communes, only officially delimited vineyard parcels qualify for the AOP. A commune boundary, production zone and parcel boundary are therefore three different things.

The name “Bordeaux” also operates at several scales. It can mean the city, the entire wine region or this particular appellation. On a label, *Bordeaux* in the designation line means the AOP; in a book, the same word may describe the far larger world of appellations, trade and landscape.

## History — a name shaped by port and trade

Bordeaux became famous because rivers, quays and merchants connected a vast hinterland with the wider world. After Eleanor of Aquitaine married Henry Plantagenet in 1152, England grew into a crucial market. The red wines of that period were often lighter in colour and structure than the image now associated with classic Bordeaux. The English name *claret* became established for them.

The port never sold wine from just one village or one soil. It brought diverse origins together under a name buyers recognised. “Bordeaux” consequently acquired two meanings early on: geographical origin and commercial identity. That tension—between broad reach and precise provenance—still runs through the large appellation.
:::

:::detail{#van-claret-naar-meer-kleur parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.inao-bordeaux-clairet-product-page source.civb-bordeaux-aop"}
### The historic colour did not stand still

Medieval and early-modern claret was generally pale, made quickly and intended for relatively early drinking. From the eighteenth and especially the nineteenth century, longer-macerated, darker and more ageworthy reds gained prestige. New cellar practices, estate identities, glass bottles and international demand changed what the market understood as great Bordeaux.

The lighter tradition did not disappear. The current specification uses [[concept.claret|claret]] for light-red Bordeaux and [[concept.clairet|clairet]] for dark-coloured rosé. Regular rosé, dry white and white with residual sugar also exist. The broad AOP therefore preserves several historical lines rather than one frozen style.
:::

:::detail{#bescherming-van-de-naam parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-aoc-history"}
### From trading name to controlled origin

Bordeaux's reputation made the name valuable and therefore vulnerable to misuse. During the early twentieth century, French origin protection moved from reputation alone towards verifiable combinations of place, grape and practice. Bordeaux AOC was recognised on 14 November 1936.

The current specification is not an unchanged document from 1936. Grape lists, environmental provisions, analyses and production methods are amended through formal procedures. Continuity here means that the name remains collectively governed, not that viticulture and rules never change.
:::

## Landscape, climate and soils — a river world, not one terroir

Bordeaux lies where Atlantic influence meets a major river system. The Gironde, Garonne and Dordogne moderate temperatures and have shaped terraces, banks and slopes. Forests shelter parts of the region from direct ocean winds; rain and humidity also keep fungal disease continually relevant. One season is a race to ripen before autumn rain, another a struggle to retain freshness and water through heat.

The ground ranges from gravel and sand to clay, limestone and mixtures of them. Famous gravel terraces sit west of the Gironde and Garonne; limestone and clay often have a larger role around [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]], [[appellation.fronsac|Fronsac]] and [[region.entre-deux-mers|Entre-deux-Mers]]. Each zone contains further differences in slope, drainage and soil depth.
:::

:::detail{#geen-enkel-bordeauxterroir parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022 source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### The appellation name does not predict the soil beneath the vine

A Bordeaux may come from a warm gravel rise, a cool clay slope, sandy ground or a limestone plateau. The broad name does not disclose that site. Soil influences water, rooting and the pace of ripening, but does not independently determine an aroma or level of quality. Variety, [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]], yield, harvest timing and cellar choices all contribute.

At this scale, [[concept.terroir|terroir]] is therefore an invitation to look further. Producer, commune, parcel information and [[concept.vintage|vintage]] make the origin more concrete. The AOP guarantees the broad zone and production rules, not one geological or sensory profile.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties"}
## Grape varieties — two palettes for blending

For reds, [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] form the best-known foundation. Merlot can bring suppleness and plum-like fruit; Cabernet Sauvignon structure and blackcurrant character; Cabernet Franc perfume and freshness. [[grape.malbec|Malbec]], [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]] complete the historic palette.

Among whites, [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]], [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.sauvignon-gris|Sauvignon Gris]] and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]] take centre stage. Sauvignon often supplies tension and overt aroma, Sémillon breadth and texture, and Muscadelle a floral note. Small proportions of other authorised white varieties are also permitted.
:::

:::detail{#twee-paletten-veel-assemblages parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties"}
### Blending is not a compulsory recipe

[[concept.assemblage|Assemblage]] means bringing separate lots together. Those lots may represent varieties, parcels or barrels. Blending is characteristic of Bordeaux, but no rule says that every wine must contain all the classic grapes or repeat the same proportions. A varietal wine made from one grape can be possible within the specification when all other conditions are met.

The logic is both practical and stylistic. Earlier-ripening Merlot can spread harvest risk alongside later Cabernet; Sauvignon can contribute acidity and aroma beside Sémillon's texture. The final proportions say something about place, season, available plantings and the producer's choices.
:::

:::detail{#aanpassingsrassen parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation"}
### New varieties receive controlled room for trials

Under formal agreements, the current specification permits a small group of adaptation varieties. For reds these include [[grape.arinarnoa|Arinarnoa]], [[grape.castets|Castets]], [[grape.marselan|Marselan]] and [[grape.touriga-nacional|Touriga Nacional]]. For certain whites, varieties including [[grape.alvarinho|Alvarinho]], [[grape.floreal|Floréal]], [[grape.liliorila|Liliorila]], [[grape.sauvignac|Sauvignac]] and [[grape.souvignier-gris|Souvignier Gris]] enter the picture.

They are not new leading players: plantings are limited to five per cent of the relevant estate area and their use in blends is also tightly controlled. Authorisation creates scope to experiment with heat, drought or disease pressure without suddenly replacing the traditional palette.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — six styles require different decisions

In the vineyard, each style begins with sound fruit picked at an appropriate moment. Preserving acidity and aroma can justify earlier harvesting for fragrant white and rosé; red wine also needs sufficient ripeness in skins and seeds. Canopy work, yield and water balance affect that equation, while a humid maritime climate demands continued attention to fungal disease.

In the cellar, skin contact is the main colour control. Red wine ferments with blue-black skins and consequently gains colour and [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. Rosé receives far shorter contact or is pressed directly. White grapes generally ferment without skins and are protected from unwanted oxidation. Temperature, oxygen, vessel choice, lees contact and blending then shape texture and aroma.
:::

:::detail{#zes-routes-uit-een-dossier parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-bordeaux-aop"}
### Colour is a continuum; the names are categories

Regular red normally receives the longest [[concept.maceration|maceration]] and greatest [[concept.extraction|extraction]]. Claret remains legally red but seeks a lighter colour and often less tannin. Clairet is darker and more vinous than much modern rosé, while rosé is generally made paler and more immediate. Grape ripeness and the duration, temperature and movement of skins jointly determine the outcome.

Dry white ferments nearly all its sugar; in white with residual sugar, [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]] stops sooner or sugar remains for another controlled reason. That does not automatically make it a great sweet wine or a botrytis-led style. Bordeaux AOP includes an accessible sweeter-white category distinct from the specific sweet appellations of the Sauternais.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-bordeaux-product-page"}
## Appellation rules — a broad foundation beneath many wines

The specification determines where grapes originate, which varieties are permitted, planting density, maximum yield and the analytical requirements a wine must meet. It protects only still red, rosé and white wines; sparkling wine belongs to another designation, such as [[appellation.cremant-de-bordeaux|Crémant de Bordeaux]].

These minima establish origin and accountability. They do not prescribe a château, vessel, fixed blend or tasting score. The distance between a simple, fruity Bordeaux and an ambitious [[concept.cuvee|parcel cuvée]] can consequently be considerable even though both legally bear the same appellation.
:::

:::detail{#zes-wettelijke-verschijningsvormen parent="appellationregels" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.civb-bordeaux-aop"}
### Claret and clairet are not alternative spellings

Within red wine, the supplementary term *claret* is reserved for light-red wine. Within rosé, *clairet* denotes a dark-coloured rosé. Regular red and rosé remain without those terms. White may be dry or contain more fermentable sugar; *Haut-Benauge* is a supplementary geographical name used only with white Bordeaux and has its own conditions.

Together these produce six practically recognisable expressions: red, claret, clairet, rosé, dry white and white with residual sugar. Their differences concern not just colour, but extraction, structure, sweetness and drinking context.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### The numbers behind the label

New vineyards planted after 1 August 2008 must in principle contain at least 4,000 vines per hectare; 3,300 can be accepted under tighter row- and vine-spacing conditions. Base yields are 67 hectolitres per hectare for white, 62 for rosé and 60 for red. White wine bearing Haut-Benauge has a base yield of 55 hectolitres per hectare.

Minimum natural alcohol varies too: 10 per cent for regular white and rosé, 10.5 for red and white with more residual sugar, 11 for claret and 11.5 for Haut-Benauge. Total chemical weed control is prohibited and treatment use must be recorded. These are snapshots from the current specification; production decisions or exam preparation require consultation of the complete rules in force.

The 2026 specification also permits limited sweetening of claret. This may take place from 1 November after harvest, using only must, concentrated must or rectified concentrated must from grapes eligible for Bordeaux, without exceeding claret’s analytical sugar limit. It is therefore a narrowly bounded cellar adjustment, not a sweet style.
:::

## Classification and producers — no ranking for the broad AOP

Bordeaux AOP has no official château classification of its own. Familiar Bordeaux rankings belong to specific areas or historical groups of producers; they do not rank every wine carrying this broad designation. Terms such as *réserve*, *cuvée prestige* or a picture of a château are likewise not official Bordeaux ranks.

The producer landscape is broad: family growers and large estates work alongside cooperatives, brands and [[concept.negociant|négociants]]. Sometimes one business grows and bottles everything itself; sometimes grapes, must or wine are brought together and marketed by a merchant. That distinction describes the production chain, not quality automatically.
:::

:::detail{#herkomst-zonder-centrale-ranglijst parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025"}
### Producer, bottler and brand can be three roles

A label may feature a château or estate name, but it may also carry a merchant's brand. The bottling statement identifies who put the wine into the bottle; that need not be the party that grew all the fruit. Cooperatives can make wine from several members, while négociants select or blend lots.

For a buyer, consistency matters more than romantic architecture. Look at the producer or brand, exact cuvée, vintage and reliability of the seller. In a broad origin, reputation at producer level becomes especially informative.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-aop source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles"}
## Wine style and bottle development — from terrace glass to dining table

Red Bordeaux ranges from juicy Merlot fruit and supple tannin to Cabernet-led wines with more blackcurrant, herbs and grip. Most affordable examples are designed for youthful drinking; selection, concentration and [[concept.elevage|maturation]] can support longer bottle development. The appellation name alone does not predict that lifespan.

The lighter and white styles have different priorities. Rosé and dry white often centre on freshness and primary aroma. White with residual sugar seeks balance between sweetness and acidity. Cellaring advice should therefore begin with style and producer, not with “Bordeaux” as a single category.
:::

:::detail{#rood-claret-en-clairet parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-aop source.inao-bordeaux-clairet-product-page"}
### Three shades between red and rosé

Red can accompany anything from roasted vegetables and mushrooms to poultry or meat; serve it lightly cool, generally around 16–18 °C. Claret is lighter in colour and structure and can be served a little cooler when fruit and drinkability lead. Clairet has more colour and grip than pale rosé, allowing it to work both on a terrace and at the table.

These categories are not exact aroma recipes. Grape, vintage and extraction remain perceptible. Rosé, clairet and claret are usually best enjoyed for their youthful fruit unless a producer explicitly makes a more serious, ageworthy interpretation.
:::

:::detail{#rose-en-wit parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-aop source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles"}
### Fresh white, softer white and rosé need their own glass context

Dry white can combine citrus, gooseberry, flowers and herbs with a light to broader texture. Cool but not ice-cold—roughly 8–12 °C depending on weight—allows both aroma and freshness to register. Pale rosé is often the most immediate, dry and fruit-led style.

White with residual sugar can range from lightly off-dry to clearly sweeter. Acidity is then essential to balance. Read the label or ask the seller about sweetness; colour does not reveal it reliably. Most are intended for relatively early drinking, although Sémillon-rich or more carefully matured white Bordeaux can develop for longer.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — read beyond the large word Bordeaux

Start with the complete designation line and the colour or style indication. Then find the producer or brand, [[concept.vintage|vintage]], alcohol level and bottler. If *claret*, *clairet* or *Haut-Benauge* appears, it adds genuine legal information. Invented terms such as *vieilles vignes* or *réserve* may be useful but do not form a general official ranking.

With such a broad AOP, repeated experience is valuable. A dependable producer can provide more guidance across several years than an ornate name or heavy bottle. For older bottles, storage, fill level and the provenance of the individual bottle remain more important than appellation alone.
:::

:::detail{#bordeaux-of-bordeaux-superieur parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025"}
### Supérieur means different rules, not automatically a better bottle

[[appellation.bordeaux-superieur|Bordeaux Supérieur]] is a separate appellation with tighter conditions governing matters including yield, ripeness and maturation. The word is therefore more than marketing. Quality still depends on fruit, producer, cellar work and storage; *Supérieur* is not a tasting score.

Nor should red Bordeaux Supérieur be compared with white or rosé Bordeaux as though one ladder ranked every style. First decide which kind of wine you want, then how much structure, sweetness or ageing potential is desirable, and only then which origin and producer fit those needs.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Contemporary developments — freshness, resilience and relevance

Warmer seasons can help late varieties ripen, but they also increase drought stress, rapid sugar accumulation and loss of acidity. Heavy rain and fungal pressure have not disappeared. Growers are therefore exploring shade, canopy balance, soil life, deeper rooting, suitable plant material and more exact harvest timing for each site.

The market is changing too. Alongside concentrated red wine, interest is growing in lighter reds, rosé and fresh white wine. The historical return of claret-like drinkability fits that movement, but only convincing wine can turn a trend into a durable category.
:::

:::detail{#breedte-als-proeftuin parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### A broad appellation can use its differences

Because the AOP encompasses so many soils, microclimates, grapes and business types, there can be no single adaptation route. Dry gravel requires something different from water-retentive clay; a brand making early-drinking red sets different goals from a grower producing aromatic white.

Adaptation varieties are one visible tool, but soil care, yield, pruning, shade and vinification matter at least as much. The strength of this broad AOP does not lie in uniformity. It can accommodate several answers, provided origin, accountability and recognisable wine quality remain intact.
:::

Bordeaux AOP is not one taste or one soil, but the broad protected language of the region: three colours, six recognisable expressions and room for very different producers—within verifiable boundaries.
