Lussac-Saint-Émilion is the northernmost and largest of the four appellations commonly called the satellites of [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]]. It covers delimited parcels in the commune of Lussac and makes red wine only.

Limestone plateau, clay-limestone slopes and sandier northern ground give Merlot-led blends different balances of ripe fruit, freshness and tannin.
:::

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
## Location and boundaries — one commune, broad relief

The appellation follows the commune of Lussac, north of Montagne and Puisseguin. Within it, parcel delimitation selects plateaus, ridges and slopes with enough drainage; wet valley floors and strongly waterlogged ground sit outside the core.

“Satellite” helps orientation but carries no legal rank. Lussac-Saint-Émilion is not a zone of Saint-Émilion and does not use its Grand Cru Classé system.
:::

:::detail{#noordelijke-percelen parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
### In the north, slope decides

Current delimitation includes primarily sufficiently sloping parcels with natural drainage in the northern sector. This detail prevents the assumption that an entire commune automatically offers equal viticultural suitability.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-overview source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
## History — monks, claret and a contested name

Cistercians from nearby Faize Abbey cleared and planted slopes around Lussac in the Middle Ages. Through the Dordogne and Libourne, local wine joined the English trade in claret. Small peasant and bourgeois holdings gave the landscape a strongly fragmented pattern.

Court decisions in 1921 and 1923 established that communes outside the old jurisdiction could use “Saint-Émilion” only with their own name. Lussac-Saint-Émilion became an AOC in 1936.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024 source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-overview"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — from limestone plateau to sandy clay

Limestone and clay-limestone occur on and around the plateau; slopes can carry molasse du Fronsadais. Farther north and lower down, sandy clay, silt and old river deposits become more important. This alternation creates no simple north-south ranking: drainage and aspect need parcel-level reading.

The temperate oceanic climate gains a mild continental tendency inland, with warm summers and long autumns. This can aid ripening, while spring frost and humidity remain risks in low positions.
:::

:::detail{#waterreserve-en-drainage parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
### Holding water and losing water

Clay can support vines through dry periods, but a shallow impermeable layer can instead cause waterlogging. The useful balance is neither maximum clay nor maximum drainage, but sufficient reserve and oxygen around the roots.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot across a broad commune

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]] dominates. [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] are also principal varieties; Carménère and Petit Verdot share a limited accessory role.

Merlot brings ripe fruit and volume, Cabernet Franc fragrance and freshness, Cabernet Sauvignon firmness and Malbec colour and spice. The precise [[concept.assemblage|blend]] can use differences in soil and ripening time.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — selection from vineyard to cellar

Minimum density is 5,500 vines per hectare. If more than five per cent of the crop is unripe or unhealthy, the current specification requires sorting in vineyard or cellar. Full-surface chemical weed control is prohibited.

After skin fermentation and malolactic conversion, wine matures into spring. Oak can affect structure and aroma but is not compulsory in Lussac-Saint-Émilion.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
## Appellation rules — red from Lussac

Lussac-Saint-Émilion protects still red wine from delimited parcels in Lussac. Base yield is 53 hectolitres per hectare and minimum natural alcohol is 11%. Wine matures until at least 15 March and reaches consumers from 31 March after harvest. [@source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-overview"}
## Classification and producers — scale without an official ranking

Lussac-Saint-Émilion has no official château classification. This relatively large appellation contains many family businesses and varied parcels; a general style summary cannot replace that diversity.

A château or cuvée name is no Grand Cru Classé title. Compare producers through concrete origin, blend and maturation.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-overview"}
## Wine style and bottle development — generous fruit, variable firmness

Plum, red and black cherry, spice and liquorice often appear in descriptions. Warm, well-drained positions can give ample ripeness; more Cabernet or firm extraction brings extra structure. Earth and tobacco can develop with bottle age.

Serve around 16–18°C. A fruit-led wine may drink young; a firm cuvée sometimes needs air and time. Producer and year are decisive.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
## Labels and buying — Lussac cannot be omitted

The protected name is Lussac-Saint-Émilion. Large lettering for “Saint-Émilion” does not change origin or confer a right to Saint-Émilion Grand Cru. Read the entire name, then producer, vintage and bottler.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-lussac-saint-emilion-specification-2024"}
## Contemporary developments — refining a large appellation

Climate variation affects plateaus, slopes and lower ground differently. Cover crops, shade, harvest date and grape choice therefore need finer adjustment. Tighter environmental conditions also make clear that origin cannot be separated from soil management.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Lussac-Saint-Émilion is the northernmost and largest satellite appellation, yet remains one independent origin. Its strength lies not in a borrowed classification but in the variation among limestone plateau, clay-limestone slope and well-drained northern parcels.
:::
