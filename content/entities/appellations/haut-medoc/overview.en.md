:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-overview source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
Haut-Médoc is the long red-wine appellation running nearly fifty kilometres north from the edge of Bordeaux along the Garonne and Gironde. It surrounds the celebrated communal appellations of Margaux, Saint-Julien, Pauillac and Saint-Estèphe, yet it is not leftover ground between grander names. Its gravel rises, clay-limestone soils and sandy transitions support classed growths, family estates and a history of cooperation.

Cabernet Sauvignon often supplies the backbone, while Merlot generally brings earlier-ripening fruit and roundness. Haut-Médoc is nevertheless neither a fixed blend nor one flavour profile: the city fringe in the south, central gravel terraces and northern reaches towards Saint-Seurin-de-Cadourne differ in soil, water and settlement. Understanding the appellation means reading that breadth without reducing every good glass to a miniature Pauillac.

## Location and boundaries — a long appellation around famous neighbours

The AOP occupies the left bank of the Garonne and Gironde, from Le Taillan-Médoc beside metropolitan Bordeaux to Saint-Seurin-de-Cadourne in the north. Its official area covers 28 communes and is little more than ten kilometres wide. [@source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025]

Within that strip also lie the communal appellations [[appellation.margaux|Margaux]], [[appellation.saint-julien|Saint-Julien]], [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]] and [[appellation.saint-estephe|Saint-Estèphe]]. Their delimited parcels normally use the more specific name. Haut-Médoc fills spaces between and around them, while still following its own official parcel boundaries.
:::

:::detail{#een-kaart-met-enclaves parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Commune, appellation and parcel are three different maps

A commune's presence in the geographical area does not make all its farmland eligible for Haut-Médoc. INAO has separately delimited the productive vineyard parcels. Within one commune, different plots may qualify for a communal AOP, Haut-Médoc or neither.

A future Atlas map must keep these layers distinct. A simple line around municipal borders would imply that vineyard, appellation and administration coincide. In this patchwork, verified parcel geometry matters more than an attractive invented boundary.
:::

## History — from Bordeaux’s outskirts to château country

Medieval wine centres followed the river road and old Levade through the [[region.medoc|Médoc]]. Religious institutions and Bordeaux citizens held land there. From the sixteenth century, nobles and urban investors established *bourdieux*: agricultural estates that became precursors of the château as a wine enterprise.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, viticulture moved north from Bordeaux. Drainage, land clearance and foreign demand made gravel soils attractive for red wines from small-berried varieties. Château country therefore grew from land improvement, capital, trade and sustained local work — not from gravel alone.
:::

:::detail{#new-french-clarets parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
### A new red style found an international market

Early-modern English and Dutch merchants sought darker, more durable reds than the pale *claret* of earlier centuries. The “New French Clarets” joined riper fruit, skin contact, barrel ageing and increasingly distinct crus. Their success helped finance eighteenth-century expansion across the gravel terraces.

The nineteenth century brought further growth but also powdery and downy mildew and phylloxera. Replanting on American rootstocks, new treatments and later mechanisation changed the vineyard. Urban expansion and twentieth-century frost damage then removed some southern and less favourable zones for good.
:::

:::detail{#van-reputatie-naar-aoc parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025 source.legifrance-haut-medoc-order-2025"}
### Classification and appellation answer different questions

The 1855 commercial ranking was already more than eighty years old when Haut-Médoc was recognised as an AOC on 14 November 1936. Classification ranked named estates by historical market value and reputation; the AOC tied an area name to delimited land, grapes and production rules.

The current specification was approved on 31 July 2025. An old name can thus operate under current rules. This does not make the AOP timeless: parcel boundaries, environmental requirements and transitional measures have changed and must be read against the valid text. [@source.legifrance-haut-medoc-order-2025]

## Landscape, climate and soils — terraces divided by water

Haut-Médoc lies between the river frontage to the east and woodland towards the Atlantic to the west. Elevation ranges roughly from three to fifty metres: slight relief to a walker, yet enough to alter drainage and exposure for a vine. Ocean and estuary moderate temperature, while rain can test a harvest severely in some years.

Old river deposits remained after erosion as successive gravel terraces and low *croupes*. Between them, *jalles* and smaller waterways cut through the land. Sandy or clay-rich gravel alternates with clay-limestone and wetter, unplanted sectors. “Haut-Médoc is gravel” is a useful first sentence but a poor last one.
:::

:::detail{#grind-klei-en-kalk parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025 source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022"}
### Soil directs ripening through water and warmth

Deep gravel generally drains freely and warms relatively quickly, which can help late-ripening [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]]. Clay stores more water and may carry [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] through dry periods; limestone and sand offer different combinations of drainage, temperature and vigour.

The official text associates certain varieties with particular soils, but this is not a direct flavour machine. Rootstock, soil life, yield, canopy and harvest decisions mediate the effect. Blackcurrant does not literally come from stones, nor softness directly from clay.
:::

:::detail{#jalles-en-jaargang parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Water makes both place and vintage legible

Jalles divide gravel rises and carry water to river or estuary. Maintenance can reduce saturation, yet flat or heavy plots remain vulnerable in a very wet year. In dry seasons, the ability to retain water becomes an advantage. The same system can therefore behave differently each year.

Haut-Médoc consequently has a pronounced [[concept.vintage|vintage effect]]. Warm, dry weather is not beneficial without limit; heat can unbalance acidity and aromatic development. Rain is not solely harmful either: timing, soil and fruit health determine whether it replenishes or dilutes.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Grape varieties — Cabernet as pivot, not compulsory recipe

Haut-Médoc is red only. Its traditional varieties are Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]], [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]]. Cabernet Sauvignon often forms the largest part of the [[concept.assemblage|blend]], but the rules impose no universal château proportion.

Cabernet can carry blackcurrant, cedar-like aromas, acidity and firm tannins. Merlot normally ripens earlier and can add red or dark fruit and roundness. Cabernet Franc may bring perfume and line, Petit Verdot colour, tannin and spice. Malbec and Carménère are permitted but usually far less visible.
:::

:::detail{#ras-per-perceel parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Blending begins at planting

A producer distributes varieties between parcels according to drainage, warmth, water reserve and risk. Cabernet Sauvignon often occupies free-draining gravel; Merlot can play an important role on clay or colluvial deposits. These decisions determine years before harvest which lots can eventually be assembled.

In the cellar, varieties and parcels are generally followed separately. The final wine emerges through tasting. Blending is therefore both a style decision and risk distribution: an earlier-ripening variety may succeed when autumn weather threatens later Cabernet.
:::

:::detail{#aanpassingsrassen parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Four varieties may take a cautious part

Under a formal agreement, [[grape.arinarnoa|Arinarnoa]], [[grape.castets|Castets]], [[grape.marselan|Marselan]] and [[grape.touriga-nacional|Touriga Nacional]] are also permitted as climate-adaptation varieties. Together they may occupy at most five per cent of an estate's planted area and ten per cent of a commercial blend.

Those low ceilings make this a bounded learning phase, not a replacement of historical grapes. Permission says nothing about actual planting or future flavour. Their value must emerge through local experience of drought, heat, disease, ripening and compatibility with existing styles.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — structure requires ripe fruit and patience

A densely planted, trellised vineyard distributes growth across many vines and creates a narrow, well-exposed canopy. Pruning, shoot work and yield management must ripen fruit without fully exposing it to heat. Picking day balances sugar, acidity, aroma, skin and seed maturity against the forecast.

Red grapes ferment with their skins. Pumping over or submerging the cap extracts colour, flavour and [[concept.tannin|tannin]]; duration and intensity must suit ripeness. After [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|malolactic conversion]], lots are blended and aged.
:::

:::detail{#extractie-en-opvoeding parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### More extraction is not automatically more Haut-Médoc

Longer maceration may support concentrated, ripe fruit, but unripe or forcefully handled skins can give dryness and hardness. Temperature, vessel and oxygen shape extraction. The right choice differs between parcel and vintage.

[[concept.elevage|Ageing]] beyond winter gives wine time to clarify and tannin to integrate. Barriques are common and can slowly dose oxygen and add oak aroma; concrete, steel and larger or older wood provide other accents. The appellation requires development, not a compulsory share of new oak.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Appellation rules — more precise than a style description

The rules reserve Haut-Médoc for still red wine from delimited parcels. They define varieties, vineyard layout, minimum ripeness, yield, analysis and ageing. This protects a collective origin and production baseline; it guarantees neither cru status nor identical quality.

Wine must be aged until at least 31 March after harvest, may be bottled from 1 April and released to consumers from 15 April. Producers choosing longer ageing or stricter selection retain that freedom. [@source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025]
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### The figures behind the AOP

New planting requires at least 6,500 vines per hectare, no more than 1.80 metres between rows and at least 0.70 metres between vines. Base yield is 55 hectolitres per hectare, with a ceiling of 65. Grapes must reach at least 11 per cent potential natural alcohol; Merlot has a higher minimum must sugar than the other varieties.

Older plantings can temporarily differ under narrowly defined transitional provisions. One number is never the entire legal reality. For learning, the current norm and existence of transition suffice; cadastral exceptions belong in the specification, not a general tasting note.
:::

:::detail{#bodem-en-certificering parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Environmental requirements are concrete, but not one universal label

The current specification prohibits total chemical weed control and chemical control on headlands, requires soil analysis before new planting and requires participation in certain recognised environmental schemes. Producers also record their treatment frequency index.

This goes beyond drawing a geographical line, but does not make every wine automatically organic. Organic certification, HVE and other recognised schemes have different requirements. The exact statement on a bottle or producer website remains relevant.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.crus-bourgeois-medoc-classification-2025"}
## Classification and producers — five classed growths, much more Haut-Médoc

Five estates selling their wine as Haut-Médoc appear in the red 1855 classification: [[producer.chateau-la-lagune|Château La Lagune]] as a third growth, [[producer.chateau-la-tour-carnet|Château La Tour Carnet]] as a fourth, and [[producer.chateau-belgrave|Château Belgrave]], [[producer.chateau-camensac|Château de Camensac]] and [[producer.chateau-cantemerle|Château Cantemerle]] as fifth growths. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]

They are historical markers, not the complete identity. Haut-Médoc also includes many [[classification.crus-bourgeois-du-medoc|Crus Bourgeois]], [[classification.crus-artisans-du-medoc|Crus Artisans]], unclassified estates and different business models. The appellation is broader than its five best-known names.
:::

:::detail{#vijf-crus-in-context parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
### 1855 ranks estates, not all wine

Cantemerle was added as a fifth growth several months after the original list. La Lagune lies near Ludon in the south; the other four are further north in or around Macau and Saint-Laurent-Médoc. Together they already show that Haut-Médoc is not one compact village.

A classification applies to named estates and their historical succession, not every parcel in the AOP. Rank, current performance, second wines and other cuvées must be read separately. Fifth growth is not a flavour term, and an unclassified estate is not a quality verdict.
:::

:::detail{#crus-bourgeois-als-route parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.crus-bourgeois-medoc-classification-2025"}
### A periodic classification can move

The 2025 Cru Bourgeois classification applies to vintages 2023–2027 and divides estates across three levels. Haut-Médoc forms a large group within it, from widely distributed châteaux to smaller names. Unlike 1855, this is not an unchanged hereditary rank.

Use the list as navigation, not a complete producer encyclopaedia. On the platform, this broad layer belongs in a filterable classification or appellation collection. Only genuinely iconic producers warrant a long monograph; other useful names receive compact, comparable profiles.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-overview source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Wine style and bottle development — structured red across a broad range

Young Haut-Médoc can combine blackcurrant, blackberry, plum, cedar, graphite-like, herbal or floral accents with clear tannin and fresh acidity. Merlot and riper years may soften the middle; Cabernet and Petit Verdot can provide more line and grip. Oak ageing may contribute vanilla, toast or spice.

Styles range from accessible and fruity to concentrated and slow-opening. Soil, vintage, selection and producer matter more than a simple opposition with Médoc AOP. Haut-Médoc may on average show more Cabernet and barrel ageing, but averages do not guarantee one bottle.
:::

:::detail{#tijd-in-de-fles parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Age changes fruit, tannin and aroma

In balanced bottles, dark fruit can move towards cedar, tobacco, leather, dried leaf, earth and spice. Tannin may feel finer as compounds react and precipitate. Acidity and remaining fruit must keep the wine alive meanwhile.

Not every Haut-Médoc improves with long storage. Lighter cuvées can charm early; a strong cru from a suitable year may develop for decades. Ageing potential is a conclusion drawn from wine, producer, vintage and condition — not the appellation name alone.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025"}
## Labels and buying — Haut-Médoc is an origin, not a rank

Read the exact appellation first, then producer, [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]], vintage, bottler and any classification. “Haut-Médoc” does not automatically mean classed growth; nor do “Grand Vin,” a château drawing or a heavy bottle. A Cru Bourgeois designation belongs to a particular classification period.

Note the distinction from communal names too. An estate may sell different parcels or wines under different appellations. Producer name alone does not prove that every cuvée has the same origin, selection or ageing.
:::

:::detail{#kopen-serveren-en-bewaren parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-haut-medoc-overview"}
### Condition can matter more than prestige

For young wine, producer style and vintage help estimate likely structure. With older bottles, fill level, leakage, storage and reliable provenance become decisive. A damaged famous cru is no better purchase than a soundly stored lesser-known wine.

Serve around 16–18 °C as a starting point. Young, firm red may benefit from air; fragile old wine calls for careful pouring. Dark, consistently cool storage slows ageing. There is no universal drinking window.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
## Contemporary developments — city, climate and soil under pressure

At its southern edge, vineyards meet the expanding Bordeaux conurbation; elsewhere vines, forest, water management and other agriculture compete for space. Warmer seasons bring earlier ripening, heat and drought stress, while heavy rain and autumn storms have not disappeared.

Producers respond through canopy adjustments, later pruning, different rootstocks, ground cover, finer harvest decisions and a limited group of new varieties. No measure works identically everywhere. Gravel needs something different from clay in drought; a cool parcel may gain value in a warm year.
:::

:::detail{#adaptatie-zonder-eindpunt parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-haut-medoc-specification-2025"}
### Identity remains a verb

The aim is not to reproduce a museum style from one historical year, but to keep making recognisable red under changing conditions. That requires preservation and experiment: ripening Cabernet without losing freshness, protecting soils and learning new varieties without immediately remaking the blend.

Economic resilience matters too. Broad Haut-Médoc producers must balance quality, labour, inventory and sales outside the price levels of the most famous communal crus. That is precisely the relevance of the appellation as the living middle layer of the Médoc.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Haut-Médoc is not a waiting room for more famous village names. It is a long, varied red-wine appellation with its own land, rules and producers — read it through place, blend, vintage and maker, and only then through classification.
:::
