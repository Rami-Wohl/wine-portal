:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023 source.cotes-de-bourg-appellation"}
Côtes de Bourg lies where Dordogne and Garonne approach the broad Gironde. On a rolling Right Bank landscape of clay, limestone, silt and gravel, it chiefly grows red wine: Merlot often leads the blend while Malbec is more visible than in many Bordeaux appellations. A small but historic production of dry white belongs here too.

Côtes de Bourg is an AOP in its own right, not part of Côtes de Bordeaux. The names *Bourg* and *Bourgeais* may also be used legally. That distinction is essential beside neighbouring [[appellation.blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux|Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux]].
:::

## Location and boundaries — compact hill country near the confluence

The geographical area covers fifteen communes around Bourg. It lies on the Right Bank of Dordogne and Gironde, north of Bordeaux city and south of Blaye. Only officially approved parcels within the zone qualify.

The terrain is notably rolling: river terraces, limestone scarps, plateaus and small valleys follow rapidly. Wind, sun, drainage and frost risk can consequently differ within one commune.
:::

:::detail{#drie-namen-een-aop parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
### Three names, one AOP

The specification protects *Bourg*, *Côtes de Bourg* and *Bourgeais*. Côtes de Bourg is the most familiar in the market, but the other two are not separate quality levels or subzones.

Do not confuse *Bourgeais* as a legal wine name with [[region.blayais-bourgeais|Blayais and Bourgeais]] as the broader regional page. The first can appear on a bottle; the second helps explain landscape.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — the port before the appellation

Bourg grew on strategic high ground by the rivers. Its port connected wine, stone and farm produce to Bordeaux and Atlantic trade. Vineyards on surrounding hills benefited from access, while limestone quarries supplied building material.

The red AOC was recognised on 11 September 1936 and white on 14 May 1941. Those two dates explain why white belongs to the historical identity despite its small current scale. [@source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023]
:::

:::detail{#oude-ranglijsten-geen-huidige-classificatie parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
### Old rankings are no current classification

Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century merchants and authors ranked Bourg wines in commercial surveys. Such documents show that the origin was known, but they do not constitute a current official château classification.

Historical reputation should not be attached to a modern estate as a certification. Ownership, vineyards and wine style may have changed completely.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023 source.gironde-landscape-blayais-libournais"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — more relief than the map suggests

Clay-limestone soils occur widely and can join water buffering to drainage. Plateaus carry reddish Quaternary silts, locally called *terre de Sienne*; slopes can mix sand, clay and gravel. The Brouillon and Moron valleys cut through the hills.

Rivers and ocean moderate climate, but humidity and rain raise fungal pressure. Slopes can drain rapidly and be vulnerable to erosion; deeper clay helps during drought. Soil care is daily risk management, not an abstract sustainability term.
:::

:::detail{#de-paleodelta-van-pugnac parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
### Pugnac's palaeodelta

Around Pugnac, the subsoil preserves deposits from an ancient river system. They add sand, gravel and clay to the limestone picture. Elsewhere the limestone edge above Bourg forms steeper faces toward the river.

This geological variation helps explain parcel differences but supplies no automatic hierarchy. Rooting depth, drainage, management and season determine how soil actually functions.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot with a notable role for Malbec

For red, [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.malbec|Malbec]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] are the traditional principal varieties. Merlot often gives rounded fruit, Cabernets structure and aroma; Malbec can contribute dark colour, violets and spicy firmness.

White uses [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.sauvignon-gris|Sauvignon Gris]], [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]], [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]] and [[grape.colombard|Colombard]]. Sauvignon often gives acid and citrus, Sémillon texture and Muscadelle perfume.
:::

:::detail{#malbec-is-lokaal-maar-niet-verplicht parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023 source.cotes-de-bourg-appellation"}
### Malbec is locally distinctive, not obligatorily dominant

Côtes de Bourg likes to highlight Malbec, traditionally also called *Côt* here. Some producers give it a clear share or a dedicated cuvée; other blends remain strongly Merlot-led.

Treat Malbec as a distinguishing possibility, not a flavour guarantee for every bottle. Vintage and ripeness matter: unripe Malbec can be angular, ripe fruit can bring colour and floral depth.
:::

:::detail{#aanpassingsrassen parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
### Adaptation varieties under agreement

[[grape.carmenere|Carménère]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] appear in the current specification as adaptation varieties under formal agreement. Together they may form no more than five per cent of an estate's area and ten per cent of the final blend.

This status creates room to learn, not to replace the local palette overnight. Permitted shares and conditions may change in future specifications.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — ripe fruit with fresh edges

Red grapes ferment with skins; extraction is adapted to seed and skin ripeness. Malolactic conversion, blending and maturation in tank, concrete or cask usually follow. Abundant new oak is no quality requirement.

White grapes are pressed and generally fermented cool to preserve freshness. Lees contact or cask can add texture. Because white is small in volume, producer style can say more than a broad “typical” profile.
:::

:::detail{#hellingen-vragen-bodembeheer parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
### Slopes demand soil management

The specification prohibits total chemical weed control and requires vegetation on headlands. On slopes, plants can slow runoff and erosion; in drought they also compete for water and require management.

No single measure suits every parcel identically. Mowing, cultivation and permanent or temporary cover must follow soil, slope and season.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Appellation rules — red and white under an independent name

The AOP permits still red and white and defines communes, parcels, grapes, yields and cellar practice. Red has a minimum maturation period; the appellation is legally independent of Côtes de Bordeaux.

Rules protect origin and a baseline. They set no fixed Merlot–Malbec proportion and require no particular oak style.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
### Current thresholds

Minimum density is 4,500 vines per hectare. Base yield is 54 hectolitres per hectare for red and 60 for white, with ceilings of 65 and 72. Red wine may be marketed from 1 April after harvest. [@source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023]

Analytical, pruning and blending rules complete these visible numbers. Producers should always consult the current specification; learners benefit more from the relationships than memorising figures.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cotes-de-bourg-appellation source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Classification and producers — no current ranking

Côtes de Bourg has no official contemporary château classification. A historical mention, imposing building or heavy bottle is no rank. Family estates and collective production structures both carry the appellation.

When buying, seek producer, cuvée, grape mix and maturation. Representative producers can later be compared on one overview page; only exceptionally iconic estates require a full entity of their own.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cotes-de-bourg-appellation source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Wine style and bottle development — dark fruit without one formula

Red can combine plum, blackberry, violets, spice and earth. Merlot often gives a rounded core; Malbec and Cabernets can sharpen colour, freshness and tannin. The range runs from juicy and young to concentrated and cask-aged.

White is generally dry, fresh and fragrant, with citrus, white fruit and flowers. More Sémillon or lees contact can fill the palate. Most whites appeal young, although structured cuvées can develop briefly.
:::

:::detail{#aan-tafel-en-in-de-kelder parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.cotes-de-bourg-appellation"}
### At the table and in the cellar

Serve red around 16–18 °C with duck, stews, roast vegetables or hard cheese. White can be served around 8–11 °C with shellfish, fish or goat's cheese. A young fruity red can be slightly cooler.

Firm tannin alone predicts no long life. Fruit concentration, acidity, balance, closure and storage jointly determine whether bottle age brings gain.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Labels and buying — Bourg is not Côtes de Bordeaux

The bottle may state *Côtes de Bourg*, *Bourg* or *Bourgeais*. All three belong to the same AOP; none is automatically a higher tier. Check colour, producer, cuvée, vintage and bottler.

Do not let the word *Côtes* mislead: Côtes de Bourg sits outside the umbrella Côtes de Bordeaux AOP. Proximity to Blaye makes the wine geographically related, not legally identical.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Contemporary developments — keeping an independent voice

Côtes de Bourg can distinguish itself through relief, Malbec and an approachable scale, without presenting itself as a small version of a famous Right Bank name. Dry white and clearer parcel information broaden the story.

Heat, drought and heavy rain make water balance and erosion control more urgent. Rules on ground cover and limited adaptation varieties show a collective response; actual results continue to differ by parcel and producer.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Côtes de Bourg is an independent AOP for red and white with a hilly soil mosaic and a notable local role for Malbec. Its three permitted names are equivalent; producer and cuvée provide further precision.
:::
