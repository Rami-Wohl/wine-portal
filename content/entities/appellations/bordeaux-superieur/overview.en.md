Bordeaux Supérieur is the more ambitious legal neighbour of the broad [[appellation.bordeaux|Bordeaux AOP]]. Its origin is almost as extensive, but the rules demand greater ripeness, restrict yield and prescribe minimum maturation. The name covers red wine and white wine with fermentable sugar—not dry white or rosé.

The word *Supérieur* is seductive, but not a gold medal. It means that the wine was made within a separate appellation with stricter production thresholds. Producer, site and vintage still determine what ultimately happens in the glass.
:::

## Location and boundaries — an appellation on the scale of Bordeaux

Bordeaux Supérieur does not come from one compact hill or village. Eligible communes extend across almost the entire [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux region]], from the Left Bank to the country between Dordogne and Garonne and the slopes of the Right Bank. Only officially delimited parcels qualify.

The name consequently describes a production category more than one terroir. Two bottles can carry the appellation while coming from different soils, mesoclimates and grape proportions.
:::

:::detail{#zelfde-kaart-andere-regels parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
### The same map, a different legal route

Many vineyards can in principle supply either Bordeaux or Bordeaux Supérieur if parcel and production meet the relevant requirements. A producer chooses which appellation to claim and must justify it from harvest onward.

Wine is not “promoted” after a final tasting. Ripeness, yield and maturation must be organised around the stricter category from vineyard to cellar. Bordeaux is not rejected Supérieur either: the broad AOP has its own colours, styles and aims.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — concentration receives a protected name

Bordeaux traded in varied wine styles for centuries. There was demand within that stream for riper, more firmly built red wine and white wine with residual sugar. The distinction acquired legal form when Bordeaux Supérieur was recognised on 14 October 1943. [@source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026]

The category reflected the idea that restricted yield, sufficient ripeness and time before release could give wine more concentration. Rules changed, but *Supérieur* remained measurable rather than a free marketing word.
:::

:::detail{#geen-eeuwige-kwaliteitsladder parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
### A historical category keeps moving

Appellation rules follow agriculture, technique and climate. Grape lists expanded with tightly limited adaptation varieties and environmental provisions gained weight. Old and new bottles with the same name were not made under identical specifications.

The protected core—Bordeaux origin, greater ripeness, controlled yield and minimum maturation—remains recognisable. The current specification is decisive for current figures.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — much of Bordeaux in one name

The Atlantic climate brings mild winters, a long growing season and rain risk around flowering and harvest. Gravel can drain water and retain warmth; clay buffers water and often suits Merlot; limestone, sand and silt add other growth patterns. Bordeaux Supérieur can emerge from all these settings.

The appellation has no single soil flavour. A stricter ripeness requirement means something different on a cool clay slope and on warm gravel. Place, season and producer choices make the difference.
:::

:::detail{#rijpheid-is-meer-dan-suiker parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
### Ripeness is more than sugar

The law measures minimum natural potential alcohol, but growers also watch acidity, aroma, skins and seeds. Heat can raise sugar while tannin still tastes green; rain can dilute ripe grapes. “Leaving fruit longer” is not an automatic recipe.

A successful Supérieur combines concentration with freshness. Canopy, crop level, soil water and picking time matter as much as the cellar number.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
## Grape varieties — two clearly different palettes

Red relies chiefly on [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], with [[grape.malbec|Malbec]], [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]] as further options. Merlot often contributes roundness; Cabernets can add freshness, fragrance and backbone.

White principally uses [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]], [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.sauvignon-gris|Sauvignon Gris]] and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]]. Composition here balances sweetness, acidity, aroma and texture.
:::

:::detail{#aanpassingsrassen parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
### Adaptation varieties receive a small trial space

Under agreement, the current specification also permits adaptation varieties, including [[grape.arinarnoa|Arinarnoa]], [[grape.castets|Castets]], [[grape.marselan|Marselan]], [[grape.touriga-nacional|Touriga Nacional]] and Vidoc for red, and [[grape.floreal|Floréal]], [[grape.liliorila|Liliorila]], [[grape.sauvignac|Sauvignac]] and [[grape.souvignier-gris|Souvignier Gris]] for white. Together they may occupy no more than five per cent of an estate's area.

Permission does not mean they have already transformed the traditional blend. It is a limited testing ground for disease resistance, heat and drought.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — building concentration without heaviness

For red, grapes ferment with their skins. Extraction supplies colour and [[concept.tannin|tannin]], after which [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|malolactic conversion]], [[concept.assemblage|blending]] and [[concept.elevage|maturation]] complete the profile. Oak can add structure but is no compulsory flavour stamp.

White Bordeaux Supérieur retains sugar after fermentation. The winemaker must balance sweetness, acidity and aromatic clarity. Noble rot may occur, but it is neither required nor defining.
:::

:::detail{#tijd-als-onderdeel-van-de-categorie parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
### Time is an ingredient without aroma

Red must mature until at least 15 June after harvest and may be marketed from 1 July. White remains in maturation until at least 31 December of the harvest year and may follow from 15 January. That waiting period lets wine knit together.

Longer is not automatically better. A fruit-led wine can thrive in neutral tank with early bottling; a concentrated red cuvée may demand longer in cask and bottle.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
## Appellation rules — what “Supérieur” really means

The rules protect origin, grapes, minimum ripeness, planting density, yield and maturation. They do not decree that a red wine must be expensive, oak-flavoured or long-lived.

Three differences from broad Bordeaux are essential: riper grapes, less wine per hectare and a minimum period before release. White is moreover sweet or medium-sweet; dry white falls under another appellation.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
### The numbers behind the category

New plantings require in principle at least 4,500 vines per hectare; density can fall to 3,300 under conditions. Base yield is 49 hectolitres per hectare for white and 58 for red, with ceilings of 60 and 66 respectively. [@source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026]

These are thresholds, not tasting notes. A producer can select more severely, but low yield alone proves no harmony. Figures can change when the specification changes.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-overview"}
## Classification and producers — no château hierarchy

Bordeaux Supérieur has no official château ranking. The [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]] and Saint-Émilion classifications belong to other contexts. A family estate, cooperative or merchant house may use this appellation when its wine meets the rules.

Reputation must be built producer by producer and cuvée by cuvée. In such a broad category, origin, farming and selection can differ greatly; the label begins the enquiry, not the final score.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-overview source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
## Wine style and bottle development — firmer red, rarer sweet white

Red is often fuller than the lightest Bordeaux: dark fruit, spice and clear tannin are common. Style ranges from supple and Merlot-led to more serious and Cabernet-led. Better examples can develop cedar, tobacco and earth.

White ranges from gentle medium-sweetness to more pronounced sweetness. Citrus, flowers, apricot and honey may stand beside fresh acidity. Balance and concentration say more about ageing potential than appellation alone.
:::

:::detail{#aan-tafel parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-overview"}
### At the table, individual style matters

Serve red initially around 16–18 °C with roast meat, mushrooms or lentils; a young version can be cooler. Sweet white suits blue cheese, fruit dessert or spiced dishes and, with enough acidity, can also be an aperitif.

Do not automatically open red hours ahead. Young tannin may benefit from air, while an older bottle can fade quickly. Tasting remains the best clock.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — read “Supérieur” precisely

Check colour and sweetness first, then producer, vintage and bottler. On red, the name points to stricter thresholds; on white, to fermentable sugar. *Supérieur* does not mean “above all Bordeaux” and is no château classification.

For white, ask about actual sweetness. For red, grape composition, maturation and producer style help distinguish generous table wine from a more serious cellar candidate.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-bordeaux-superieur-specification-2026"}
## Contemporary developments — ripeness is no longer the only shortage

Warmer years make sugar accumulation easier but increase the risk that alcohol races ahead of freshness and tannin maturity. Drought, heat and heavy rain demand different canopy, soil and harvest management.

The category must prove anew what “more” means: not necessarily more alcohol or extraction, but more balance and attention. Adaptation varieties and ground cover are tools; convincing wine remains the aim.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Bordeaux Supérieur is a separate appellation with stricter production thresholds for red and white with residual sugar. Read *Supérieur* as a framework for ripeness, yield and time—never as an automatic tasting score.
:::
