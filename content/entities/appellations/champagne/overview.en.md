:::summary{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-region source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation"}
**Champagne is a protected sparkling wine from the French wine region of the same name.** Still base wine acquires its bubbles through a second fermentation in the bottle, then ages on the yeast sediment. Origin, grapes, blending and ageing produce many different wines within that one name. The [[concept.traditional-method|traditional method]] explains production; Champagne adds a specific origin and its own rules. [@source.champagne-region] [@source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation]
:::

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-region"}
## Location and boundaries

The vineyards lie in north-eastern France, east of Paris. Reims and Épernay are important centres, but the wine-growing area extends much further. Montagne de Reims, Vallée de la Marne, Côte des Blancs and Côte des Bar are four important subregions. The last lies further south, near Troyes. [@source.champagne-region]

The appellation comprises delimited vineyards spread across several departments. Landscape and protected wine name serve different functions: the region is a place, while the appellation determines which wine may be sold as Champagne. [@source.champagne-region]
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-history"}
## History

Champagne had vineyards long before sparkling wine came to define its image. From the late seventeenth to the early eighteenth century, effervescence increasingly became a deliberate production choice. Stronger bottles, control of sugar and fermentation, and later riddling and disgorgement made production more reliable. AOC recognition in 1936 enshrined the protected origin. This development spanned generations, with contributions from growers, trade and technology. [@source.champagne-history]
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-climate source.champagne-soil"}
## Landscape, climate and soils

The northerly climate combines oceanic and continental influences. Ripe grapes with sufficient acidity are important for base wine, while frost and changeable weather can threaten the harvest. Slopes give grapes better access to sunlight and help drain excess water. [@source.champagne-climate]

Chalk is a familiar part of the subsoil, alongside marl, limestone and clay, among others. Porous chalk can store water and make it available again. The distribution of soils and varieties differs between subregions; Champagne therefore has no single uniform soil profile. [@source.champagne-soil]
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-grapes"}
## Grape varieties

**Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier** are the principal varieties. Chardonnay is often associated with freshness and citrus or floral impressions; Pinot Noir with structure and red fruit, among other notes; Meunier with fruitiness and roundness. These descriptions are reference points for their local roles, rather than guarantees for every bottle. Other permitted varieties also occur on a small scale. A Champagne can combine varieties or highlight a single one. [@source.champagne-grapes]
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-pressing source.champagne-blending source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation source.champagne-riddling source.champagne-disgorgement source.champagne-dosage"}
## Viticulture and winemaking

Grapes are pressed carefully. Dark-skinned grapes can also yield pale base wine: much of the colour is in the skins, and extended skin contact is avoided for white wine. After the first fermentation, the maker selects and combines base wines. This **[[concept.assemblage|assemblage]]**, or blending, can bring together varieties, vineyards and harvest years. [@source.champagne-pressing] [@source.champagne-blending]

Yeast and sugar are added at bottling for the [[concept.second-fermentation|second fermentation]]. The bottle retains the carbon dioxide produced. After ageing on the yeast sediment, [[concept.remuage|riddling]] moves it towards the bottle neck; [[concept.disgorgement|disgorgement]] removes it. The maker can then use [[concept.dosage|dosage]] to adjust the final sweetness profile. [@source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation] [@source.champagne-riddling] [@source.champagne-disgorgement] [@source.champagne-dosage]

## Appellation rules

The protected origin combines a delimited area with conditions for grapes and production, including the second fermentation in the bottle. Non-vintage Champagne requires at least fifteen months of cellar ageing; a millésimé, Champagne from one harvest year, requires at least three years. Producers can age their wines longer. These minima provide a starting point for understanding the wine, rather than an exact prediction of its flavour. [@source.champagne-region] [@source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation] [@source.champagne-maturation-on-lees]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-label-faq"}
## Classification and producers

In Champagne, a **cru** is a wine-growing municipality. Grand cru and premier cru refer to origin in designated municipalities. These terms give a different kind of information from a producer's name or an individual cuvée. [@source.champagne-label-faq]

Professional codes can appear on labels. **RM**, récoltant-manipulant, denotes a grower making own-label Champagne from their own grapes. **NM**, négociant-manipulant, may purchase grapes, must or wine alongside any own-grown grapes. Cooperatives also play a role in production. These business forms explain how an enterprise operates; style needs to be assessed wine by wine. [@source.champagne-label-faq]
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-blending source.champagne-maturation-on-lees source.champagne-label-faq"}
## Wine style and bottle development

Blending gives the maker room to emphasise, for example, a recognisable house style or the characteristics of one harvest. [[concept.lees-ageing|Ageing on the lees]] also changes the wine: the breakdown of yeast cells can contribute to aromas and mouthfeel. Young fruit and floral impressions can combine with more mature, bread-like notes. [@source.champagne-blending] [@source.champagne-maturation-on-lees] [@source.champagne-label-faq]
:::

:::detail{#rijping-voor-en-na-degorgement parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.champagne-maturation-on-lees source.champagne-disgorgement"}
### Ageing before and after disgorgement

During cellar ageing before disgorgement, the wine remains in contact with yeast sediment. Once that sediment has been removed, a different phase of bottle development begins. Harvest year, ageing duration and any disgorgement date therefore describe different moments. Knowing the harvest year alone does not tell you how long a bottle spent on its lees. [@source.champagne-maturation-on-lees] [@source.champagne-disgorgement]
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-label-faq source.champagne-dosage source.champagne-blending"}
## Labels and buying

Read the label through three questions:

- **Which grapes?** Blanc de blancs uses white grapes; blanc de noirs uses dark-skinned grapes for a white wine. Chardonnay is important for the first group, Pinot Noir and Meunier for the second.
- **Which harvest?** A millésimé comes from one harvest year; a non-vintage Champagne can combine wines from several years.
- **Which sweetness?** Brut, extra dry and demi-sec describe different sugar classes. Extra dry is sweeter than brut. Brut nature requires less than 3 grams of sugar per litre and no added sugar.

These terms can appear together on one bottle because they answer different questions. [@source.champagne-label-faq] [@source.champagne-dosage] [@source.champagne-blending]
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-climate"}
## Contemporary developments

A changing climate affects the relationship between ripeness and freshness. Harvest dates more often fall earlier than before; extreme weather also requires vineyard adaptation. The interaction of site, grape and picking time therefore remains a practical question for the maker. Climate explains the circumstances, while blending and cellar decisions also shape the finished bottle. [@source.champagne-climate]
:::
