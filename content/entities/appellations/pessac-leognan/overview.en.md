Pessac-Léognan begins at Bordeaux’s southern edge and runs through suburbs and forest towards Léognan and Martillac. Vines sometimes stand literally among houses, roads and university buildings. Yet this contains some of Bordeaux’s oldest internationally known vineyards. The appellation makes red and dry white wine and includes every Classified Growth of Graves.

Red generally combines Cabernets with Merlot; white Sauvignon Blanc with Sémillon. Gravel terraces give the area its name and drainage, while clay, sand and limestone-bearing subsoil help determine how vines find water. That tension defines Pessac-Léognan: an old vineyard landscape living through a modern city.
:::

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
## Location and boundaries — vineyards at Bordeaux’s edge

The PDO includes approved parcels in ten communes, among them Pessac, Talence, Léognan, Martillac and Villenave-d’Ornon. Its northern part touches Bordeaux; farther south, forest and open farmland dominate. The Garonne lies east and the Landes forest west. This setting brings Atlantic mildness, local frost risks and enough rain to make drainage essential.

Pessac-Léognan was historically northern Graves. Since recognition in 1987 it has been a separate appellation. Graves remains geologically and historically correct, but [[appellation.graves|Graves]] on a modern label means a different, more southerly PDO.
:::

:::detail{#stad-is-ook-terroircontext parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
### The city is part of the terroir context

Urban growth fragmented vineyards, raised land values and put parcels under pressure. Buildings may also affect airflow, heat and logistics. The city is not an ingredient in wine, but it is a human and physical condition under which the vineyard landscape is protected and worked.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-history source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — before the Médoc became the famous Left Bank

The gravel lands immediately around Bordeaux belonged early to the region’s commercial heart. Trade with England and northern Europe gave Graves wines a leading position for centuries. Château Haut-Brion was ordered by name in seventeenth-century London—an early example of an individual estate rising above a general origin.

In 1855 [[producer.chateau-haut-brion|Château Haut-Brion]] became the only estate outside the Médoc included in the red classification. Disease, phylloxera and later Bordeaux’s expansion threatened vineyard area. A separate appellation, recognised in 1987, gave northern Graves a clearer legal and collective framework.
:::

:::detail{#een-nieuwe-naam-voor-oude-wijngaarden parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-rouge source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
### A new name for old vineyards

1987 was not the birth of viticulture but a reorganisation of origin. The compound name linked Pessac with Léognan and surrounding communes. Older labels may therefore say Graves where the same estate later uses Pessac-Léognan. Legal and historical continuity do not line up exactly.

## Landscape, climate and soils — gravel islands among forest and city

Ancient rivers deposited terraces of pebbles, gravel, sand and silt. Streams later cut them into separate ridges. Clay, sand, limestone or iron-bearing layers occur below the gravel. Their structure determines how quickly rain drains and how much water roots recover in a dry summer.

Pebbles warm readily and can support Cabernet ripening. Clay may buffer water and carry Merlot; cooler sites can favour white grapes and acid retention. These are opportunities, not flavour guarantees. [[concept.terroir|Terroir]] also includes vineyard management, season and human judgement.
:::

:::detail{#rook-is-geen-steen parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
### Smoke is not stone

Red and white Pessac-Léognan are often described as “smoky”, alongside graphite, flint or tobacco. These are tasting associations. Gravel does not simply transfer a smoke compound into grapes. Ripeness, reductive aromas, fermentation, sulphur compounds and oak can all contribute to the impression.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024 source.inao-pessac-leognan-blanc"}
## Grape varieties — Cabernet for red, Sauvignon and Sémillon for white

Red uses Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Cabernet Franc at its core, with Petit Verdot, Malbec and Carménère as possible additions. Cabernet Sauvignon often suits warm gravel ridges and brings blackcurrant, acidity and tannin; Merlot earlier fruit and volume.

White mainly combines [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]] and [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]], sometimes with Sauvignon Gris and Muscadelle. Sauvignon supplies tension, citrus and herbs; Sémillon breadth, texture and bottle development. Proportions and styles vary widely by château.
:::

:::detail{#twee-kleuren-een-perceelstrategie parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
### Two colours require one parcel strategy

White grapes must arrive fresh and aromatic; red grapes need mature skins and seeds. Soil, exposure and water reserve determine where each variety works best. A producer making both colours therefore does not plant or harvest by one calendar. Parcel selection connects geology to cellar.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — two cellar routes

Red ferments on skins and gains structure through controlled extraction. Malolactic conversion, [[concept.assemblage|blending]] and maturation, often in barrique, follow. White is pressed; juice may ferment in tank or barrel and mature on fine lees. Temperature and oxygen are carefully managed to preserve fruit and texture.

Barrel fermentation and new oak are visible in prestigious whites but not compulsory. Red can also lose its place beneath too much wood. The aim is not maximum cellar influence, but enough structure to carry fruit and development.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
## Appellation rules — red and dry white under one name

Pessac-Léognan protects still red and dry white wine. Its specification sets colour-specific rules for grapes, origin, planting density, pruning, ripeness, yields, analysis and maturation. It prescribes neither a fixed blend nor an oak regime. Styles can therefore vary on the same legal foundation.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
### The current production basis

The base yield is 54 hectolitres per hectare for both red and white; the general ceiling is 60. New planting requires high vine density and wine must meet colour-specific ripeness and analytical rules. A modification procedure opened in 2026 had not replaced the specification homologated in 2024 at this review.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
## Classification and producers — every Classified Growth of Graves

The [[classification.graves|Graves classification]] now comprises fourteen châteaux, classified for red, white or both. All lie in Pessac-Léognan. The classification has no hierarchy like 1855: a Cru Classé is not premier, deuxième or troisième within this system.

Haut-Brion is exceptional as both a Classified Growth of Graves and an 1855 First Growth. [[producer.chateau-la-mission-haut-brion|La Mission Haut-Brion]], [[producer.chateau-haut-bailly|Haut-Bailly]], [[producer.chateau-smith-haut-lafitte|Smith Haut Lafitte]], [[producer.domaine-de-chevalier|Domaine de Chevalier]] and [[producer.chateau-carbonnieux|Carbonnieux]] are other useful landmarks. Names reveal breadth; they do not replace colour, cuvée or vintage.
:::

:::detail{#classificatie-volgt-de-wijn parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths"}
### Classification follows the wine

A château may be classified for red, white or both. Rank does not automatically attach to every product an estate makes. Producer, appellation and classification statement must be read together on the label. This avoids transferring the reputation of one colour uncritically to the other.
:::

:::detail{#producent-la-mission-haut-brion parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths source.mission-haut-brion-history source.mission-haut-brion-wines"}
### Château La Mission Haut-Brion

La Mission Haut-Brion stands in the urban northern zone, opposite neighbouring Château Haut-Brion, but it is not a second label of that estate. Its history reaches back to 1540; Lazarist priests managed the property from 1682. The Woltner family, owners from 1919, modernised the cellar and began making a white wine in 1927. Since 1983 the estate has belonged to the Dillon family, which also owns Haut-Brion.

Red Château La Mission Haut-Brion is the wine recognised as a Cru Classé de Graves. The range also includes a white grand vin, a second red called La Chapelle de La Mission Haut-Brion and the shared dry white La Clarté de Haut-Brion. The estate therefore teaches careful reading: one owner can connect several châteaux and cuvées while vineyard identity and classification status remain separate. Making white wine does not mean that La Mission's present classification extends to white. The name on the bottle therefore matters more than the family connection alone.
:::

:::detail{#producent-haut-bailly parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths source.haut-bailly-history source.haut-bailly-vineyard"}
### Château Haut-Bailly

Haut-Bailly stands on a well-drained gravel ridge near Léognan. Firmin Le Bailly and Nicolas de Leuvarde assembled the core of the present property in the seventeenth century. Alcide Bellot des Minières bought it in 1872; the Sanders family followed in 1955 and American banker Robert G. Wilmers in 1998. After his death in 2017, his family continued its long-term stewardship.

The grand vin is red, and it is this wine to which the Graves classification applies. Cabernet Sauvignon generally supplies the backbone, joined by Merlot and other permitted red varieties. A remarkable historic parcel, planted in 1905 with six intermingled varieties, preserves something of the old mixed vineyard. That rare planting is historic heritage, not a general recipe for modern Pessac-Léognan. Haut-Bailly II is the second wine. Haut-Bailly thus shows how an estate without a classified white counterpart can still tell a rich story about soil, old vine material and selection within one colour, without turning that exception into a norm.
:::

:::detail{#producent-smith-haut-lafitte parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths source.smith-haut-lafitte-chateau source.smith-haut-lafitte-wines"}
### Château Smith Haut Lafitte

Smith Haut Lafitte lies in Martillac and takes its name from the Scottish merchant George Smith, who acquired the property in the eighteenth century. After a period under the Eschenauer wine business, Daniel and Florence Cathiard bought the château in 1990. They invested in vineyard and cellar and made biodiversity and in-house research into plant material visible parts of the estate story.

Unlike a producer recognised for only one colour, Smith Haut Lafitte is a Cru Classé de Graves for both red and white. The red grand vin is Cabernet-led; the white relies chiefly on Sauvignon Blanc with Sémillon as partner. The estate also makes second and other cuvées, but these do not automatically inherit the classification. Its sustainability and research claims represent the producer's perspective, not classification criteria in themselves. Smith Haut Lafitte is therefore most useful as a two-colour château: the same gravel setting produces two independent principal wines, requiring different grapes, picking dates and cellar routes.
:::

:::detail{#producent-domaine-de-chevalier parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths source.domaine-de-chevalier-estate source.domaine-de-chevalier-wines"}
### Domaine de Chevalier

Domaine de Chevalier occupies a clearing in the forest west of Léognan. Archives record an estate called Chivaley here in the seventeenth century, but its reputation as a wine property took shape mainly from the second half of the nineteenth century under the Ricard family. The Bernard family bought it in 1983; Olivier Bernard has led it since then with an explicitly long-term perspective.

Both Domaine de Chevalier rouge and blanc are Crus Classés de Graves. The red is built around the classic Cabernet-Merlot family; the white around Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon. L’Esprit de Chevalier also exists in both colours as the second wine. The white grand vin is not a footnote to red here, but one of the estate's defining identities. The wooded setting also distinguishes its context from enclosed vineyards closer to Bordeaux. Chevalier thus shows how one property can produce two seriously selected, separately classified styles without imposing a single cellar recipe on both.
:::

:::detail{#producent-carbonnieux parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths source.carbonnieux-history source.carbonnieux-vineyard"}
### Château Carbonnieux

Carbonnieux stands on a broad gravel ridge near Léognan and is among the area's oldest named properties: its name already appears in medieval records. Benedictine monks from Sainte-Croix played an important role for centuries in both the estate and its white wine. After a long succession of owners, Marc Perrin bought the château in 1956, a difficult year of severe frost. The Perrin family restored vineyard and buildings and still runs the property.

Carbonnieux is classified for both red and white. Each appears as an independent grand vin under the château name; the two require different grapes, harvest timing and vinification. Carbonnieux Blanc's long renown in particular corrects a misconception: dry white Graves is not a modern sideline beside prestigious red. Its medieval origins do not, however, prove an uninterrupted, unchanging wine style across eight centuries. This château forms a historical bridge between the colours and makes Pessac-Léognan's dual identity tangible.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-pessac-leognan-rouge source.inao-pessac-leognan-blanc"}
## Wine style and bottle development — cassis and cedar, citrus and wax

Red may combine blackcurrant, plum, cedar, graphite, tobacco and smoky notes with fresh acidity and firm tannin. White ranges from taut, citrus and herbal to rich, creamy and waxy. Sémillon and barrel maturation can add volume and development; Sauvignon generally preserves tension.

With bottle age, red becomes earthier and softer; white may develop honey, nuts and dried citrus. Not every example is built for decades. Producer, selection, vintage and storage determine actual longevity.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025"}
## Labels and buying — read colour and classification separately

Confirm Pessac-Léognan as appellation, then colour, producer, cuvée, [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and bottler. “Cru Classé de Graves” may apply to one colour only. Vintage matters especially for older bottles because labels before 1987 may still say Graves.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-pessac-leognan-specification-2024"}
## Contemporary developments — protecting vineyards and tracking climate

The appellation faces both urban pressure and a warmer, less predictable climate. Drought, heat and acid loss are increasing, while moisture and fungi remain real. Soil life, organic matter, ground cover, canopy, plant material and harvest dates are adjusted by colour and parcel. Protecting remaining vineyards is as spatial a task as it is viticultural.
:::
