Canon-Fronsac is a small red appellation covering two communes in the southern [[region.fronsadais|Fronsadais]]. From the tertre de Canon, steep limestone slopes overlook the Dordogne and its meeting with the Isle.

The name sits beside [[appellation.fronsac|Fronsac]], not above it. Merlot usually forms the heart; Cabernets and Malbec can add freshness, firmness and dark spice.
:::

## Location and boundaries — a compact zone in two communes

Canon-Fronsac lies solely on delimited parcels in Fronsac and Saint-Michel-de-Fronsac. The zone concentrates around limestone plateaus, tertres and slopes above the Dordogne. It is geographically more compact than Fronsac but not legally a sub-appellation of it.

The proximity is literal: some estates may own land in both appellations. Wine from each parcel nevertheless follows its own boundary and cannot move freely between the two names.
:::

:::detail{#kleiner-is-niet-hoger parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024"}
### Smaller does not automatically mean higher

A narrower boundary can offer a more precise landscape story, but it is not a quality class. Canon-Fronsac has its own rules and name; quality still depends on parcel, vintage and producer.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-overview source.vins-fronsac-vineyard"}
## History — from Côtes de Canon Fronsac to Canon-Fronsac

The hills share Fronsac's long military, religious and trading history. Their position above the Dordogne made the district visible and accessible, while the title of the dukes of Fronsac helped spread its wine name through elite circles from the seventeenth century.

The appellation was recognised on 1 July 1939 as Côtes de Canon Fronsac. Since 1964 it has used the shorter name Canon-Fronsac. “Canon” refers here to the local tertre, not to Château Canon in Saint-Émilion or to a canon of quality.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024 source.inao-canon-fronsac-overview"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — limestone on the river side

Limestone with fossil traces, locally called calcaire à astéries, forms a recognisable part of plateau and substrate. Calcareous clay and molasse du Fronsadais occur on the slopes. The boundary follows suitable plateaus and hillsides and excludes modern alluvial river soils.

The Dordogne and Isle can moderate temperature extremes; aspect and slope aid drainage. Steep parcels nevertheless require erosion care, and limestone ground varies greatly in depth and water availability by position.
:::

:::detail{#kalk-is-geen-smaak parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024"}
### Limestone is not an aroma

Limestone affects drainage, rooting and water relations but does not literally deliver a chalk or mineral flavour to wine. Grape, ripeness, extraction, maturation and bottle age also shape the glass. Soil language is most useful when it explains a growing site.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot on limestone and clay

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] are principal grapes; Carménère and Petit Verdot are accessory. Principal grapes must together account for at least eighty per cent of an estate's appellation plantings.

Merlot can retain rounded fruit and volume on clay-limestone. Cabernet Franc often gives a fresher aromatic line, Cabernet Sauvignon extra structure in warm positions and Malbec colour and broad tannin.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — steep parcels, measured extraction

The appellation requires at least 5,000 vines per hectare. Grassed headlands and restrictions on soil disturbance help protect the slopes. In the cellar, grapes ferment with their skins, after which the [[concept.assemblage|blend]] and maturation establish the balance among fruit, tannin and oak.

Maturation lasts at least until 1 June after harvest. This is a minimum, not a requirement for new oak or a particular style.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Appellation rules — a separate PDO for red wine

Canon-Fronsac protects still red wine only. Base yield is 53 hectolitres per hectare and minimum natural alcohol is 11%. Rules also cover ripeness, pruning, canopy, cellar analysis and labelling. [@source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024]

These minima guard origin and production conditions; they promise no aroma, oak choice or rank.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-overview"}
## Classification and producers — small without an official ladder

Canon-Fronsac has no official château classification. Limited area and family estates give the district a human scale but do not dictate one collective style. Producers can work with different parcels, oak regimes and cuvées.

Treat familiar estates as starting points for research rather than a hidden cru classification.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-overview source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Wine style and bottle development — compact fruit, limestone tension

Many examples combine plum, cherry and blackberry with spice and clear but ripe tannin. A fresh line and firm structure can support bottle age; earthy, leathery or truffle-like nuances may emerge with time.

That profile is a range. A supple cuvée may open young, whereas greater selection, extraction or oak asks for patience. Serve generally around 16–18°C.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Labels and buying — the hyphen holds the origin together

Look for the complete name Canon-Fronsac. Historic bottles may show Côtes de Canon Fronsac; “Canon” alone does not identify this appellation. “Grand Vin de Bordeaux” is likewise only a permitted broader indication.

Then compare producer, year, bottler and cuvée. Smaller size justifies no automatic price or quality assumption.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-canon-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Contemporary developments — protecting limestone slopes

Climate change increases both drought stress and the risk of erosive downpours. Canopy, soil life and water infiltration therefore need parcel-specific care. This compact appellation can preserve its distinction credibly only if its physical relief also remains intact.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Canon-Fronsac is a small, independent red appellation around the tertre de Canon. It shares the Fronsadais with Fronsac, but its limestone-rich plateaus, own boundary and full name must be read on their own terms.
:::
