:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-graves-overview source.inao-graves-specification-2025"}
Graves is simultaneously a word for gravelly soils, a historic wine region south of Bordeaux and a protected origin for red and dry white wine. Today’s Graves PDO begins south of [[appellation.pessac-leognan|Pessac-Léognan]] and follows the Garonne’s left bank towards Langon. Readers must therefore always ask which meaning is intended.

Red wine is built around Merlot and the Cabernets; dry white around Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc. The finest wines share neither colour nor a fixed aroma, but a landscape of old river terraces where gravel, sand, clay and subsoil govern drainage and water reserves.
:::

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-graves-overview source.inao-graves-specification-2025"}
## Location and boundaries — along the Garonne, south of the city

The vineyard extends in a long, irregular band from La Brède towards Langon. The Garonne lies to the east; the Landes forest partly shelters the west from Atlantic influence. Villages, woodland, farmland and vines alternate. Only officially delimited parcels in permitted communes may produce Graves.

Historically, the idea of Graves extended to Bordeaux’s gates. Since 1987 its northern core has had the separate Pessac-Léognan PDO. A modern bottle labelled “Graves” therefore does not come from Haut-Brion or Léognan, although both belong to the larger historical and geological Graves family.
:::

:::detail{#drie-betekenissen-een-naam parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-graves-specification-2025 source.graves-winegrowers-terroir"}
### Three meanings, one name

*Graves* is the French plural of *grave*: gravel and pebbles. Such soils also occur beyond this PDO. The historical region includes Pessac-Léognan and borders sweet-wine areas. The legal origin on a label is narrower. These three layers overlap but are not interchangeable.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-history source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — Bordeaux’s oldest trading axis

Viticulture around the city and along the Garonne expanded strongly in the Middle Ages. After Eleanor of Aquitaine married Henry II in 1152, a large English market came within reach. Bordeaux’s trading privilege long favoured wines from the city’s immediate surroundings over those arriving from upriver. The gravel lands of Graves were therefore at the beginning, not the margin, of commercial Bordeaux.

From the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries, larger estates emerged and “vins de Graves” stood for leading Bordeaux wine in northern Europe. The nineteenth century brought disease and phylloxera; the twentieth frost, war, economic pressure and urban growth. Recovery from the 1970s came with investment in vineyard and cellar. Graves became an AOC in 1937.
:::

:::detail{#rood-wit-en-een-veranderende-markt parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-history"}
### Red, white and a changing market

Historic colour terms are difficult: medieval *claret* was paler and made differently from modern red. Dry white Graves later gained its own prominent identity, while red vineyard area expanded again in the modern era. This is not a straight line towards today’s blend, but a history of changing taste, technique, market and legal categories.

## Landscape, climate and soils — lacework of river terraces

The Garonne and its predecessors deposited terraces of pebbles, gravel, sand and silt during successive Quaternary periods. Sand, clay, limestone or iron-bearing layers may lie underneath. Stream valleys later cut the deposits into an irregular pattern of ridges and slopes rather than one continuous gravel sheet.

Coarse topsoil drains rain and warms readily; finer subsoil may retain water. Slope assists runoff. The Atlantic climate is mild but wet enough for flowering, fungal pressure and harvest rain to shape a [[concept.vintage|vintage]]. “Gravel” begins the explanation; it does not complete it.
:::

:::detail{#steen-maakt-geen-smaak parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-terroir"}
### Stone does not manufacture flavour

Pebbles can absorb solar warmth and affect the bunch environment, but they do not literally impart mineral flavour to wine. Texture, depth and subsoil primarily govern water, roots and ripening. Variety, plant material, yield, weather and winemaking then translate those conditions into the glass.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-graves-specification-2025 source.graves-winegrowers-wines"}
## Grape varieties — two colours, two blends

For red, [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] are central; Petit Verdot, Malbec and Carménère may supplement them. Merlot often brings roundness and earlier-ripening fruit, Cabernet Sauvignon structure and length. Proportions vary by soil and estate.

Dry white relies on [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], with Sauvignon Gris and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]] as possible partners. Sauvignon usually supplies tension and pronounced citrus or herbal aromas; Sémillon can add breadth, waxy texture and development. The [[concept.assemblage|blend]] may be fresh and unoaked or richer and barrel-matured.
:::

:::detail{#wit-is-geen-bijproduct parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-wines"}
### White is not a by-product

Dry white Graves has its own harvest logic. Sauvignon can lose aroma and acidity when left too long; Sémillon may find texture and ripeness later. Separate picking and fermentation enable a precise blend. Some producers choose steel and early bottling, others barrel fermentation, lees contact and longer maturation. Both can legally be Graves.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-graves-specification-2025"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — colour determines the route

For red, grapes ferment with their skins; extraction, temperature and time shape colour and tannin. Malolactic conversion, blending and barrel or tank maturation follow. For white, juice is generally separated from skins earlier and temperature and oxygen are carefully managed. Fermentation may occur in tank or barrel; lees contact can add texture.

Oak is a choice for either colour, not a compulsory quality mark. Too much new wood can cover fruit; carefully used barrels can support structure and controlled oxygen exposure. Producer style therefore matters at least as much as colour.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-graves-specification-2025 source.eambrosia-graves-superieures-register-2026"}
## Appellation rules — dry Graves is red or white

The Graves PDO protects still red and dry white wine. Sweeter white wine from the broader zone belongs to the separate [[appellation.graves-superieures|Graves Supérieures]] name when it meets those rules. On the verification date, 22 September 2026, that name remained registered in the EU register. “White” alone therefore does not tell you dry or sweet; the exact appellation does.
:::

:::detail{#verschillende-rendementen parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-graves-specification-2025"}
### Different yields for different colours

The current specification sets a base yield of 55 hectolitres per hectare for red and 58 for white, with higher general ceilings. Rules for grapes, ripeness and analysis also vary by colour. Those thresholds protect a product category; they do not guarantee complexity or replace the complete specification.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths"}
## Classification and producers — the list now lies to the north

The [[classification.graves|Graves classification]] was established in the 1950s for individual châteaux and for red, white or both. Every classified estate now lies in Pessac-Léognan. A modern Graves PDO bottle from the southern area is therefore not a “Cru Classé de Graves”, although it comes from the historic region and soil family.

For Graves, producer, cuvée and local setting are more useful buying anchors than an official rank. An overview can introduce representative estates without inventing its own pseudo-classification.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-wines source.inao-graves-specification-2025"}
## Wine style and bottle development — red with line, white with tension

Red Graves ranges from supple, red- and black-fruited wines to firmer, cedar-scented examples suited to bottle age. Dry white may be taut and citrus-led or broader, smoky and creamy through Sémillon and barrel maturation. The familiar word “smoky” is a style association, not evidence that gravel literally releases smoke.

Red may develop tobacco and forest floor; richer white honey, wax and nuts while acidity preserves freshness. Not every bottle is intended for long ageing. Producer, vintage, grape balance and storage decide.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.eambrosia-graves-superieures-register-2026"}
## Labels and buying — two names in transition

Read the full appellation, colour, producer, cuvée, vintage and bottler. On 22 September 2026, “Graves” on a dry white bottle and “Graves Supérieures” on a sweet one were still legally separate names. Older books may still treat Pessac-Léognan under Graves; vintage and the rules then in force prevent confusion.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-graves-specification-2025"}
## Contemporary developments — water, heat and two harvest clocks

Climate change advances ripening and raises drought, heat and acidity-loss risks, but wet disease periods remain. Producers making red and white often follow several varieties with different harvest windows. Ground cover, shade, organic matter, plant material and picking plans form a toolbox, not a universal recipe.
:::
