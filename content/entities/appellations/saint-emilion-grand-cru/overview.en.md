Saint-Émilion Grand Cru is not an honour that a producer awards itself after a particularly successful harvest. It is a separate appellation for dry red wine, covering the same geographical territory as [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]] but imposing stricter requirements for yield, ripeness and ageing. [@source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023]

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] dominate here too. The additional rules create room for selection and time, but do not make every bottle great. One distinction is essential: **Grand Cru** is the appellation; **Grand Cru Classé** and **Premier Grand Cru Classé** are ranks in the separate [[classification.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion Classification]].
:::

## Location and boundaries — the same place, a separate promise

The appellation lies on the Right Bank of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]], around the medieval town of Saint-Émilion and east of Libourne. It comprises eight communes and a delimited part of Libourne. Only still dry red wine may carry the name.

Remarkably, the geographical area is identical to that of Saint-Émilion. The difference is not a tidy line with ordinary vineyards on one side and “grand cru soil” on the other, but the production and maturation regime under which the wine is declared. [@source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023]
:::

:::detail{#dezelfde-zone-een-andere-aop parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
### Geographical equality does not settle parcel eligibility

The outer geographical area is shared, but wine must still come from officially admitted production parcels and meet every condition. The specification identifies approved parcel delimitation and deposited maps as the legal reference.

Atlas may eventually show the two appellations as overlapping areas, but it must distinguish an outer boundary, parcel eligibility and production rule. One coloured “Grand Cru zone” would be misleading.
:::

## History — a young appellation in an old wine landscape

The landscape is far older than the Grand Cru name. Roman viticulture, medieval religious institutions, the Jurade, Libourne's port and centuries of trade shaped the vineyard around Saint-Émilion. Town, quarries, cellars and vineyards grew together so closely that UNESCO recognised the historic jurisdiction as a cultural landscape in 1999.

The modern legal hierarchy arrived only in the twentieth century. Saint-Émilion was defined as an AOC in 1936; Saint-Émilion Grand Cru received its own place in the system in 1954.
:::

:::detail{#van-vier-naar-twee-appellations parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
### From four appellations to two appellations and a classification

The 1954 decree distinguished Saint-Émilion, Saint-Émilion Grand Cru, Saint-Émilion Grand Cru Classé and Saint-Émilion Premier Grand Cru Classé. The first classification followed in 1955, was completed in 1958 and was intended for periodic revision.

Since 1984, only Saint-Émilion and Saint-Émilion Grand Cru remain appellations. The two *classé* phrases became classification ranks within Grand Cru. That history explains much of today's terminology. [@source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023]
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — no separate Grand Cru hill

Grand Cru shares Saint-Émilion's landscape: limestone plateau and slopes around the town, clay-limestone soils, sandy areas, gravel rises towards [[appellation.pomerol|Pomerol]] and lower alluvial plains towards the Dordogne. Relief and soil chiefly influence water, warmth, vigour and rooting.

The appellation can therefore produce wines of very different construction. Limestone slope, deep clay and warm gravel respond differently to rain, heat and grape choice; none is automatically “the Grand Cru soil”.
:::

:::detail{#een-mozaiek-geen-grand-cru-bodem parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
### Stricter rules select wine, not one geology

A producer may declare parcels from one estate under different appellations, provided the wine and production meet the relevant conditions. The category therefore selects a product made to a stricter specification, not one soil type.

Soil remains important for ripening and water, but not as a direct aroma stamp. “Limestone”, “clay” or “gravel” cannot explain a tasting note without parcel, season, grape and cellar work.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
## Grape varieties — the same family, more selective fruit

Merlot is dominant and can provide ripe red and dark fruit, volume and broad texture. Cabernet Franc may add perfume, freshness and firm yet fine tannin. [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] is less common and can bring structure and ageing potential on suitable warm sites.

Grand Cru prescribes no fixed blend. [[concept.assemblage|Blending]] may combine grapes, parcels and cellar lots to balance fruit, acidity, tannin and length.
:::

:::detail{#dezelfde-familie-meer-selectieruimte parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
### The less visible permitted varieties

[[grape.carmenere|Carmenère]] and [[grape.malbec|Côt or Malbec]] are also principal varieties. [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] is accessory and may form no more than ten percent of both estate plantings and the final blend. A wine may not consist solely of this accessory variety. [@source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023]

That completes the legal family without implying that these minor grapes are noticeable in every bottle.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — time begins with selection

Healthy, ripe fruit is the foundation. During red fermentation, skins release colour, aroma and [[concept.tannin|tannin]]; the maker guides [[concept.extraction|extraction]] through temperature, time and movement. Grapes, parcels and vessels can then be assembled into a coherent wine.

Grand Cru does not demand maximum power. Lower yields may support concentration, but balance also depends on water, ripeness, sorting and extraction suited to the skins. Drawing more material from a grape is not the same as obtaining more finesse.
:::

:::detail{#langere-opvoeding-is-geen-vatrecept parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
### Mandatory time, free choice of vessel

The wine undergoes longer [[concept.elevage|élevage]] than Saint-Émilion, but no universal share of new oak is required. Barrique, older wood, large cask, concrete and other permitted vessels can play a role.

Ageing allows tannin to develop and lots to knit together; it can also add excessive oak, oxygen or weight. The legal duration provides time, while quality depends on what a producer does with it.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023 source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
## Appellation rules — lower yield, riper fruit and more time

Compared with Saint-Émilion, Grand Cru lowers the base yield, raises some ripeness thresholds and substantially extends compulsory ageing. Those are the tangible reasons why the two names differ in law.

The rules guarantee origin and a production floor, not a star rating. A producer must still decide which parcels and lots are strong enough, and an unclassified bottle may be excellent.
:::

:::detail{#de-verschillen-in-cijfers parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023 source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### Saint-Émilion and Grand Cru side by side

Both require at least 5,500 vines per hectare for new planting. Base yield falls from 53 hl/ha in Saint-Émilion to 46 in Grand Cru. Grand Cru requires at least 194 grams of sugar per litre of must for Merlot and 189 for other grapes, with at least 11.5% potential natural alcohol. [@source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023]

The greatest time difference is in the cellar. Saint-Émilion ages until at least 31 March after harvest; Grand Cru until 1 February of the second year and may reach consumers from 15 May that year. Transitional rules may cover older plantings.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
## Classification and producers — the appellation is larger than the ranking

Every classified Saint-Émilion growth makes wine within Saint-Émilion Grand Cru, but not every Saint-Émilion Grand Cru is classified. Only officially admitted estates may use *Grand Cru Classé* or *Premier Grand Cru Classé*.

The classification is periodically revised and therefore more mobile than [[classification.bordeaux-1855|Bordeaux 1855]]. It assesses candidates through wine quality and consistency, reputation, estate characteristics and management. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022]
:::

:::detail{#de-editie-van-2022 parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021"}
### Eighty-five classified growths, plus famous absences

The 2022 edition contains 14 Premiers Grands Crus Classés, including Château Figeac and Château Pavie with distinction A, and 71 Grands Crus Classés: 85 in all. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022]

[[producer.chateau-cheval-blanc|Château Cheval Blanc]] and [[producer.chateau-ausone|Château Ausone]], historic icons of the highest rank, did not apply in 2022. [@source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021] Their wines may remain Saint-Émilion Grand Cru; only the current classification wording is absent.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview"}
## Wine style and bottle development — concentration without uniformity

Saint-Émilion Grand Cru may show ripe red and black fruit, flowers and spice, with a palate ranging from velvety to compact and tannic. The category is generally aimed at longer development than Saint-Émilion, yet still encompasses hundreds of producer choices and very different sites.

With bottle age, fruit may shift towards dried plum, cedar, tobacco, leather, mushroom and forest floor. Not every wine needs long cellaring: structure, acidity, concentration, [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and storage determine the real window.
:::

:::detail{#concentratie-zonder-uniformiteit parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview"}
### The label does not predict weight

Lower yield and longer ageing can support intensity and integration, but do not dictate flavour. One producer may pursue freshness, perfume and fine extraction; another ripeness, density and prominent new oak.

Compare producer, grape mix, site and year. “Grand Cru” alone is too broad to assign a blind style or lifespan.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023 source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
## Labels and buying — the complete name matters

First find **Saint-Émilion Grand Cru**, then producer, [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]], vintage and bottler. *Grand Cru Classé* or *Premier Grand Cru Classé* is additional information and must match the applicable classification; decorative language or a château image is not a rank.

For older bottles, storage, fill level and provenance matter greatly. A classified name or fine year cannot repair poor storage.
:::

:::detail{#vier-regels-op-het-etiket parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
### Reading from broad to precise

1. **Bordeaux** is the large region.
2. **Saint-Émilion Grand Cru** is the appellation.
3. **Grand Cru Classé** or **Premier Grand Cru Classé** is a possible rank.
4. **Producer, cuvée and vintage** identify the actual wine in the bottle.

Serve at roughly 16–18 °C as a starting point. Young, firm wine may benefit from air; old wine calls for gentle pouring. Treat decanting as a choice for the bottle, not a compulsory appellation ritual.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
## Contemporary developments — more time creates more trade-offs

Warmer and less predictable seasons alter the balance between sugar, acidity, skin ripeness and water. A higher legal ripeness threshold may not be harder to reach; retaining freshness and healthy tannin can instead become the greater challenge.

Producers respond through soil management, canopy, picking date, gentler extraction and more exact vessel choice. Long compulsory ageing also requires cellar space, energy and capital before wine can be sold.
:::

:::detail{#meer-tijd-meer-afwegingen parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
### Stricter rules are economic choices too

Less volume per hectare and later release can support selection, but increase financial pressure. That helps explain why Grand Cru is neither a free upgrade nor merely a marketing word.

The category nevertheless remains broad. Climate adaptation and cellar style will vary by soil, grape mix and estate; reliable understanding requires producer knowledge alongside the rulebook.
:::
