Castillon Côtes de Bordeaux lies where limestone hills east of Saint-Émilion descend towards the Dordogne. Merlot-led red is the only permitted style, yet within that frame the spectrum runs from juicy and immediate to deep, muscular wine made for bottle development.

The appellation takes its name from Castillon-la-Bataille. Its identity rests not on one famous château façade but on river terraces, clay-rich lower slopes, steep hillsides and limestone plateaus.

## Location and boundaries — upstream from Saint-Émilion

The vineyards occupy the Right Bank of the Dordogne immediately east of [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]]. Nine communes or historic municipal areas form the legal zone, from Castillon-la-Bataille by the river to Saint-Philippe-d'Aiguille on the higher eastern edge.

Castillon is a geographical denomination within [[appellation.cotes-de-bordeaux|Côtes de Bordeaux]]. “Castillon Côtes de Bordeaux” is the complete origin name and protects only still red wine.
:::

:::detail{#rivier-helling-en-plateau parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.castillon-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview"}
### River, slope and plateau

Across more than one hundred metres of elevation, three broad zones follow one another: gravel and alluvium below, clay-rich feet and slopes, then clay-limestone and limestone above. This profile helps explain differences in drainage, frost risk and ripening without forcing every parcel into one category.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — wine country around a decisive battle

The Dordogne linked the area to Libourne and Bordeaux for centuries. Castillon-la-Bataille became famous for the 1453 battle that ended the Hundred Years' War; viticulture remained part of a landscape of villages, mixed farming and river trade.

The origin was recognised in 1955 as Bordeaux-Côtes de Castillon and renamed Côtes de Castillon in 1989. Since the 2009 vintage it has sat as Castillon within the collective Côtes de Bordeaux specification. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.castillon-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — limestone above, clay and gravel below

Limestone, often mixed with clay, marks higher ground; clay, sand and gravel occur in changing proportions on slopes and lower positions. The Dordogne and Atlantic climate temper extremes, while the eastern inland position brings more continental variation than the Médoc.

Limestone-rich slopes can drain well and clay can retain water. Site and soil depth determine how those properties work in practice. “Limestone” is no automatic promise of finesse, just as “clay” cannot guarantee power.
:::

:::detail{#water-en-rijping parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Water and ripening

A warm, free-draining slope can give Cabernet more time to ripen; a clay-rich parcel can sustain Merlot through a dry summer. In wet years, drainage and an open canopy matter more. The recognisable Castillon pattern comes from this interaction, not soil alone.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot with Cabernet as backbone

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]] generally leads and can supply ripe plum fruit and a rounded core. [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] often adds perfume and freshness; [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] can bring structure on warm, free-draining sites. Malbec is also principal; Carménère and Petit Verdot are accessory.

The precise [[concept.assemblage|blend]] follows parcels and vintage. A high proportion of Merlot says little by itself about extraction, tannin ripeness or maturation.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — ripeness without weight as the goal

Different elevations and aspects allow picking to be spread over time. In the cellar, red ferments with skins; extraction may be gentle or firmer, followed by malolactic conversion, blending and maturation in tank, concrete or oak.

The best choices preserve ripe fruit and tension. Abundant new oak or maximum concentration are producer decisions, not appellation rules or automatic measures of quality.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Appellation rules — a red denomination with its own rigour

Castillon protects only still red wine from delimited parcels. The shared specification determines grapes, pruning, ripeness, yield, extraction conditions and minimum maturation. Red must undergo malolactic conversion.

The place name therefore adds more than marketing: it narrows origin and raises several production thresholds beyond umbrella red Côtes de Bordeaux.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Current thresholds

Castillon requires at least 5,000 vines per hectare, against 4,500 for the other geographical denominations. Base yield for red is 52 hectolitres per hectare and minimum natural alcohol 11.5%. Maturation continues at least until 15 March after harvest; consumer release follows from 31 March. [@source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.castillon-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview"}
## Classification and producers — ambition without official rank

Castillon has no official château classification. Small family estates, larger properties and outside investors work alongside one another. Proximity to Saint-Émilion can explain interest but does not make an estate an extension of its neighbour.

Compare producers through site, cuvée, grape mix, extraction and maturation. A future collection can show representative estates without inventing an informal ranking.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.castillon-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview"}
## Wine style and bottle development — ripe fruit with limestone tension

Castillon can combine plum, cherry and blackberry with violets, spice and earthy notes. Tannin ranges from supple to firm. Wine from riper hillside parcels and careful selection may develop for years towards tobacco, leather and woodland; lighter cuvées are most vivid young.

Serve young, structured red around 16–18 °C and give it air if needed. Ageing potential follows balance, concentration, acidity, tannin and storage — not the Castillon name alone.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
## Labels and buying — Castillon is an origin, not a château

Read “Castillon Côtes de Bordeaux” as one complete appellation name. Then check producer, cuvée, vintage and bottler. Older labels may say Côtes de Castillon; that historic name calls for dating, not an assumption that it was another modern rank.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.castillon-cotes-de-bordeaux-overview"}
## Contemporary developments — precision on a warmer hillside

Castillon attracts attention for limestone sites and increasingly precise red wine. Heat and drought also increase the value of clay and shade, while heavy rain can intensify erosion on slopes.

Later picking is not automatically better. Soil care, canopy, harvest timing and measured extraction must preserve fruit, freshness and tannin ripeness together.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Castillon Côtes de Bordeaux is a red-only Right Bank appellation across terraces, clay slopes and limestone plateaus. Merlot supplies the core; elevation, water, Cabernet and producer choices decide whether the wine is juicy or deep and ageworthy.
:::
