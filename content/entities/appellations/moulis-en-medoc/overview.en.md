:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-overview source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
Moulis-en-Médoc lies away from the Gironde, slightly inland in the [[region.medoc|Médoc]]. It is the smallest of the six communal appellations and makes red wine only. Gravel from old river terraces, clay and limestone occur close together. Merlot therefore often shares the lead with Cabernet Sauvignon more evenly than the familiar image of a wholly Cabernet-dominated Left Bank suggests.

Moulis has no classified growth from 1855. Its identity rests on a mosaic of landscapes and estates that built their reputations outside that historic list. The best wines combine ripe dark fruit with freshness, a firm core and enough suppleness to avoid severity.
:::

:::section{#ligging-en-afbakening depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-overview source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
## Location and boundaries — the western communal Médoc

Moulis lies west of Margaux and southwest of Listrac-Médoc, farther from the Gironde’s moderating influence than the celebrated riverside appellations. Its core is the commune of Moulis-en-Médoc; precisely designated parcels in several neighbouring communes may also carry the origin. Only approved vineyards within that zone qualify.

The legal name may read either “Moulis” or “Moulis-en-Médoc”. These are two forms of the same PDO, not quality levels. Médoc places the wine geographically; it does not make Moulis a cuvée of the broader Médoc appellation.
:::

:::detail{#landinwaarts-is-niet-afgesloten parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
### Inland does not mean isolated

Forest shelters parts of the peninsula from Atlantic winds, while ditches and small streams carry away rain. The Gironde still helps moderate the climate at a distance, but local elevation, soil and airflow matter more visibly here. A parcel boundary may therefore reflect both legal history and a real difference in drainage.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.moulis-winegrowers-history source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — from grain mills to vineyards

In the Middle Ages this part of the Médoc supplied Bordeaux with grain. Its many windmills and watermills are thought to have left their name in *Moulis*, derived from the Latin *molinis*. On poor, stony and naturally drained land, vines later became more attractive than many other crops. Trade through Bordeaux gave the red wines a market beyond the village.

Vineyard area grew in the nineteenth century before oidium, downy mildew and [[concept.phylloxera|phylloxera]] caused the same rupture seen across Bordeaux. Replanting, changes of ownership and economic crises make today’s estates less timeless than their buildings imply. The winegrowers’ syndicate was founded in 1936 and the AOC recognised in 1938.
:::

:::detail{#reputatie-zonder-1855 parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-overview source.moulis-winegrowers-history"}
### Reputation without 1855

The 1855 list concentrated on estates then commanding the strongest prices through the Médoc trade. Moulis was absent, but not commercially invisible. Crus Bourgeois classifications and individual château reputations later supplied other reference points. Its history is therefore not one of “forgotten greatness”, but of status built by a different route.

## Landscape, climate and soils — three soils in one small appellation

Moulis combines Garonne and Pyrenean gravel terraces, clay-bearing ground and limestone outcrops. Grand-Poujeaux is known for gravel ridges; elsewhere clay can hold more water and nutrients. Gentle slopes and jalles help drain excess water. In a maritime climate with rain during the growing season, this water balance matters at least as much as visible stones.

Gravel warms readily and often suits late-ripening [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]]. Clay and limestone-bearing subsoil can give [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] an equal place. Soil does not predict aroma, however: rooting, season, yield and human work all belong to the same [[concept.terroir|terroir]].
:::

:::detail{#water-is-de-verborgen-draad parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
### Water is the hidden thread

Free-draining gravel sheds rain quickly but can feel summer drought early. Clay stores more water but may remain cold and heavy through a wet spring. Producers therefore match variety, rootstock, ground cover and harvest timing to each parcel. Geological diversity is not a collection of flavour formulas, but a series of distinct water and ripening challenges.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
## Grape varieties — Cabernet and Merlot in balance

Cabernet Sauvignon brings blackcurrant, fresh acidity and tannic structure; Merlot ripens earlier and can add plum fruit and roundness. [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] often supplement them. Malbec and Carménère are also permitted but usually minor.

The [[concept.assemblage|blend]] is not an appellation recipe. Each variety’s share follows the parcels, season and intended style. Moulis is a particularly good lesson in why “Left Bank means Cabernet” is a starting point, not a conclusion.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — ripeness without weight

Canopy and fruit zone must balance sunlight, ventilation and protection. At harvest, growers assess sugar and acidity alongside skins, seeds and weather forecasts. Red grapes ferment on their skins; extraction controls the colour and tannin taken up. Malolactic conversion, selection, blending and [[concept.elevage|maturation]] follow.

Barriques are common, but new oak is not legally required. An accessible wine may mature for less time in more neutral vessels; ambitious selections may receive longer élevage and more oak. Ripe fruit and tannin must still support one another.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
## Appellation rules — two names, one red origin

The PDO protects still red wine and defines origin, grapes, planting density, pruning, ripeness, yield, analysis and maturation. It does not prescribe the Cabernet share of every bottle or decide which château makes the finest wine. Moulis and Moulis-en-Médoc are legally equivalent.
:::

:::detail{#regels-als-ondergrens parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
### Rules as a floor

The base yield is 57 hectolitres per hectare and the general ceiling 63. Such figures limit production; they do not measure concentration or finesse. The complete current specification remains the authority for technical decisions.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-overview source.moulis-winegrowers-history"}
## Classification and producers — outside the 1855 list

Moulis has no 1855 classified growth. Crus Bourgeois therefore matter more as historic and current guideposts, but any such status belongs to a particular producer and period, not the whole appellation. [[producer.chateau-chasse-spleen|Château Chasse-Spleen]] and [[producer.chateau-poujeaux|Château Poujeaux]] are useful landmarks; neither defines Moulis alone.
:::

:::detail{#producent-chateau-chasse-spleen parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-overview"}
### Château Chasse-Spleen

Chasse-Spleen is a recognisable reference estate on gravel and clay-limestone ground. Use it as an entry into Moulis’s diversity, not as an official top rank: reputation, Cru Bourgeois status and appellation are separate layers.
:::

:::detail{#producent-chateau-poujeaux parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-overview"}
### Château Poujeaux

Poujeaux is tied to the Grand-Poujeaux gravel ridge and shows how an estate name can make part of a landscape internationally recognisable. It remains an example within Moulis, not its definition.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-moulis-overview source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
## Wine style and bottle development — dark fruit, grip and suppleness

Moulis can combine blackcurrant, plum, cedar, graphite and spice with a firm but usually rounded tannic frame. More Merlot or clay may fill the mid-palate; Cabernet from gravel can provide line and length. Vintage and producer shift that balance considerably.

Simpler bottlings are often appealing for their fruit. More serious wines can develop tobacco, leather and earthy notes in bottle. Ageing potential follows concentration, acidity, tannin, provenance and storage—not the appellation name alone.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025"}
## Labels and buying — read château, vintage and origin separately

Look for “Moulis” or “Moulis-en-Médoc” as the appellation, then read producer, cuvée, [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and bottler separately. Cru Bourgeois is a classification statement, not a synonym for the PDO. For older bottles, storage and fill level may matter more than historic reputation.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-moulis-specification-2021"}
## Contemporary developments — drainage and drought

Warmer years help Cabernet ripen but increase drought stress and the risk that sugar rises faster than aromas and tannin mature. Moisture and fungal pressure remain. Ground cover, organic matter, adapted canopy work and precise picking must therefore be chosen for each soil type.
:::

Moulis-en-Médoc is small but geologically broad: gravel, clay and limestone let Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot build a distinct red Médoc identity beyond the 1855 classification.
