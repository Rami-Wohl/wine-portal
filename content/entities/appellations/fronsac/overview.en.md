Fronsac occupies limestone and clay hills above the confluence of the Isle and Dordogne, across from Libourne. It is the larger of the two appellations in the [[region.fronsadais|Fronsadais]] and protects red wine only.

Merlot is central, but Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon and Malbec help explain how Fronsac can combine generous fruit with a serious tannic frame.
:::

## Location and boundaries — seven communes around the tertre

The appellation covers Fronsac, La Rivière, Saillans, Saint-Aignan, Saint-Germain-de-la-Rivière, Saint-Michel-de-Fronsac and a precisely named set of parcels in Galgon. Only delimited vineyard land within that zone qualifies.

[[appellation.canon-fronsac|Canon-Fronsac]] belongs to the same wider district but is a separate, smaller appellation. It is not a higher class of Fronsac, and an estate with land in both zones must keep each wine's origin distinct.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-overview source.vins-fronsac-vineyard"}
## History — the trading name came before the appellation

High ground beside two rivers gave Fronsac military and commercial importance. Wine could travel through Libourne to larger markets; religious houses and later estates contributed to its agricultural history. The name circulated through aristocratic and trading circles in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

The AOC was recognised on 4 March 1937 as Côtes de Fronsac. Since 1976, the protected name has simply been Fronsac. That change makes old labels historically interesting but does not turn them into members of Côtes de Bordeaux.
:::

:::detail{#oude-faam-nieuwe-lezing parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.vins-fronsac-vineyard"}
### Old fame is context, not rank

Fronsac held a stronger market position in some earlier periods than it did later. This illuminates trading networks and taste history, not a claim that every present wine outranks neighbouring appellations. Producer, parcel and vintage are more useful than a reconstructed prestige order.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024 source.inao-fronsac-overview"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — limestone above, molasse on the slope

Limestone plateaus and slopes on molasse du Fronsadais form the backbone. Clay, sand and gravel can occur lower down. Modern river alluvium immediately beside the water is excluded from the vineyard parcels selected for Fronsac.

Slopes shed water and rivers moderate local temperature extremes. Yet a wet spring remains risky and a dry summer can cause water stress. Soil is not a flavour switch: it interacts with rooting depth, aspect, weather and human management.
:::

:::detail{#erosie-en-water parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024"}
### Good drainage can become too fast

On steep ground, flowing water helps until it carries soil away. Grassed headlands, limits on chemical weed control and a ban on major alteration of relief show how erosion management has become part of the protected origin.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot, Cabernets and Malbec

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] are principal varieties. Carménère and Petit Verdot are accessory. At least eighty per cent of an estate's appellation plantings must be principal grapes.

Merlot generally brings volume and ripe fruit; Cabernet Franc can add perfume and freshness, Cabernet Sauvignon firmness and Malbec colour and dark spice. Their actual role varies by vineyard and [[concept.assemblage|blend]].
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — ripeness without excess

A minimum density of 5,000 vines per hectare and a limit on crop load aim for sufficient leaf area and ripe fruit. Red wine ferments with its skins, after which malolactic conversion must be effectively complete.

Extraction and oak are producer choices, not fixed Fronsac recipes. Balance may be powerful but should preserve freshness and ripe tannin. Required maturation until at least 1 June after harvest gives young wine time to cohere.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Appellation rules — red, dry and matured

Fronsac is for still red wine. The current specification governs parcel boundaries, grapes, pruning, ripeness, analytical limits, yield and maturation. Base yield is 53 hectolitres per hectare; this legal ceiling predicts neither actual yield nor quality. [@source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-overview"}
## Classification and producers — estates without château classes

Fronsac has no official château classification. Family businesses and relatively compact estates predominate, but scale, cellar choices and ambition vary. A familiar name is therefore a producer reference rather than a legal rank.

Compare producers through origin, farming, blend and transparent cuvée information. A future collection can introduce significant estates without inventing its own classification.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-overview source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Wine style and bottle development — dark fruit on a fresh line

Fronsac can combine plum, cherry, blackberry, spice and earthy notes with medium to firm tannin. Younger, gently extracted wines may give pleasure early; more concentrated examples benefit from bottle age and can become savoury.

Serve around 16–18°C and give a young, firm wine air. Ageing potential depends on producer, cuvée and vintage; the appellation alone promises no fixed lifespan.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Labels and buying — Fronsac is the complete name

On modern bottles, “Fronsac” is the protected origin. “Côtes de Fronsac” generally points to the historic name. Canon-Fronsac must be written in full and should not be read as a cru or sublabel of Fronsac.

Then check producer, vintage, bottler and any cuvée. “Grand Vin de Bordeaux” may appear as a broader geographical indication but is not a quality class.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-fronsac-specification-2024"}
## Contemporary developments — slopes under a less predictable climate

Warm years can ease ripening, while drought and sunburn threaten balance. Short, intense rain makes erosion control more urgent. Newer rules on ground cover, treatment records and soil disturbance therefore connect origin more explicitly with landscape care.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation"}
Fronsac is an independent red appellation on limestone and clay hills beside the Isle and Dordogne. Merlot usually leads the blend, but relief, Cabernets, Malbec and producer choices determine how much suppleness, freshness and ageing structure a bottle actually shows.
:::
