Saint-Émilion is one of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]'s great red-wine names, yet it feels far removed from the stately [[region.medoc|Médoc]]. Vines surround a medieval town and spread across limestone slopes, small valleys and an undulating plain. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] usually leads the blend; [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] gives many of the finest wines freshness, fragrance and backbone.

The famous name demands immediate precision. **Saint-Émilion** and **[[appellation.saint-emilion-grand-cru|Saint-Émilion Grand Cru]]** are separate appellations from the same wider vineyard. **Grand Cru Classé** is something else again: a rank in the periodically revised [[classification.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion Classification]]. Keeping those three layers apart makes labels, producers and reputations much easier to read.
:::

## Location and boundaries — a vineyard around the stone town

Saint-Émilion lies on Bordeaux's Right Bank, east of Libourne and north of the Dordogne. The PDO is reserved exclusively for still dry red wine. Its geographical area comprises eight communes around the historic town plus a delimited part of Libourne: nine communes in all. [@source.saint-emilion-specification-2023]

In ordinary speech the name can mean the town, the surrounding vineyard or the appellation. In law, only the delimited origin and its rules count. Not every vine with a view of Saint-Émilion's tower automatically carries the same appellation.
:::

:::detail{#unesco-is-geen-appellationgrens parent="ligging-en-afbakening" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.unesco-saint-emilion-jurisdiction source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### Eight UNESCO communes, nine appellation communes

The UNESCO World Heritage “Jurisdiction of Saint-Emilion” comprises eight communes and 7,847 hectares. It protects a historic cultural landscape of vineyards, villages, monuments and underground quarries. The wine-law area also includes part of Libourne and answers a different question: where may grapes for the PDO come from? [@source.unesco-saint-emilion-jurisdiction]

A future Atlas map must therefore show both boundaries as separate layers. Until the official geometry has been validated, this page offers textual orientation rather than a self-drawn, falsely precise border.
:::

## History — winegrowing as the backbone of a living landscape

The Romans introduced viticulture to this part of Aquitaine; it intensified in the Middle Ages. Saint-Émilion stood on the pilgrimage route to Santiago de Compostela, and churches, monasteries and hospices grew from the eleventh century around a place where winegrowing, faith and trade met. [@source.unesco-saint-emilion-jurisdiction]

Limestone was quarried for buildings and left a network of galleries below ground; vineyards, cellars, villages and slopes became one productive landscape. UNESCO recognised precisely that centuries-old interaction as World Heritage in 1999.
:::

:::detail{#jurade-handel-en-libourne parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023 source.inao-saint-emilion-overview"}
### The Jurade, English privileges and a route outward

Under English rule, Saint-Émilion received the status of a jurisdiction in the twelfth century. The Jurade governed the town and surrounding parishes; privileges and the founding of Libourne's port in 1269 helped wine trade to grow. The Dordogne connected the interior with Bordeaux and more distant markets. [@source.saint-emilion-specification-2023]

The old government disappeared after the French Revolution, but local organisation remained important. France's first winegrowers' union was created here in 1884: a practical instrument for defending the name, origin and shared interests.
:::

:::detail{#van-aoc-naar-twee-appellations parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-overview source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview"}
### From recognition to a double legal system

Saint-Émilion was recognised as an AOC in 1936. A 1954 decree created four appellations on the territory, including categories for classified growths. Since 1984, two appellations have remained: Saint-Émilion and Saint-Émilion Grand Cru. The classification is attached to the latter. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview]

This is not a neat ladder on which every bottle automatically moves up one rung. Appellation and classification are different legal instruments.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.unesco-saint-emilion-jurisdiction source.saint-emilion-terroir source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — plateau, slope and plain

The classic postcard shows the limestone town above the vineyards, but Saint-Émilion is not a single hill. Its landscape includes a limestone plateau, slopes and concave valleys, as well as lower plains towards the Dordogne. Limestone and clay-limestone soils are common; gravel, sand and mixtures occur elsewhere. [@source.unesco-saint-emilion-jurisdiction]

Relief and soil influence drainage, exposure to sun and wind, and rooting depth. Clay can retain water; gravel and sand generally drain faster and warm differently. The result is a mosaic, not a simple two-part division.
:::

:::detail{#plateau-hellingen-en-vlakte parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-terroir source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### Water and ripening connect soil to grape

Merlot ripens relatively early and often suits cooler, moisture-retentive clay. Cabernet Franc may ripen long enough to preserve fragrance and freshness on limestone or warmer, freely draining sites. Later-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon generally needs warmer, drier positions. These are useful patterns, not parcel laws.

Fast drainage may help in wet years; water retention becomes precious in dry summers. Exposure, soil depth, roots, canopy and picking date always interact.
:::

:::detail{#bodem-is-geen-aromarecept parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-terroir"}
### Limestone does not literally taste of limestone

Words such as “mineral” and “chalky” are too easily copied from soil description into tasting note. The stronger causal account is indirect: soil and relief affect water, temperature, vigour and ripening; grape, microbiology and cellar work then shape aroma and flavour.

Likewise, “the côte”, “the plateau” and “the gravel zones” are orientations, not guarantees of one style. Claims about individual parcels require much more precise geological and geographical evidence.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-overview source.saint-emilion-grapes"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot first, Cabernet Franc as co-star

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]] is by far the most planted variety. It can bring ripe red and dark fruit, volume and tannins ranging from supple to firm. [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], locally called *bouchet*, often contributes perfume, freshness and line. [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] plays a smaller average role but can add structure and ageing capacity.

The final [[concept.assemblage|blend]] is not a fixed Saint-Émilion recipe. Some estates rely almost entirely on Merlot; others give Cabernet Franc exceptional weight. Parcel, year and intended style matter more than any prescribed proportion.
:::

:::detail{#merlot-en-cabernet-franc parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-grapes source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### More than softness versus structure

Merlot is often summarised as “soft” and Cabernet Franc as “fresh”. That helps initially, but ripeness and [[concept.extraction|extraction]] can make Merlot powerful, while ripe Cabernet Franc can be refined and silky. The two complement each other because they do not ripen at the same time or favour exactly the same sites.

Cabernet Sauvignon can succeed on warmer gravelly or sandy ground, but later ripening makes it less automatic. Blending therefore spreads vineyard and harvest risk as well as flavours.
:::

:::detail{#de-kleine-wettelijke-familie parent="druivenrassen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### Six permitted names, three visible protagonists

The specification also lists [[grape.carmenere|Carmenère]] and [[grape.malbec|Côt, or Malbec]] as principal varieties. [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] is accessory and may form no more than ten percent of an estate's plantings. [@source.saint-emilion-specification-2023]

Legal permission does not mean a variety is common or appears in every wine. These minor grapes matter chiefly when reading unusual old parcels or blends accurately.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — from ripe skins to controlled structure

Saint-Émilion is made as red wine: skins ferment with the juice and provide colour, aroma and [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. Picking date is decisive. Fruit harvested too early can taste green and lean; picked too late or too warm, it can lose freshness. Producers therefore judge balance by variety and often by parcel.

After fermentation, lots from different grapes, parcels or vessels are tasted and combined. Blending is not a trick that erases origin, but a way of distributing ripeness, aromatic clarity, texture and structure across one wine.
:::

:::detail{#extractie-en-assemblage parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### More extraction does not automatically mean more wine

During fermentation, a maker can submerge the skins or pump juice over the cap. Temperature, duration and movement govern how much colour and tannin is extracted. Ripe, healthy skins allow a different approach from fruit made fragile by heat, rain or uneven ripening.

Blending then seeks coherence. Modern precision — smaller vessels, parcel selection and gentler extraction — expands the choices, but does not guarantee a better wine.
:::

:::detail{#opvoeding-zonder-uniform-recept parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### Oak is a tool, not an appellation style

Many ambitious wines undergo [[concept.elevage|élevage]] in oak barrels. New oak can bring oxygen exchange, structure and toasted or spicy notes; older oak is more neutral. Concrete, large casks and amphorae also occur. The share of new wood and duration are producer choices above legal minima.

A strong oak signature does not prove Grand Cru status, and restrained oak does not imply low ambition. The relevant question is whether élevage brings fruit, tannin and origin into a whole.
:::

:::section{#appellationregels depth="foundation" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023 source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview"}
## Appellation rules — here, Grand Cru is first an origin name

Saint-Émilion protects still dry red wine from its delimited area and regulates varieties, planting, pruning, ripeness, yield, vinification and minimum ageing. Those rules form a floor; they do not prescribe château quality, blend or flavour.

Saint-Émilion Grand Cru is a separate PDO on the same wider territory, with requirements including a lower yield and longer ageing. The words “Grand Cru” alone therefore do not mean that a château is classified. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview]
:::

:::detail{#grand-cru-is-een-aparte-aop parent="appellationregels" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
### Grand Cru and Grand Cru Classé are not interchangeable

A wine may be called Saint-Émilion Grand Cru when it meets that appellation's rules. Only an estate admitted to the applicable classification may use *Grand Cru Classé* or *Premier Grand Cru Classé* for the relevant wine. Appellation and rank require two separate checks.

Other French regions use “grand cru” differently again. Never read the term apart from the complete appellation name and local system.
:::

:::detail{#actuele-drempels parent="appellationregels" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### Density, ripeness, yield and time

The 2023 specification requires at least 5,500 vines per hectare for new planting. The base yield is 53 hectolitres per hectare. Merlot must reach at least 194 grams of sugar per litre of must and the other grapes 180; the wine must reach at least 11% potential natural alcohol. Élevage continues until at least 31 March after harvest. [@source.saint-emilion-specification-2023]

These figures define legal eligibility, not ideal wine. Older plantings may fall under transitional rules, and producers may crop far lower, bottle later or select more strictly.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-producenten depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
## Classification and producers — a ranking that can move

Unlike Bordeaux's nearly immutable 1855 Classification, the Saint-Émilion Classification is periodically revised. It applies to growths within Saint-Émilion Grand Cru and has the levels *Grand Cru Classé* and *Premier Grand Cru Classé*. Rank is not a synonym for every good wine from the area.

The 2022 edition provides the current formal snapshot. Criteria, procedures and results have prompted debate and litigation over the years; anyone quoting a rank should therefore know the edition too. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022]
:::

:::detail{#een-beweeglijke-rangorde parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
### Revision makes the list current — and contested

Periodic assessment allows promotion, demotion, admission or disappearance. That keeps the ranking closer to a changing producer landscape than a permanently frozen list, but also raises the stakes: reputation and commercial value may depend on the result.

The classification entity covers criteria, editions and the precise list. Here the essential insight is that rank attaches to an estate and edition, not to all land or wine in Saint-Émilion.
:::

:::detail{#iconen-en-bredere-werkelijkheid parent="classificatie-en-producenten" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021"}
### Icons can stand outside the current list

[[producer.chateau-cheval-blanc|Château Cheval Blanc]] and [[producer.chateau-ausone|Château Ausone]] are historically among Saint-Émilion's most famous names, but did not apply in 2022. [@source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021] Their absence from that edition does not erase their influence; nor does fame substitute for a formal current rank.

The platform must show both: verifiable classification status by edition and a producer's wider historical significance.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-overview source.saint-emilion-grapes"}
## Wine style and bottle development — ripe fruit in many forms

Young Saint-Émilion may suggest cherry, plum, blackberry and violets, with a palate ranging from juicy and supple to deep, tannic and compact. Merlot often gives the rounded middle; Cabernet Franc can add perfume, freshness and line. Oak may contribute spice, cedar or toast.

This is a range, not one template. An accessible early-drinking Saint-Émilion and a rigorously selected, long-aged wine share an origin name, but not necessarily weight, concentration or lifespan.
:::

:::detail{#geen-enkele-saint-emilion parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-terroir source.saint-emilion-grapes"}
### There is no single Saint-Émilion style

Limestone slopes may produce a different growing pattern from deep clay or warm sandy and gravelly ground. Grape mix, yield, picking date, extraction and oak magnify those differences. [[concept.vintage|Vintage]] adds rain, heat and harvest window.

Tasting notes are therefore more useful when they explain how a wine is built than when they translate a soil type directly into an aroma.
:::

:::detail{#tijd-in-de-fles parent="wijnstijl-en-flesontwikkeling" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-overview"}
### From fruit to forest floor — if structure supports it

With bottle age, fresh fruit may shift towards dried plum, tobacco, cedar, leather, mushroom and forest floor as tannins soften. Concentrated wine with sufficient acidity and ripe tannin can develop for a long time; lighter examples are often more appealing in youth.

Vintage, cork, bottle size and storage all affect the pace. A famous name or classification is no guarantee of longevity for every bottle.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-aankoop depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-overview source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
## Labels and buying — read the whole line, not just “Grand Cru”

Start with the complete appellation: Saint-Émilion or Saint-Émilion Grand Cru? Then find producer, [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]], vintage and bottler. Only the additional correct wording *Grand Cru Classé* or *Premier Grand Cru Classé* indicates a formal classification rank.

For learning and buying, producer is usually more informative than an isolated prestige word. With older bottles, also consider provenance, fill level, leakage and storage.
:::

:::detail{#drie-termen-drie-beloften parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
### The quickest three-step check

1. **Saint-Émilion**: the base PDO for red wine from the delimited area.
2. **Saint-Émilion Grand Cru**: a separate PDO with stricter production requirements.
3. **Grand Cru Classé or Premier Grand Cru Classé**: a rank from a particular classification edition, within Saint-Émilion Grand Cru.

The four satellites — [[appellation.lussac-saint-emilion|Lussac-Saint-Émilion]], [[appellation.montagne-saint-emilion|Montagne-Saint-Émilion]], [[appellation.puisseguin-saint-emilion|Puisseguin-Saint-Émilion]] and [[appellation.saint-georges-saint-emilion|Saint-Georges-Saint-Émilion]] — are independent appellations. A related name does not make them subzones of this PDO.
:::

:::detail{#serveren-en-bewaren parent="etiket-en-aankoop" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-overview"}
### Air and temperature as practical tools

About 16–18 °C is a useful starting point; an overheated room makes alcohol feel heavier and fruit flatter. Young tannic wine may open with decanting, while fragile old wine calls for gentle pouring and attention.

Roasted flavours, mushrooms, lentils and savoury sauces often work alongside dark fruit and tannin. Treat that as a tasting direction rather than a rule: the actual bottle determines how much weight the food needs.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
## Contemporary developments — ripeness is no longer the only goal

Warmer years can make ripe Merlot and Cabernet Franc easier to achieve, but may also put acidity, water and an unhurried harvest window under pressure. The modern question is therefore less often “will the fruit ripen?” and more often “can freshness, healthy skins and balance be retained?”

Producers respond through soil care, adapted canopies, later pruning, more precise harvesting and sometimes a different balance of varieties, extraction and oak. There is no universal solution: what helps on dry gravel may be wrong on moisture-retentive clay.
:::

:::detail{#warmte-water-en-keuzes parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.saint-emilion-specification-2023"}
### Climate adaptation also changes style

Earlier picking may retain freshness but still requires ripe skins; gentler extraction can limit dry tannin but must not leave thin wine. More shade protects fruit in heat, while an overly dense canopy can encourage disease in wet periods.

The appellation is not moving towards one “new style”. It is becoming a collection of more exact local choices, with water, soil life, grape selection and harvest date as increasingly important links.
:::
