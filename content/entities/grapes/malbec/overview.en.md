:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-cot source.wset-malbec"}
Malbec connects two wine worlds. In France's Cahors it belongs to a tradition of dark red wine; in Argentina it became a recognisable face of the country, with Mendoza as its main reference. That journey did not create a fixed flavour recipe. Malbec can be generous and supple, or floral, fresh and firm: place, ripeness and winemaking determine how dark fruit and structure come together.
:::

:::section{#identiteit depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-cot source.ucdavis-malbec-history source.wset-malbec"}
## Identity and origins — French roots, Argentine significance

Malbec is a black wine grape from south-west France. Its official French varietal name is Cot; Côt is a familiar spelling in wine writing. The historical heart of its French story lies at [[appellation.cahors|Cahors]] on the Lot. Dark wine mattered here long before Malbec became a globally recognisable Argentine grape name. [@source.plantgrape-cot] [@source.ucdavis-malbec-history]

In Argentina the variety gained a full leading role, not merely a second life as a Bordeaux blending grape. [[region.mendoza|Mendoza]] is the central reference, alongside other Argentine origins. Learning Malbec only through [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] therefore misses both its main French home and its contemporary international significance. [@source.wset-malbec]
:::

:::detail{#geschiedenis-en-verspreiding parent="identiteit" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.ucdavis-malbec-history"}
### A history of spread, loss and renewed interest

During the nineteenth century, French planting material travelled with viticultural knowledge to Argentina. The Foundation Plant Services historian describes the role of Michel Aimé Pouget and the development of vineyards in western Argentina. Malbec's later international recognition did not arrive automatically with those first plantings: changes in vineyards, cellars and Argentina's wine position also mattered. [@source.ucdavis-malbec-history; Success in Argentina]

Its French history differed by place. [[concept.phylloxera|Phylloxera]], the insect pest attacking vine roots, and the severe frost of 1956 contributed to decline in Bordeaux. Poor fruit set and preferences for other grapes played roles too. Cahors subsequently retained a central place for the variety. A single disaster is therefore not a complete explanation of today's distribution. [@source.ucdavis-malbec-history]
:::

## Regions and appellations — Cahors and Mendoza as two main references

Cahors is the French origin to remember. Malbec can carry firm reds with dark fruit and ageing potential, but “French Malbec” does not necessarily mean rough or impenetrable wine. Ripe grapes and cellar choices also permit more accessible expressions. Local soils, producers and rules belong to the appellation.

Mendoza contains clear differences between warmer vineyards and cooler, higher sites. [[region.lujan-de-cuyo|Luján de Cuyo]] is an important historical reference; the [[region.uco-valley|Uco Valley]] reveals other combinations of elevation, climate and soil. Ripe fruit, flowers and freshness can come forward in different proportions. Learning a place within Mendoza is more useful than treating all Argentine Malbec as one style. [@source.wset-malbec]
:::

:::detail{#argentinie-en-overige-herkomsten parent="regios" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.wines-argentina-varieties-2022 source.wset-malbec source.ucdavis-malbec-history"}
### Argentina is larger than Mendoza; France larger than Cahors

Northern Argentina, including [[region.salta|Salta]], and southern origins in [[region.patagonia|Patagonia]] broaden the picture. Wines of Argentina describes regional differences in colour, aroma and structure. These are regional syntheses, not mandatory results for every bottle. Ripening temperature, water, light and harvest decisions remain important. [@source.wines-argentina-varieties-2022; Malbec, pp. 6–14]

In France, Cot also grows in the [[region.loire-valley|Loire]], while Bordeaux retains a smaller blending role. Outside France and Argentina there are examples in the United States, Australia and New Zealand. Malbec is international, but these contexts receive less space here than its two main references. [@source.ucdavis-malbec-history] [@source.wset-malbec]
:::

## Wine styles and sensory profile — dark fruit is a starting point

Malbec can produce deeply coloured reds with associations of plum, blackberry, other dark fruit and sometimes violets. Structure ranges from supple generosity to firmer wines intended for bottle development. [[concept.tannin|Tannin]], compounds that can make the mouth feel drier, need not feel harsh: ripeness and extraction influence perception. [@source.wset-malbec]

A light floral nose does not exclude concentration, and deep colour does not automatically reveal alcohol or tannin level. Plantgrape also notes potential for rosé. That is a different treatment of a black grape, not a separate pink Malbec cultivar. [@source.plantgrape-cot]

A simple contrast between “supple Argentine” and “stern French” helps only at an introductory level. The actual range within both countries is wider. Producer, vineyard, [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and maturation make a style description useful.
:::

:::section{#klimaat depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-malbec source.wines-argentina-varieties-2022"}
## Climate, site and soils — sunshine and coolness can coexist

Malbec needs adequate ripeness to deliver convincing fruit and appropriate structure. Mendoza can combine abundant sunshine with coolness from elevation and night temperatures. Dry conditions reduce certain fungal risks, but make water availability and distribution especially important. The Cafayate photograph shows one vineyard combining training and drip irrigation; it does not represent all Argentine vineyards. [@source.wset-malbec]

Cahors and other French settings require understanding different combinations of water, temperature and season. A soil name alone does not explain the wine. Soil acts through rooting, water and vigour, among other factors; its interaction with place and human choices gives [[concept.terroir|terroir]] meaning.
:::

:::detail{#hoogte-zonder-smaakformule parent="klimaat" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.wset-malbec source.wines-argentina-varieties-2022"}
### Elevation is a factor, not a quality ladder

Higher vineyards can ripen more coolly, but elevation is not the only variable. Exposure, latitude, wind, water and harvest timing also change conditions. “Higher is better” is therefore not a viticultural rule, and a number of metres should not become a flavour prediction.

Wines of Argentina identifies ripening temperature as important in different aromatic expressions. That supports examining site differences, not the idea that all Malbec has one exclusive aroma molecule or one immutable scent. [@source.wines-argentina-varieties-2022; Malbec and terroir]
:::

:::section{#groeicyclus depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-cot"}
## Vine and growing cycle — flowering can already shape the harvest

Cot is vigorous and susceptible to [[concept.coulure|coulure]], when too few flowers develop into berries after flowering. A season's yield can consequently diverge early from what the number of flowers suggested. French clonal selection has, according to Plantgrape, substantially reduced this susceptibility without making every vine and site identical. [@source.plantgrape-cot]

Plantgrape places budburst four days after Chasselas and maturity roughly two and a half weeks after that reference variety. This comparison is not a worldwide harvest calendar. The decisive question remains when fruit, skins and the intended wine style have developed sufficiently in the actual site. Medium-sized bunches and round berries belong to the described profile.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-cot source.wset-malbec"}
## Viticulture and vulnerabilities — vigour and weather need different responses

Plantgrape notes vulnerability to winter frost during the first years after planting and limited fruitfulness of replacement shoots after spring frost. Ripe fruit can also shed berries. These differ from rot risks: a vineyard may face little fungal pressure yet still lose crop. The source also notes possible susceptibility to phomopsis, a fungal disease affecting shoots among other tissues, and leafhoppers, small sap-feeding insects. [@source.plantgrape-cot]

Mendoza requires attention to hail, spring frost and strong sunlight. Nets can provide protection; canopy management adjusts shade and exposure. Where irrigation is used, water must match growth and availability. Low yield or a bare fruiting zone is not the goal in itself: sound fruit at suitable ripeness is. [@source.wset-malbec]
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-cot source.wset-malbec"}
## Winemaking and ageing — strength does not always call for more extraction

Malbec is often made as a varietal wine, but can also join an [[concept.assemblage|assemblage]], or blend, with grapes such as [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] or [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]]. Blending changes the relationship between fruit, fragrance, [[concept.acidity|acidity]] and structure; Malbec is not automatically there only to add colour. [@source.wset-malbec]

For red wine, [[concept.maceration|skin contact]] brings colour and other compounds into the juice. [[concept.extraction|Extraction]], transferring compounds from skins and seeds, helps shape texture. A maker may choose gentler handling to preserve floral character and drinkability. Abundant colour in the grape does not require the cellar to pursue maximum power.

During [[concept.elevage|élevage]], the care of wine before bottling, barrel ageing can add aroma and structural development; more neutral vessels can foreground fruit differently. New oak is not a condition of good Malbec. Some wines are made for youthful enjoyment, others for further bottle development.
:::

:::section{#verwantschap depth="advanced" source_refs="source.plantgrape-cot source.ucdavis-malbec-history"}
## Synonyms, clones and relationships — Cot, Malbec and a shared parent

Cot and Malbec identify the same variety. Historical names such as Pressac in Bordeaux and Auxerrois in Cahors require context: elsewhere Auxerrois can identify a different white grape. A name is therefore not treated as an identity without knowing its use. [@source.ucdavis-malbec-history]

Genetic research connects Malbec to [[grape.magdeleine-noire-des-charentes|Magdeleine Noire des Charentes]] and [[grape.prunelard|Prunelard]]. The first parent is shared with Merlot. That does not make Malbec a Merlot clone, nor explain every similarity of aroma. [@source.plantgrape-cot] [@source.ucdavis-malbec-history; The origin and parentage of Malbec]

[[concept.clonal-selection|Clonal selection]] concerns variation within a variety. Historical French and Argentine material may differ, but a country is not a clone number or a biological guarantee of small or large berries. Genetic identity and growing behaviour are assessed with multiple kinds of evidence.
:::

:::section{#etiket depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-malbec source.ucdavis-malbec-history"}
## Labels and recognition — read both grape and place

Many Argentine bottles clearly state Malbec. Mendoza or a more specific origin then helps locate the fruit; producer information reveals more about vineyard and cellar choices. In Cahors the appellation name may lead, sometimes alongside Malbec. Cot in a French text therefore need not mean an unfamiliar different grape.

When tasting, colour, dark fruit, floral notes and texture provide clues, not proof. The same applies in vineyards: [[concept.ampelography|ampelography]], the description of grapevines, combines leaves, shoots, bunches and berries. A photograph of one bunch can never show the whole worldwide range.
:::

:::section{#heden depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.wines-argentina-varieties-2022 source.wset-malbec"}
## Contemporary developments — from a recognisable grape to a recognisable place

Malbec's international recognition makes it possible to look beyond the variety alone. Vineyard and origin differences within Argentina, together with different extraction and maturation choices, support a richer story. A familiar grape name can provide the entrance; place then explains why two bottles speak differently. [@source.wines-argentina-varieties-2022] [@source.wset-malbec]

Heat, water availability and harvest risk remain practical considerations even for a successful international variety. This page therefore avoids an undated world total or a permanent growth prediction. The more durable knowledge is what Malbec needs and how makers shape different styles from those conditions.
:::
