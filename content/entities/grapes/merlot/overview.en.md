:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.civb-merlot"}
Merlot is the grape that can give Bordeaux roundness without erasing its sense of place. It ripens earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon, is notably at home on many clay- and limestone-rich soils, and can produce both uncomplicated fruit and monumental, long-lived wine. That broad range made it one of the wine world's major black varieties. [@source.plantgrape-merlot] [@source.civb-merlot]

Its name is often used as a synonym for softness. That is only half the truth. Merlot can be velvety, but also dark, muscular and tannic. The decisive questions are where it grows, how fully it ripens, how much fruit the vine carries, and what the maker does with skins, vessel and time.

## Identity and origins — a Bordeaux variety that found the world

Merlot is a black-berried *Vitis vinifera* variety with its documented origin in the Bordeaux region. There it acquired a leading role in red wine: often the largest component on the Right Bank, elsewhere a partner in a [[concept.assemblage|blend]]. Beyond Bordeaux it also appears under its own name, from simple fruit-driven wine to ambitious cuvées capable of years of ageing. [@source.plantgrape-merlot] [@source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025]

That versatility is not an absence of identity. Merlot couples relatively early ripening with an ability to build colour, alcohol, fruit and texture. It can therefore suit places where Cabernet Sauvignon does not comfortably ripen every year, as well as warm regions where freshness and water set the limits.
:::

:::detail{#van-lokale-naam-naar-wereldras parent="identiteit-en-oorsprong" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009 source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### From local name to global variety

The name Merlot appears in written wine literature only towards the end of the eighteenth century. The variety was described more fully by ampelographers in the nineteenth, while its great international expansion came much later, especially in the second half of the twentieth century. A grape that now seems ubiquitous has a surprisingly short documented career under this name. [@source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009] [@source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025]

Its spread also changed its public image. In Bordeaux, Merlot often remained hidden behind château and appellation. Elsewhere the grape name moved to the front label and “Merlot” itself became a style promise. That promise made the variety approachable, but sometimes flattened its many origins into a single idea of soft red fruit.
:::

:::detail{#genetische-ouders parent="identiteit-en-oorsprong" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009"}
### Two parents, not a flavour formula

DNA research identified [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] and the rare [[grape.magdeleine-noire-des-charentes|Magdeleine Noire des Charentes]] as Merlot's genetic parents. Researchers found the nearly vanished Magdeleine in a handful of places in western France and could thereby fill a missing branch in the family tree of south-western varieties. [@source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009]

Parentage helps distinguish varieties and explain historical relationships. It does not mean that Merlot simply inherits “softness from one parent and structure from the other” in fixed proportions. Growth and wine quality result from many genes, clonal variation, environment and human action. DNA is a family tree, not a tasting note.
:::

:::section{#wijnstok-en-groeicyclus depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot"}
## Vine and growing cycle — quick to start, quick to finish

Merlot buds early and ripens before Cabernet Sauvignon. The vine has medium to high vigour, is quite fertile and readily produces extra shoots. Small to medium, often winged bunches carry round berries of medium size. Vigour and fertility need direction: too much foliage or fruit can cancel the head start in ripening. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

“Early” is always relative to a site and comparison variety. A cool season, heavy crop or shade may delay maturity; a hot season can push sugar ahead of colour, aroma and tannin ripeness. The calendar offers direction, not a harvest date.
:::

:::detail{#herkennen-is-vergelijken parent="wijnstok-en-groeicyclus" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot"}
### Identification means comparison

In Merlot, ampelographers look among other things for a densely hairy young shoot tip, dark-green adult leaves with five or seven lobes, an open U-shaped petiole sinus and a distinctly blistered leaf surface. Bunch shape, round berries and shoot colour add further clues. No single feature decides identity. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

[[concept.ampelography|Ampelography]] compares patterns throughout the season. Young and old leaves differ, disease can change appearance and related varieties may look alike. Reliable planting material therefore depends on provenance, certified identity and DNA where needed, alongside the trained eye.
:::

:::detail{#klonen-bewaren-variatie parent="wijnstok-en-groeicyclus" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot"}
### One variety, hundreds of preserved lines

France recognises several Merlot clones and maintains a Bordeaux conservation collection containing hundreds of selections. [[concept.clonal-selection|Clonal selection]] can preserve differences in yield, bunch form, ripening and health without creating a new grape variety. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

A clone number is not a quality ranking. Rootstock, virus status, propagation, soil, age and management also shape performance. A broad collection matters precisely because the best choice for a damp twentieth-century vineyard is not automatically the best choice for a warmer and drier future season.
:::

## Climate, site and soils — water defines the room to move

Merlot's early cycle helps it reach dependable ripeness in temperate regions, but exposes young growth to spring frost. Later in the season it needs enough warmth without prolonged water shortage. Plantgrape describes severe drought as unfavourable and clay-limestone terroirs as suitable. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

That is not a magical preference for one soil name. Clay can retain water and nutrients; limestone, sand, gravel and soil depth alter drainage, rooting and temperature. The best site does not simply produce “clay flavour”; it holds growth and ripening in a useful balance.
:::

:::detail{#klei-en-water parent="klimaat-ligging-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.civb-merlot"}
### Why clay keeps entering the Merlot story

Clay, limestone and sand occur in different proportions across Bordeaux's Right Bank. Clay can buffer water in dry periods and remain cooler than rapidly warming gravel. This may help Merlot ripen steadily, provided roots can reach sufficient depth and drainage does not become suffocating. [@source.civb-merlot]

Too much water may instead encourage rampant growth, diluted fruit and disease-prone bunches. Too little closes stomata, restricts photosynthesis and can make berries shrivel early. “Merlot likes clay” is therefore only the opening sentence; profile, structure, rain, drainage and rooting space write the rest.
:::

:::detail{#vroeg-is-geen-vrijbrief parent="klimaat-ligging-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025 source.plantgrape-merlot"}
### Early ripening does not mean heat-proof

An earlier harvest can keep Merlot clear of autumn rain. In hot conditions, however, sugar and alcohol may rise faster than acidity, aroma and tannins find balance. High berry temperatures and drought also affect pigments and vine water relations. [@source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025]

Warm sites therefore require more than simply picking sooner. Shade, exposure, soil depth, rootstock, crop load and water availability together decide whether grapes become both ripe and lively. Under extreme heat, the advantage of a short cycle may turn into a narrow picking window.
:::

:::section{#regios-en-appellations depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-merlot source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
## Regions and appellations — the Right Bank as first compass

Merlot grows throughout Bordeaux, but it stands most clearly in front on the Right Bank. [[appellation.pomerol|Pomerol]], [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]] and [[appellation.fronsac|Fronsac]] show that a Merlot-led wine can be round, powerful, layered and long-lived at once. Elsewhere in the region it often softens or fills out Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc. [@source.civb-merlot]

The banks are a useful compass, not a law of nature. The Left Bank also grows Merlot, and the Right Bank also grows Cabernet Franc and other varieties. Appellation, soil, plot and producer say more than one percentage in a blend.
:::

:::detail{#rechteroever-in-detail parent="regios-en-appellations" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-pomerol-overview source.inao-saint-emilion-overview source.inao-fronsac-overview"}
### Three Right Bank roles

Pomerol is small and holds a mosaic of clay, gravel and sand; Merlot can build power, breadth and complexity there. [@source.inao-pomerol-overview] Saint-Émilion is larger and more varied: limestone, clay, gravel and sand do not make one uniform style, and Cabernet Franc may be an important partner. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-overview] On its hills above the Isle and Dordogne, Fronsac presents another distinct interplay of Merlot and the Cabernets. [@source.inao-fronsac-overview]

These differences cannot be read from the grape alone. Merlot is the shared language, but every appellation has its own landscape, rules and producer culture. Treating the “Right Bank” as one flavour misses precisely why origins have names.
:::

:::detail{#buiten-bordeaux parent="regios-en-appellations" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### Beyond Bordeaux: the same grape, a different grammar

Merlot became an international variety with significant plantings in Italy, the United States, Chile, Australia and parts of Eastern Europe. It may be bottled as a varietal wine, work in a Bordeaux-style blend or find a regional role beside local grapes. [@source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025]

North-eastern Italy can be fresh, direct and herbal; warmer Tuscan zones may be riper and firmer. California and Washington together cover an enormous range, as does Chile from coastal influence to warmer inland sites. These are learning anchors, not national stereotypes. Elevation, ocean, soil, water and maker remain more precise than a flag.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijlen-en-smaakprofiel depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
## Wine styles and sensory profile — roundness with more than one face

Merlot generally makes red wine with generous colour, ripe fruit, moderate [[concept.acidity|acidity]] and [[concept.tannin|tannin]] that often feels rounder than Cabernet Sauvignon's. Plum, black cherry, blackberry, redcurrant, violet, cocoa, tobacco and herbs are recognition points, not a compulsory aroma list. Dried fruit and greater alcoholic warmth may move forward in warm or late-picked examples. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

Texture often makes Merlot immediately attractive: the mid-palate feels full and the edges can be soft. Yet low yields, a concentrated site or firm extraction can produce a powerful wine that needs years to open.
:::

:::detail{#soepel-is-geen-synoniem-voor-eenvoudig parent="wijnstijlen-en-smaakprofiel" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### Supple is not a synonym for simple

A fruit-led Merlot may be made with gentle extraction and little oak for early pleasure. A more ambitious example can be deeper in colour, carry more skin tannin, spend longer in barrel and reveal its layers only after bottle rest. Both can be true to the variety; intensity is not a moral ranking.

“Lower acidity” also needs context. Ripeness, climate, yield and any cellar adjustments influence final balance. Merlot may taste warm and broad, but can be fresh and energetic in a cooler origin or at an earlier harvest. The grape defines a playing field, not a final score.
:::

:::detail{#tijd-in-de-fles parent="wijnstijlen-en-smaakprofiel" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### Time moves fruit towards landscape

With bottle age, fresh plum or cherry may turn towards dried fruit, cedar, tobacco, cocoa, leather and forest floor. Tannins can feel finer as compounds rearrange; colour travels from purple and ruby towards garnet. Oak may have added vanilla, toast or spice earlier and is therefore not a grape aroma.

Ageing potential does not come from the name Merlot. Concentration, acidity, ripe tannin, oxygen during maturation, closure and storage together determine the curve. A simple wine may lose its fruit before gaining complexity; a great Pomerol or Saint-Émilion may instead change for decades.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-gevoeligheden depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot"}
## Viticulture and vulnerabilities — fertility needs direction

Merlot combines medium to strong growth with good fertility. Its semi-upright to horizontal shoots require sufficient support, and short pruning may suit it. The grower needs to bring air and light around bunches without leaving berries unprotected in heat. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

Early budding and relatively early ripening do not simply shorten the work. Spring frost threatens young shoots, fruit set may falter in poor flowering weather, and compact or shaded bunches can face more disease pressure when conditions are damp.
:::

:::detail{#bladerdek-opbrengst-en-bloei parent="wijnbouw-en-gevoeligheden" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### Canopy, yield and flowering pull on the same wine

Many shoots and bunches divide the vine's energy. A dense canopy holds moisture and delays ripening; excessive thinning may instead cause sunburn, rapid heating and lost freshness. Pruning, shoot selection, leaf work and possible bunch thinning are therefore connected choices.

Under certain climatic conditions Merlot can suffer [[concept.coulure|coulure]]: not every flower becomes a berry, leaving looser bunches and a lower yield. That is not automatically good or bad. Its cause, the remaining leaf-to-fruit balance and evenness of ripening decide the consequence.
:::

:::detail{#ziekten-vorst-en-droogte parent="wijnbouw-en-gevoeligheden" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### Vulnerabilities rarely arrive alone

Plantgrape describes Merlot as particularly susceptible to downy mildew on inflorescences and bunches, leafhoppers and, to a lesser degree, grey rot. Powdery mildew and certain trunk diseases generally present a smaller varietal risk according to the same sheet. Local pressure, weather, planting material and management remain decisive. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

Frost and drought strike different moments of the same cycle. Early budbreak raises spring exposure; severe drought may later slow photosynthesis and ripening. A measure against one risk can sharpen another: an open bunch zone dries sooner after rain, but also heats more strongly.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-rijping depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
## Winemaking and ageing — texture is made, not found

Red Merlot ferments with its skins. Temperature, time and movement determine how much colour, aroma and tannin enter the wine through [[concept.extraction|extraction]]. [[concept.elevage|Maturation]] in tank, concrete, large wood or small barrels can then round texture and admit oxygen in measured amounts.

Because Merlot can naturally provide a generous mid-palate, excess is a danger. Very ripe fruit, hard extraction and abundant new oak may bury any origin under weight. Gentle handling is not always better either: concentrated grapes may need sufficient structure to avoid becoming shapeless.
:::

:::detail{#solo-of-in-assemblage parent="wijnmaken-en-rijping" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-merlot source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### Solo or in a blend

A wine presented entirely as Merlot may still assemble several plots, picking dates and barrels. In Bordeaux, the grape often works beside Cabernet Franc and [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]]. Merlot may provide ripe fruit and breadth, Cabernet Franc perfume and line, Cabernet Sauvignon tannin and length. [@source.civb-merlot]

[[concept.assemblage|Blending]] is neither a fixed recipe nor necessarily a late quality correction. Each lot is judged for its own ripeness, structure and destination. Earlier Merlot may deserve a larger share in a cool year; in a warm season, Cabernet or a fresher Merlot parcel may guard the balance.
:::

:::detail{#extractie-zuur-en-hout parent="wijnmaken-en-rijping" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### Three controls that can contain breadth

Skin contact builds colour and tannin, but unripe seeds or forceful movement can intensify bitterness and dryness. Acidity and pH govern freshness, colour stability and microbial safety; warm-grown grapes can confront the cellar with sharper decisions here. Wood brings oxygen and aroma, but may readily overshadow soft fruit.

These controls act together. Greater extraction may require longer maturation; much new oak can make tannin feel harder for a time; high pH changes how stable and lively a wine remains. The best approach follows the fruit and intended drinking moment, not a universal Merlot method.
:::

:::section{#synoniemen-klonen-en-verwantschap depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009"}
## Synonyms, clones and relationships — family is not identity

Merlot, Cabernet Franc and Cabernet Sauvignon are related but remain separate varieties. A Merlot clone remains Merlot; a seedling from a cross has a new genetic identity. This distinction prevents similarity in leaf or flavour being mistaken for evidence.

Historical and local names may occur in old texts, while Plantgrape currently lists no officially recognised Merlot synonym in France or other EU countries. A nickname, an official catalogue name and a label claim each have a different status. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]
:::

:::detail{#merlot-blanc-is-geen-kleurmutatie parent="synoniemen-klonen-en-verwantschap" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009"}
### Merlot Blanc is not pale Merlot

The name [[grape.merlot-blanc|Merlot Blanc]] suggests a white colour mutation, as found in some other varieties, but DNA research shows something else. Merlot Blanc is a separate offspring of Merlot and [[grape.folle-blanche|Folle Blanche]]. The shared name reflects historical naming, not one cultivar with two berry-colour variants. [@source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009]

The same research places Merlot in a larger south-western French family containing [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]] through shared parents or close relationships. A family tree can resolve old confusions, but does not predict a shared wine style.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-herkenning depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-merlot source.eu-wine-variety-labelling-2019"}
## Labels and recognition — sometimes the grape is nowhere to be seen

Many international bottles place Merlot prominently on the front. In Bordeaux, château and appellation lead: a Pomerol or Saint-Émilion may be largely Merlot without displaying the variety prominently. The label first tells where the wine comes from and under which rules it was made.

Read in layers: exact origin, producer, [[concept.vintage|vintage]], any grape statement and additional technical information. None of these is a complete tasting note on its own.
:::

:::detail{#druif-of-herkomst parent="etiket-en-herkenning" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.eu-wine-variety-labelling-2019"}
### A grape name need not mean one hundred per cent

Within the European Union, naming a single grape variety generally requires at least 85 per cent of the wine to come from that variety, under the stated conditions and exceptions. A bottle labelled “Merlot” may therefore legally contain a smaller share of other permitted grapes. [@source.eu-wine-variety-labelling-2019]

That rule is jurisdiction-specific and sits beside appellation regulations. A Pomerol without a grape name and a European varietal Merlot make different kinds of statement. The first promises a protected origin; the second foregrounds the grape.
:::

:::detail{#kijken-is-geen-dna-test parent="etiket-en-herkenning" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009"}
### Looking and tasting produce hypotheses

The historical plate above teaches what ampelographers observe, but a photograph or detached leaf does not prove identity. Season, disease and growth alter appearance; members of the Cabernet family may look very similar. Carménère was historically confused with Merlot in Chile, a distinction that genetic work could confirm. [@source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009]

Blind tasting has limits too. Plum, round tannin or moderate acidity may fit Merlot, but are not exclusive to it. Certified planting material, documentation and DNA answer the botanical question; tasting explores what happened in the glass.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025 source.plantgrape-merlot"}
## Contemporary developments — early ripening in a warmer climate

Merlot's early cycle is insurance in cool seasons, but not only an advantage under climate warming. Sugar may rise quickly, acidity may fall and drought may halt ripening. The challenge shifts from “will it ripen?” to “will fruit, tannin, alcohol and freshness remain balanced at the same time?”. [@source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025]

The grape nevertheless remains widely adaptable. Cooler sites may become more dependable while established warm regions need to reconsider vineyard and cellar choices. One global prediction cannot do justice to those opposing movements.
:::

:::detail{#aanpassen-aan-warmte-en-water parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025 source.plantgrape-merlot"}
### Adaptation begins before harvest day

Possible responses include cooler exposure or elevation, different rootstocks and clones, ground cover, shade, adjusted pruning, yield, water management and picking date. No measure works identically everywhere. More foliage may protect against sun but encourage disease and slower ripening in damp conditions.

The cellar can adjust balance but cannot return a shrivelled berry to the vine. Gentle extraction and restrained oak may leave room for ripe fruit; they do not replace healthy grapes with sufficient acidity and ripe tannin.
:::

:::detail{#cijfers-horen-bij-een-jaar parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025"}
### Global fame is not a fixed number

Merlot ranks among the world's most planted black grapes, but area and ranking change. Plantgrape shows how French plantings grew strongly over decades and later shifted; international surveys likewise carry a measurement year and definition. [@source.plantgrape-merlot] [@source.frontiers-merlot-review-2025]

This page therefore keeps the durable explanation: why the variety spread and which possibilities and vulnerabilities accompanied that growth. Current hectares and market rankings belong in dated datasets, not as timeless numbers in the story.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
See Merlot not as a guarantee of softness but as a question of balance. Find fruit and texture, then taste how much acidity and tannin frame the wine and ask what oak or bottle age has added. The difference between supple accessibility and real depth then becomes visible—and each can convince on its own terms.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Merlot's great strength is its mobility. It can ripen earlier, fill out a blend or speak alone, charm young or develop for years. That suppleness belongs to the variety; site, season, planting material and maker determine its final form.
:::

:::section{#verder-ontdekken depth="foundation"}
## Explore further — follow place, family or cellar

Follow Merlot to [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] and then Pomerol, Saint-Émilion or Fronsac to separate its Right Bank roles. Compare it with parent Cabernet Franc, blending partner Cabernet Sauvignon and rare parent Magdeleine Noire des Charentes to understand the family.

To read the plant, continue to ampelography and clonal selection. To analyse the wine, follow blending, extraction, maturation, acidity and tannin. Merlot then ceases to be a loose aroma and becomes a junction between vine, place and glass.
:::
