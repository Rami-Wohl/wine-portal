:::section{#identiteit depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard"}
## Identity and origins — more than a distillation grape

Colombard is a white variety from the wine world of south-western France. Plantgrape classifies it as both a wine grape and a spirits grape. Unlike a variety that only occasionally becomes a by-product, both routes belong to its history. [@source.plantgrape-colombard]

Its identity is not one fixed aroma. The same vine may supply fruit for a lively, stand-alone wine or for a base wine later distilled. What the grower intends to make helps determine when and how to harvest.
:::

## Regions and appellations — between Charentes and Gascony

In the Charentes, Colombard is among the permitted grapes for Cognac, though it plays a smaller role than [[grape.ugni-blanc|Ugni Blanc]]. It is also one of Armagnac's traditional white varieties. The Armagnac trade body notes that Colombard is now used more often for local wine than for Armagnac. These are not contradictory roles: one grape can serve different production chains. [@source.bnic-cognac-grapes] [@source.bnia-armagnac-grapes]

In Gascony and elsewhere in south-western France, Colombard is known as a lively white wine grape. A second important world is South Africa, where the variety is often called Colombar. Wines of South Africa highlights the Breede River region and its fresh, fruity white wines in warmer areas. [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] is not its natural centre of gravity, although it can be used, for example, in [[appellation.cremant-de-bordeaux|Crémant de Bordeaux]]. Such rules are local; they do not define the variety everywhere. [@source.wosa-colombard] [@source.inao-cremant-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::section{#smaak depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard"}
## Wine styles and sensory profile — freshness with aromatic range

Plantgrape describes Colombard as capable of pleasant, aromatic wine with good acidity. In a fresh young wine, expect liveliness rather than a heavy oak-led profile. Citrus, ripe white fruit or a floral note may appear, but none is an identity test. [@source.plantgrape-colombard]

A base wine for distillation is judged differently from a white wine intended for drinking. The eventual spirit's character develops later, through distillation and perhaps time in barrel; tasting notes for the finished spirit cannot simply be assigned to the raw grape juice.
:::

:::detail{#twee-oogstdoelen parent="smaak" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard source.bnic-cognac-grapes"}
### The same acidity, a different purpose

For Colombard intended as a drinking wine, freshness has to sit alongside sufficient fruit flavour. Cognac follows another goal: its trade body describes high acidity and relatively low sugar as desirable for the distillation base. It also values Colombard's aromatic contribution when blended. The variety can serve both routes, then, but the target composition of the harvested grapes and the way the result is judged are not interchangeable. [@source.plantgrape-colombard] [@source.bnic-cognac-grapes]
:::

:::section{#klimaat depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard"}
## Climate, site and soils — keeping freshness

Colombard can bear plenty of fruit. The question is therefore not just whether a site receives enough warmth, but whether yield and picking date suit the intended wine. For a fresh style, the maker wants to retain acidity while flavour develops. Soil and water supply influence growth, but no soil dictates a Colombard aroma by itself.
:::

:::section{#groeicyclus depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard"}
## Vine and growing cycle — a fruitful vine

Plantgrape calls Colombard vigorous, fertile and productive. It describes mid-season ripening, about three weeks after Chasselas in its comparative collection; that is not a fixed harvest date for every region. Ripe berries may detach, making a long wait a practical risk. [@source.plantgrape-colombard]

Its vigour explains why pruning and crop management matter. A substantial yield is not automatically bad for fresh wine or a distillation base, but a purposeful wine requires a purposeful balance between volume and ripeness.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard"}
## Viticulture and vulnerabilities — fruit condition matters

Alongside the risk of ripe berries detaching, Plantgrape notes some susceptibility to powdery mildew on leaves and grey rot in ripe clusters. Monitoring around harvest matters, especially when wet weather meets a wish to leave the fruit hanging longer. [@source.plantgrape-colombard]

Pruning can be short or long according to the production goal. The vine offers options, not an automatic level of quality: clean fruit and a suitable harvest window remain the basis of both routes.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard source.bnic-cognac-grapes"}
## Winemaking and ageing — one grape, two routes

For a fresh white wine the grapes are usually pressed and the juice fermented with attention to clean fruit and retained acidity. Colombard can also be used in [[concept.assemblage|a blend]] with another variety; the proportion changes aroma, texture and tension. Oak or long ageing is possible, but not necessary to understand its fresh appeal.

For Cognac the route begins with a dry white base wine, which is then distilled. That base wine matters, but “delicious as still wine” and “well suited to distillation” are not the same task. Colombard contributes a smaller share than Ugni Blanc in this world. [@source.bnic-cognac-grapes]
:::

:::section{#verwantschap depth="advanced" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard source.wosa-colombard"}
## Synonyms, clones and relationships — names and plant material in context

According to genetic analyses summarised by Plantgrape, Colombard probably arose from a crossing of [[grape.gouais-blanc|Gouais Blanc]] and Chenin. That does not make its taste the average of its parents. The same source lists no officially recognised synonym for propagation material in France or other EU countries. [@source.plantgrape-colombard]

Wines of South Africa writes the name as “Colombar(d)”. That usage in another country does not contradict the French source: one describes a familiar variety name, the other a formally recognised synonym for a particular purpose and jurisdiction. Plant material also varies within a single name. Plantgrape documents French clones with differing yield, sugar and acidity profiles. [[concept.clonal-selection|Clonal selection]] can therefore change a grower's options, but clone figures do not predict how a bottle from another site or vintage will taste. [@source.wosa-colombard] [@source.plantgrape-colombard]
:::

:::section{#etiket depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-colombard"}
## Labels and recognition — the name does not tell the whole use

When Colombard appears on a white wine label, check origin and any blending partners. On a spirit, the origin name tells much more about the production route than the grape alone. The photograph of an identified bunch helps us examine its shape, not predict the taste of its wine.
:::

:::section{#heden depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.bnia-armagnac-grapes"}
## Contemporary developments — a changing role

The shift described by the Armagnac trade body — Colombard being used more often for local wine — shows that a grape's role is not fixed. Its historic distillation role and modern attraction as a fresh white wine can coexist. That is more revealing than calling it only a “Cognac grape” or only an “aromatic grape”. [@source.bnia-armagnac-grapes]
:::
