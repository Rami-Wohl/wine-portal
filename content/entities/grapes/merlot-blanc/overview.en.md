Merlot Blanc sounds like a white version of Merlot, but is a separate variety. The probable crossing of Merlot with Folle Blanche arose in the Bordeaux vineyards. Once much more widely planted, it is now a rare white grape with a modest but distinct place in the region's story.
:::

:::section{#identiteit depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Identity and origins — related, not the same grape

Merlot Blanc is a white wine grape that, according to Plantgrape, arose in the vineyards of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. Genetic evidence points to a crossing of [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] and [[grape.folle-blanche|Folle Blanche]]. It is therefore not simply a white mutation of Merlot, nor the white wine that might be made by gently pressing red Merlot grapes. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

The name makes sense: Merlot is probably one parent. But its character and cultivation need to be understood as those of a distinct variety.

## Regions and appellations — a small Bordeaux chapter

The historical Bordeaux connection matters here. Plantgrape records 5,275 hectares in France in 1958 and only 24 in 2018. These are **dated French vineyard figures**, not a current estimate for Bordeaux or the world. Above all, they show a steep decline. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

Merlot Blanc may play a supporting role in [[appellation.cremant-de-bordeaux|Crémant de Bordeaux]]. That does not mean it appears in every bottle or is widely planted locally. [@source.inao-cremant-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

:::section{#smaak depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Wine styles and sensory profile — no white alter ego of red Merlot

Plantgrape describes the wine as relatively neutral. Do not expect the dark fruit or texture of red Merlot in white form. A maker may use Merlot Blanc for a restrained white wine or as part of a blend, but neither a fixed aroma nor exceptional quality follows from its name. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::

:::section{#klimaat depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Climate, site and soils — water remains a limit

A fertile vine can bear plenty of fruit, but Plantgrape notes susceptibility to drought. A warm, dry site is therefore not automatically attractive. Soil and position matter for water supply and vine health, not as direct predictors of aroma. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::

:::section{#groeicyclus depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Vine and growing cycle — reliable cropping is not enough

Plantgrape calls Merlot Blanc very fertile, productive and regular. In its comparative collection it ripens around three weeks after Chasselas, a relative measure rather than a universal picking date. Clusters are medium-sized and berries small. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

Regular yield is useful, but the amount of fruit must suit the intended wine and the chance of harvesting it in good condition.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Viticulture and vulnerabilities — rot is the main warning

Plantgrape calls Merlot Blanc very susceptible to grey rot. Wet weather near harvest can therefore matter greatly. An airy canopy, inspection and a sensible picking date are more important than assuming a productive vine is always an easy vine. Drought also needs attention. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Winemaking and ageing — a restrained starting point

The grapes are pressed and fermented as white wine. From a neutral base, a maker might choose a fresh, direct style or a blend in which another variety brings more aroma. Oak, long ageing or a highly aromatic profile are not automatic features of Merlot Blanc. Sound fruit matters more to either route than a heavy-handed cellar recipe.
:::

:::section{#verwantschap depth="advanced" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009"}
## Synonyms, clones and relationships — precise about parentage

Plantgrape presents the parentage as a **probable** crossing of Folle Blanche and Merlot. That nuance belongs to genetic reconstruction; it should not be turned into a documented deliberate crossing with a known date and breeder. The profile lists no officially recognised synonym in France or other EU countries. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

The existing study of Merlot and related varieties helps place the family in context, but a similar name is no proof of mutation or identity. [@source.boursiquot-merlot-parentage-2009]
:::

:::section{#etiket depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Labels and recognition — read beyond the family name

Merlot Blanc on a label names a white grape variety, not a colour variant of Merlot wine. Nor does the name reveal its proportion in a possible blend. The identified bunch in the photograph shows the grape, but cannot tell us how the wine will taste.
:::

:::section{#heden depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Contemporary developments — rarity without romanticism

The historical decline in French plantings makes Merlot Blanc a piece of living wine history. Rare does not automatically mean better. Its practical limits — especially rot sensitivity and drought risk — help explain why preserving and using it require a suitable place and clear purpose. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::
