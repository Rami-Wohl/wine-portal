Merlot Blanc sounds like a white version of Merlot, but is a separate variety. The probable crossing of Merlot with Folle Blanche arose in the Bordeaux vineyards. Once much more widely planted, it is now a rare white grape with a modest but distinct place in the region's story.
:::

:::section{#identiteit depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Identity and origins — a distinct white variety

Merlot Blanc is a white wine grape that, according to Plantgrape, arose in the vineyards of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. Genetic evidence points to a crossing of [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] and [[grape.folle-blanche|Folle Blanche]]. It is therefore a distinct white variety with two parents, one of them the black grape Merlot. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

The name makes sense: Merlot is probably one parent. But its character and cultivation need to be understood as those of a distinct variety.

## Regions and appellations — a small Bordeaux chapter

The historical Bordeaux connection matters here. Plantgrape records 5,275 hectares in France in 1958 and only 24 in 2018. These **dated French vineyard figures** chiefly show a steep decline; present-day Bordeaux or global area requires a separate count. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

Merlot Blanc may play a supporting role in [[appellation.cremant-de-bordeaux|Crémant de Bordeaux]]. The precise blend remains a producer choice, and permission says little about the size of local plantings. [@source.inao-cremant-bordeaux-specification-2026]
:::

## Wine styles and sensory profile — restrained white and blending partner

Plantgrape describes the wine as relatively neutral. A maker may use Merlot Blanc for a restrained white wine or as part of a blend. The finished aroma, texture and quality arise from the fruit, blend and cellar approach. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::

:::section{#klimaat depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Climate, site and soils — water remains a limit

A fertile vine can bear plenty of fruit, while Plantgrape also notes susceptibility to drought. Soil and position therefore matter for water supply and vine health; aroma subsequently arises from more factors than site alone. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::

:::section{#groeicyclus depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Vine and growing cycle — yield, water and health

Plantgrape calls Merlot Blanc very fertile, productive and regular. In its comparative collection it ripens around three weeks after Chasselas, a relative measure rather than a universal picking date. Clusters are medium-sized and berries small. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

Regular yield is useful, but the amount of fruit must suit the intended wine and the chance of harvesting it in good condition.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Viticulture and vulnerabilities — rot is the main warning

Plantgrape calls Merlot Blanc very susceptible to grey rot. Wet weather near harvest can therefore matter greatly. An airy canopy, inspection and a sensible picking date are more important than assuming a productive vine is always an easy vine. Drought also needs attention. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

The combination of rot and drought susceptibility requires an approach suited to site and season. Waiting longer may increase the rot risk in wet bunches, while a dry site can stress the vine even before harvest. Sound, usable fruit therefore matters more than yield alone.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Winemaking and ageing — a restrained starting point

The grapes are pressed and fermented as white wine. From a relatively neutral base, a maker might choose a fresh, direct style or a blend in which another variety brings more aroma. Oak and longer ageing are cellar choices that should suit the harvested fruit and intended wine. Plantgrape's restrained assessment offers a general tasting observation; each bottle still deserves individual assessment. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::

:::section{#verwantschap depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Synonyms, clones and relationships — a reconstructed family

Plantgrape presents the parentage as a **probable** crossing of Folle Blanche and Merlot. The source describes a genetically reconstructed family, not a documented deliberate crossing with a known date and breeder. A white mutation of Merlot would be different again: it would not require a second parent to explain a separate variety. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]

The same profile lists no officially recognised synonym for the variety in France or other EU countries and records one certified French clone. That describes formally registered plant material in France; it is not proof that no other Merlot Blanc vines exist elsewhere. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::

:::section{#etiket depth="foundation" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Labels and recognition — read beyond the family name

Merlot Blanc on a label names the white grape variety. Producer information is needed for its proportion in a possible blend. The identified bunch in the photograph shows the grape; place and winemaking help determine how the wine tastes.
:::

:::section{#heden depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc"}
## Contemporary developments — rarity without romanticism

The historical decline in French plantings makes Merlot Blanc a piece of living wine history. Its rarity is historically interesting, while quality belongs to the individual wine. Rot sensitivity and drought risk can make cultivation difficult, but Plantgrape's area figures do not by themselves explain why the variety declined so sharply. Anyone growing it now needs above all a suitable site, sound fruit and a clear winemaking purpose. [@source.plantgrape-merlot-blanc]
:::
