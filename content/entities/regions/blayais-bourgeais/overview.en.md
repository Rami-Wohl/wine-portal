The Blayais and Bourgeais form the northwestern gateway to Bordeaux's Right Bank. Limestone slopes and gently rolling vineyards overlook the Gironde and Dordogne here. Blaye and Bourg were port towns long before their wine names entered appellation rules.

The area is not one AOP. [[appellation.blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux|Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux]] and [[appellation.cotes-de-bourg|Côtes de Bourg]] are legally separate origins with their own boundaries and rules. They share rivers, trading history and a preference for Merlot, but they are not synonyms.
:::

## Overview — between estuary and hills

The Blayais lies around the fortified town of Blaye on the Gironde's Right Bank. Farther south, where Dordogne and Garonne approach their confluence, the Bourgeais surrounds Bourg. Inland, the open riverfront gives way to hills, valleys, woodland and mixed farmland.

The river is boundary, transport route and climatic influence at once. It opens the horizon and moderates extremes, but not all vineyards sit beside the water. Aspect, elevation and distance from the estuary create local differences.
:::

:::detail{#twee-kernen-geen-halve-regio parent="overzicht" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gironde-landscape-blayais-libournais source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
### Two centres, not half a region

“Blaye–Bourg” is a useful tourist and commercial grouping. It should not suggest one uniform wine zone. The Blaye denomination within Côtes de Bordeaux covers a different legal area from Côtes de Bourg, which has fifteen communes and three permitted names.

The regional page supplies shared scenery; the appellation pages supply legal precision. That distinction prevents a landscape term from accidentally being read as labelling entitlement.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## History — wine at the river gateways

Blaye guarded access to Bordeaux; Bourg occupied a strategic place near the confluence. Their ports connected farmers to city merchants and markets across the sea. Limestone quarrying, shipping and viticulture shaped landscape and economy together.

Taxes, trade routes and privileges changed under English and later French rule, but the river remained the artery. Mildews and phylloxera struck these slopes in the nineteenth century. Replanting, cooperation and appellation protection built a new framework in the twentieth.
:::

:::detail{#citadel-en-steengroeven parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gironde-landscape-blayais-libournais source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026"}
### Citadel and quarries tell more than wine history

Blaye's citadel recalls military control over the Gironde. Old limestone quarries show how the subsoil also supplied building material; some galleries later served wine storage or sparkling production.

That heritage makes the area tangible, but not every underground cellar or fortress wall belongs to one appellation. Landscape history and wine law overlap without coinciding.
:::

## Landscape, climate and soils — limestone as backbone, not uniform

Limestone forms many plateaus and slopes, often covered by clay. Sand, silt and gravelly deposits occur elsewhere. River valleys cut through the relief and reveal differences in drainage, aspect and frost risk.

The maritime climate is mild, but rain and humidity demand open canopies and precise disease management. Clay can retain water in dry summers; slopes drain faster. Such properties guide grape choice and growth, not one aroma directly.
:::

:::detail{#bourg-heeft-een-eigen-mozaiek parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
### Bourg has its own mosaic

The Côtes de Bourg specification distinguishes red Quaternary silts on plateaus, sandy clay with gravel on slopes and widespread clay-limestone soils. An ancient river delta near Pugnac and the Brouillon and Moron valleys add relief.

This local language is more useful than calling Bourg a copy of Saint-Émilion. Limestone and Merlot occur in both, but geography, history and appellation rules are independent.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijlen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux source.cotes-de-bourg-appellation"}
## Wine styles — red on stage, white in the wings

Red defines the public face, from juicy Merlot-led cuvées to darker, longer-matured blends. Dry white is far smaller but can join citrus and flowers to gentle texture. The regional name predicts no weight: appellation, producer and cuvée provide focus.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Grape varieties — Merlot leads, but not alone

[[grape.merlot|Merlot]] is the dominant red language, joined by [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] and [[grape.malbec|Malbec]]. Malbec has a notably local place in Bourg. Blends range from juicy and supple to dark and firm.

There is also dry white wine, generally based on [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]]. White is far less visible than red, but neither historically nor legally a footnote.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — working with slope and humidity

Wet years demand open canopies and precise disease management; dry years make water buffering and shade more important. On slopes, soil management must slow erosion and manage competition for water. Red lots ferment with skins; white grapes proceed without skins after pressing.

Producers choose tank, concrete or wood and combine sites and grapes through [[concept.assemblage|blending]]. Those choices can express or obscure regional differences; Blaye and Bourg share no single cellar formula.
:::

:::section{#appellations depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Appellations — read the complete name

Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux is a geographical denomination within [[appellation.cotes-de-bordeaux|Côtes de Bordeaux]] AOP and makes red and dry white. Côtes de Bourg is a separate AOP with the permitted names Bourg, Côtes de Bourg and Bourgeais, also for red and white.

The two routes share regional context but not the same labelling entitlement. The complete name keeps tourist grouping and appellation structure apart.
:::

:::section{#classificaties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Classifications — no current château hierarchy

Neither appellation has a current official château classification. Producer, cuvée and vintage matter more than an imagined rank. Historical commercial rankings from Bourg document former reputation but are no certification of present wine.
:::

:::section{#handel-en-instituties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## Trade and institutions — ports, estates and cooperation

Blaye and Bourg owed much of their wine visibility to river trade. Family estates, larger businesses and cooperatives coexist today; syndicates defend appellation interests and organise joint promotion. Representative producers belong in compact appellation guides, not an endless series of regional pages.
:::

:::section{#etiketten-en-begrippen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Labels and terminology — regional language is not a bottle name

“Blayais and Bourgeais” names the broader region. Precise names govern labels: Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux on one side, Côtes de Bourg, Bourg or Bourgeais on the other. The shared word *Côtes* does not make these wines one AOP.
:::

:::section{#in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux source.cotes-de-bourg-appellation"}
## In the glass — a human-scale Right Bank

Red often combines plum, blackberry and spice with round to firm tannin. Simpler cuvées may give young pleasure; more ambitious selection and cask maturation bring depth and ageing potential. White is usually fresh, citrus-led and floral, sometimes with added Sémillon texture.

Small towns, viewpoints and short distances make the area inviting to visit. Do not taste by château appearance alone: cooperatives and modest family estates are central to the region.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-cotes-bordeaux-specification-2026 source.inao-cotes-bourg-specification-2023"}
## Contemporary developments — visibility and resilience

The region sits outside Bordeaux's most famous price hierarchies. That can offer value, but demands a clear identity: not “the cheaper Right Bank”, but a landscape with its own ports, limestone slopes, grape choices and producers.

Climate change intensifies drought, heat and extreme rain. Ground cover, water buffering, disease management and suitable grapes must be weighed parcel by parcel. The future is not one new formula but better local precision.
:::
