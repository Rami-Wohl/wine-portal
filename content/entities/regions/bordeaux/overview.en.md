Bordeaux is not one wine with one flavour, but an entire wine country gathered around a port city. Here the Garonne and Dordogne meet to form the Gironde estuary; around their waters lie thousands of vineyards, dozens of protected names and styles ranging from structured red to luminous sweet white and lively sparkling wine.

The best way into Bordeaux is therefore not a list of famous estates. Begin with its landscape, history and the names on a label. Riverbanks, trade, grapes, growers and rules then fall into place as parts of one of the world's most influential wine regions. [@source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas] [@source.lavaud-bordeaux-vineyard-landscape-2014]

## Overview — a wine country, not a single wine

The name Bordeaux works at several scales. It can mean the full wine region around the city, but also [[appellation.bordeaux|Bordeaux AOP]], one broad protected designation inside that region. More precise appellations, subregions and individual wines sit beneath and beside it. These layers resemble a geographical family tree, not a simple ladder from ordinary to superior.

Water provides the clearest first orientation. The Garonne approaches the city from the south and the Dordogne from the east. After they meet, the Gironde flows north to the Atlantic. Around these waters lie the [[region.medoc|Médoc]], [[region.graves-sauternais|Graves and Sauternais]], [[region.entre-deux-mers|Entre-deux-Mers]], the [[region.libournais|Libournais]] and the [[region.blayais-bourgeais|Blayais and Bourgeais]]. They are useful landscape families, not five uniform legal compartments. [@source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas] [@source.civb-bordeaux-designations]
:::

:::detail{#identiteit-wettelijke-lagen parent="identiteit-en-schaal" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-aop-overview source.civb-bordeaux-designations"}
### From region to protected name

A [[concept.aop|protected designation of origin]], known in French as an AOP, protects a geographical name and connects it to a delimited area and production rules. On this page, *appellation* means such a protected designation. A wine grown in Bordeaux does not automatically carry the most precise name available for its location: it must also meet that appellation's rules. [@source.inao-aop-overview]

“Left Bank” and “Right Bank” are convenient expressions, but left or right of which water? Usually they contrast the Médoc and Graves west of the Garonne and Gironde with the Libournais east of the Dordogne. Entre-deux-Mers lies literally between the two rivers. Tributaries, hills and transition zones complicate the picture, so the bank model is a compass rather than a complete map.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis-in-beweging depth="foundation" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.lavaud-bordeaux-vineyard-landscape-2014"}
## History — a region shaped by movement

Winegrowing in Aquitaine reaches back to Roman times, but recognisable Bordeaux emerged above all because vineyards and trading city continually shaped one another. Ships carried barrels to foreign markets; demand in those markets influenced what was made; ownership, capital and technique then altered the landscape. Bordeaux was never a timeless terroir that happened to become famous. It repeatedly adapted to new connections. [@source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history]

The broad arc runs from medieval English trade through Dutch influence and new vineyard development to nineteenth-century diseases and twentieth-century protection of origin. Each period left something behind: trade routes, tastes, drained land, estate structures, classifications and rules. [@source.lavaud-bordeaux-vineyard-landscape-2014]
:::

:::detail{#geschiedenis-engeland-en-haven parent="geschiedenis-in-beweging" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.boutoulle-bordelais-wine-trade-2000"}
### England and the port

The marriage of Eleanor of Aquitaine to the future Henry II of England in 1152 placed Aquitaine in a long political and commercial relationship with the English crown. Exports of Bordeaux wine to England expanded especially strongly from the thirteenth century. The often young, pale *claret* of that period was not simply today's red Bordeaux, yet the trade made port and hinterland durably dependent on each other. [@source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history]

Urban merchants, privileges and shipping rules affected who could sell wine and when. Bordeaux history is therefore about power and market access as much as grapes and vineyards. Producers nearer the city could hold advantages over those farther upriver. The port was not scenery; it was an engine of selection and growth. [@source.boutoulle-bordelais-wine-trade-2000]
:::

:::detail{#geschiedenis-water-en-medoc parent="geschiedenis-in-beweging" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.medoc-marsh-drainage-1957"}
### The Dutch, water and the Médoc

Dutch merchants became increasingly important from the late sixteenth and particularly the seventeenth century. They sought wines suited to transport and encouraged new products and commercial practices. Their influence also reached into the landscape. In the low-lying Médoc, marshy and waterlogged ground had to be drained before some later-famous vineyard land could be used reliably.

Dutch entrepreneurs and engineers played a documented part in draining areas of the Bas-Médoc. [@source.medoc-marsh-drainage-1957] They did not single-handedly “invent the Médoc”: local landowners, investors, labourers and continued maintenance also mattered. The episode instead shows that [[concept.terroir|terroir]] — the interplay of place, nature and people — has a human history. Ditches, embankments and property decisions changed which ground could carry vines.
:::

:::detail{#geschiedenis-handel-en-slavernij parent="geschiedenis-in-beweging" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.musee-aquitaine-bordeaux-slavery source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
### Prosperity and its shadow

Bordeaux flourished as an Atlantic trading city in the eighteenth century. That prosperity cannot be described honestly without colonial commerce and slavery. Bordeaux ships, merchants and families participated in the colonial system and the trade in enslaved people; profits and networks contributed to private wealth and urban development. [@source.musee-aquitaine-bordeaux-slavery]

Not every wine estate was financed directly by the slave trade, and economic connections varied by family and period. Even so, a celebratory story of port, château and world market is incomplete if it removes this setting. Bordeaux's international reputation developed in the same Atlantic world as sugar, coffee, colonial goods and forced labour.
:::

:::detail{#geschiedenis-crises-en-herstel parent="geschiedenis-in-beweging" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-aoc-history source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
### Disease, fraud and new rules

The nineteenth century brought prosperity but also powdery mildew, downy mildew and eventually [[concept.phylloxera|phylloxera]]. This North American root louse devastated European vines; replanting on resistant American [[concept.rootstock|rootstocks]] transformed the vineyard. Fraud and misleading origin names continued to damage trust afterwards.

These crises helped drive an increasingly strict system of delimitation and control. A 1935 decree established the institutional basis of France's controlled-appellation system, predecessor of today's AOP structure. [@source.inao-aoc-history] Origin became not merely reputation or trade custom, but a legally described combination of place, grapes and production conditions.
:::

:::section{#landschap-van-water depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas source.lavaud-bordeaux-vineyard-landscape-2014"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — water draws the map

The sea is often invisible from the vineyards, yet its influence is everywhere. The Atlantic gives Bordeaux a temperate, humid climate. The Garonne, Dordogne and Gironde cut through the region and deposited gravel, sand, clay and silt over long periods. The estuary is at once drainage channel, tidal landscape and historic shipping route. [@source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas]

Relief tends to be gentle rather than dramatic: terraces, low plateaux, slopes and valleys. Small changes in height can still affect drainage, sunlight and frost exposure. Bordeaux is best imagined as a finely folded river landscape, not one flat plain. [@source.lavaud-bordeaux-vineyard-landscape-2014]
:::

:::detail{#landschap-klimaat parent="landschap-van-water" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas"}
### An oceanic climate with local differences

The Atlantic moderates temperature extremes but also brings rain and variability. Wet years increase fungal pressure; poor weather during flowering can disrupt fruit set; rain near harvest can decide whether a grower waits or picks. Warm, dry periods create a different challenge for water supply and ripening.

Forests towards the coast provide shelter, large bodies of water can soften local temperatures, and slope or aspect changes daily exposure to the sun. “Maritime” is the background, not a full weather report for every vineyard.
:::

:::detail{#landschap-bodem-en-drainage parent="landschap-van-water" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022 source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas"}
### Gravel, clay, limestone — and everything between

The familiar rule says gravel on the Left Bank, clay and limestone on the Right. It is useful for first orientation but erases too much. River terraces contain changing mixtures of gravel, pebbles, sand and fine material; plateaux and slopes may expose limestone-rich rock and clay; contrasting profiles can occur within a single appellation. [@source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022]

More important than one ingredient's name is how the soil functions as a whole. Does it retain water or drain quickly? Does it warm early, how deeply can roots grow, and how does it respond in a wet or dry year? Gravel can aid drainage, clay can retain water and limestone affects structure and rooting, but no soil translates directly into one aroma or quality level.
:::

:::section{#wijnfamilies depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025"}
## Wine styles — Bordeaux comes in more than one colour

Red wine accounts for much of the production and international image, but the region makes far more. Dry white can be brisk and immediate or broad and barrel-matured. Sweet white ranges from gently sweet to intense and ageworthy. Bordeaux also produces rosé, the historically deeper-coloured [[concept.clairet|clairet]], and sparkling [[appellation.cremant-de-bordeaux|Crémant de Bordeaux]]. [@source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles]

Even “red Bordeaux” is not one style. A supple, fruit-led regional bottle made for early drinking and a concentrated wine from a small appellation may share the regional name while differing in grapes, yield, selection, cellar work, price and purpose. Wine family is the first branch, not the final answer.
:::

:::detail{#wijnfamilies-productie parent="wijnfamilies" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025 source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles"}
### Each family requires different choices

Red wine must normally undergo [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]] with grape skins. As yeast turns sugar into alcohol, colour and [[concept.tannin|tannin]], the drying compounds found in skins and seeds, can be extracted. For white wine, juice is generally separated from skins earlier; pressing and temperature choices focus differently on freshness, texture and aroma. Rosé and clairet receive shorter skin contact than red. Crémant gains its bubbles through a second fermentation in bottle.

Sweet Bordeaux is not merely dry white with residual sugar. Ripe grapes, often concentrated by noble rot, are gathered in several selective passes and yield little, sugar-rich juice. Each appellation's specification determines what may be made under its name. The broad Bordeaux AOP has its own permitted colours and conditions, which do not automatically apply to every other Bordeaux appellation. [@source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025]
:::

:::section{#druiven-en-rollen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties"}
## Grape varieties — grapes as an ensemble

For red wine, [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] play the leading roles. Merlot generally ripens earlier and can bring roundness and plum-like fruit. Cabernet Sauvignon ripens later, naturally carries more tannin and acidity, and can provide structure. Cabernet Franc can contribute perfume, freshness and fine herbal notes. These are directions, not fixed flavour controls. [@source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties]

The best-known white trio is [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]]. Sauvignon can provide tension and vivid aroma; Sémillon often brings texture and matters in dry as well as sweet wine; Muscadelle can add a floral note. Their effect changes with site, ripeness, winemaking and proportion.
:::

:::detail{#druiven-keuzes-en-minderheden parent="druiven-en-rollen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025"}
### Minority grapes and a changing climate

[[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]], [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]] also belong to red Bordeaux. Their planted area or share of a blend is usually smaller, but a modest proportion can still be perceptible. Permitted varieties differ between appellations, and legal permission does not imply extensive planting.

Under limited conditions, the broad Bordeaux AOP also permits a small group of adaptation varieties. [@source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025] That does not make them the new standard. They are one controlled response to warmer, drier conditions, alongside later pruning, canopy management, suitable rootstocks and shifts in harvest timing.
:::

:::section{#van-wijngaard-tot-kelder depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-blending source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — the maker writes part of the story

An appellation sets outer limits, but many choices remain within them. In the vineyard these include variety, rootstock, planting density, pruning, soil management, disease control, yield and harvest date. Selection, pressing, fermentation, [[concept.extraction|extraction]] — how much colour, tannin and flavour is drawn from solid grape material — composition and maturation follow in the cellar. Two neighbours can consequently make recognisably different wines.

There is no single “Bordeaux recipe”. Some wines aim for direct fruit and early drinkability; others receive long [[concept.maceration|maceration]], strict selection and are built to develop for years. Appellation, vintage, scale and ambition frame the possibilities, while the producer sets the emphasis.
:::

:::detail{#assemblage-en-elevage parent="van-wijngaard-tot-kelder" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-blending-code source.civb-bordeaux-blending source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025"}
### Blending and maturation

Bringing separate lots together into a finished composition is called [[concept.assemblage|blending]]. The lots may differ by grape, parcel, picking date or vessel; a blend therefore does not have to contain more than one variety. The winemaker looks for coherence, balance and a style suited to the wine and year. [@source.oiv-blending-code] [@source.civb-bordeaux-blending]

Afterwards — and partly before — comes [[concept.elevage|maturation]], the period between fermentation and bottling in which the young wine is raised. It may take place in tank, barrel, amphora or a combination. New oak can affect aroma, tannin and oxygen exchange, but it is neither compulsory Bordeaux branding nor automatic proof of quality. Time, racking, clarification and vessel choice belong to the same process.
:::

:::detail{#zoete-wijn-en-botrytis parent="van-wijngaard-tot-kelder" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025"}
### When rot becomes noble

In Sauternes and its surroundings, morning mist followed by drier afternoons can favour [[concept.botrytis|botrytis, or noble rot]]. The fungus makes berry skins porous so water evaporates and sugar, acidity and flavour become concentrated. Only sound, beneficially affected grapes are wanted; destructive grey rot in persistently wet weather is different.

Pickers may therefore pass through the same vineyard several times, selecting suitable berries or parts of bunches. The process yields little juice, demands much labour and creates wine whose sweetness needs acidity and aromatic complexity for balance. [[appellation.sauternes|Sauternes]] and [[appellation.barsac|Barsac]] are the most famous names, but sweet wine has a wider Bordeaux history. Its development was linked to local conditions and foreign demand alike. [@source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history]
:::

:::section{#appellations-als-navigatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-aop-overview source.civb-bordeaux-designations"}
## Appellations — the region's contents page

An appellation states where a wine comes from under a particular production specification. Broad names such as [[appellation.bordeaux|Bordeaux AOP]], [[appellation.bordeaux-superieur|Bordeaux Supérieur]] and Crémant de Bordeaux can come from large parts of the region. Other appellations delimit a subregion, commune or group of communes and set their own conditions for matters such as grapes, yields and wine styles. [@source.inao-aop-overview]

A more precise name is not an automatic promise that every bottle tastes better. It principally provides more specific origin and rules. Producer, parcel, vintage and execution still matter. Use appellations for navigation: establish area and wine family first, then compare.
:::

:::detail{#appellationfamilies parent="appellations-als-navigatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-designations"}
### Five routes through Bordeaux

- In the [[region.medoc|Médoc]], broad [[appellation.medoc|Médoc AOP]] and [[appellation.haut-medoc|Haut-Médoc]] sit alongside communal names such as [[appellation.margaux|Margaux]], [[appellation.saint-julien|Saint-Julien]], [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]] and [[appellation.saint-estephe|Saint-Estèphe]].
- South of the city, [[appellation.graves|Graves]] and [[appellation.pessac-leognan|Pessac-Léognan]] lead towards dry red and white wine; farther on, Sauternes and Barsac form a heartland for sweet white.
- The land between Garonne and Dordogne is large and mixed. [[appellation.entre-deux-mers|Entre-deux-Mers AOP]] itself chiefly names dry white wine, although many other styles are made in the geographical area.
- Around the Dordogne lie [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]], [[appellation.pomerol|Pomerol]] and [[appellation.fronsac|Fronsac]], surrounded by further satellite and regional names.
- On the estuary's northeastern side are [[appellation.blaye-cotes-de-bordeaux|Blaye Côtes de Bordeaux]] and [[appellation.cotes-de-bourg|Côtes de Bourg]], among others.

These routes are a learning aid, not an official five-part hierarchy. [@source.civb-bordeaux-designations]
:::

:::detail{#appellations-en-regels parent="appellations-als-navigatie" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-aop-overview source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025"}
### The name comes with a specification

Every AOP rests on a production specification. It describes the area, permitted grapes, growing and production rules, analytical limits and sometimes provisions for maturation or presentation. Rules have developed historically and can change. “Permitted in Bordeaux” is therefore usually incomplete: does it mean the region, the broad AOP or another appellation?

Similar-looking terms can also work differently in law. A geographical district may contain several appellations, while one appellation may permit only certain colours. Entre-deux-Mers AOP is a useful example. For an exact production claim, consult the current specification of the particular appellation rather than a regional rule of thumb.
:::

:::section{#classificaties-zijn-meervoud depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-classifications source.inao-aop-overview"}
## Classifications — not the same as origin

An appellation does not rank quality; it protects an origin and its rules. A classification or recognition system selects particular estates, wines or *crus* within its own scope. A [[concept.cru|cru]] literally refers to a growth or growing place, but the term's precise meaning depends on region and system. “Grand cru” therefore does not mean the same thing everywhere.

Bordeaux has several systems that cannot be stacked into one ranking. They differ in date, geography, criteria, revision and whether the estate, vineyard or wine is central. A classification can illuminate history and the structure of reputation, but cannot guarantee how one bottle performs in a given vintage. [@source.civb-bordeaux-classifications]
:::

:::detail{#classificatiesystemen parent="classificaties-zijn-meervoud" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-classifications source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
### Five systems to keep separate

- The [[classification.bordeaux-1855|Bordeaux 1855 classification]] ranked a limited group of red crus, almost entirely from the Médoc, and sweet white crus from Sauternes and Barsac. It does not encompass all Bordeaux wine. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]
- The [[classification.graves|Graves Classification]] covers selected red and/or white crus in the area now known as Pessac-Léognan.
- The [[classification.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion Classification]] has its own procedure and is periodically revised; the 2022 edition was established through a formal INAO process. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022]
- [[classification.crus-bourgeois-du-medoc|Crus Bourgeois du Médoc]] is a separate classification and recognition framework for participating Médoc estates outside the 1855 system.
- [[classification.crus-artisans-du-medoc|Crus Artisans du Médoc]] is another distinct historic category and recognition.

Pomerol, for example, has no parallel official classification of all its famous estates. Absence from a list is not the same as absence of reputation.
:::

:::detail{#classificaties-veranderen parent="classificaties-zijn-meervoud" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-classifications source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
### Fixed, revised or recognised anew

A classification's strength and weakness follow from its design. A historic list may preserve an exceptionally clear view of market order when it was created, but reflect later changes in ownership, vineyards and performance only imperfectly. A periodically revised system can update, yet invites debate about criteria, procedure and continuity.

Every rank term therefore needs a second question: under which system, for what object and from which date? *Classé*, *grand cru classé*, *cru bourgeois* and *cru artisan* are not interchangeable compliments. Each derives its meaning from its own rules.
:::

:::section{#handel-en-place depth="foundation" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade"}
## Trade and institutions — wine travels through a network

Bordeaux is a production region and a trading centre. For centuries, growers supplied merchants who selected and stored lots, sometimes blended or bottled them, and moved wine to distant customers. Many estates now bottle their own wine, yet specialist merchants remain an important bridge to importers and markets worldwide. [@source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade]

This network is often called the [[concept.place-de-bordeaux|Place de Bordeaux]]. It is neither a physical exchange nor one company, but a commercial system in which producers, intermediaries and [[concept.negociant|négociants]] have distinct roles. Not every Bordeaux wine is sold in the same way or through this network.
:::

:::detail{#negociants-en-primeur parent="handel-en-place" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade"}
### Négociants, brokers and sales before bottling

A négociant is a wine merchant who aggregates market access, handles distribution and carries commercial risk. A [[concept.courtier|courtier]] is a broker who helps arrange contact and transactions between château and négociant. Neither role maps neatly onto that of an importer or retailer.

In [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]] sales, young wine is offered while still maturing in the cellar and delivered later. Buyers assess samples and purchase a future delivery. The system can provide earlier cash flow to producers and allocations to merchants, but leaves the buyer uncertainty about development, price and the final bottle. It is most visible in part of the prestige market, not a universal sales method for Bordeaux. [@source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade]
:::

:::detail{#wereldmarkt-en-reputatie parent="handel-en-place" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade"}
### How reputation is made and transmitted

Quality alone does not explain why some names became globally familiar. Access to capital, trading contacts, classification, reliable distribution, criticism and collecting culture reinforced each other. Established reputation could fund vineyard and cellar investment; that investment could in turn strengthen reputation.

Market price is therefore not meaningless, but neither is it identical to intrinsic quality. Beyond collectible wines, Bordeaux contains a much larger landscape of affordable regional and local bottles. Looking only at famous labels hides the region's economic breadth and stylistic diversity.
:::

:::section{#etiket-lezen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025"}
## Labels and terminology — read the bottle from large to small

Begin with the exact appellation: does the label say only Bordeaux, or a name such as Pessac-Léognan, Saint-Émilion or Barsac? Then identify colour or wine type, producer or [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]], bottler and [[concept.vintage|vintage]]. Alcohol, volume and country of origin also belong to formal label information. Grape varieties are not always displayed prominently. [@source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025]

This order stops one striking word from carrying all the meaning. Precise origin tells you which rules apply; producer identifies who made the choices; vintage locates the wine in a growing season. A classification term becomes useful only when you know its system.
:::

:::detail{#etiket-naam-en-rang parent="etiket-lezen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.inao-aop-overview"}
### Château, cru and rank words

A [[concept.chateau|château as a wine term]] may identify an estate or brand; it need not have a fairytale castle. The château name is not automatically the appellation. [[concept.estate-bottling|Mis en bouteille au château]] tells you where the wine was bottled, not that it is classified.

*Cru*, *grand cru* and *classé* also demand context. Saint-Émilion Grand Cru is an appellation with its own rules, while *Grand Cru Classé* can indicate a classification level there. Médoc terminology works differently again. Never read these words alone: find the complete phrase and the system behind it.
:::

:::section{#bordeaux-in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties"}
## In the glass — what might you expect?

Red Bordeaux often moves among dark or red fruit, savoury notes, fresh acidity and tannin, yet may be supple and juicy or firm and reserved. Dry white can be citrus-led, herbal and taut, or riper, creamier and supported by oak. Sweet white combines ripe and dried fruit, honeyed notes and spice, ideally with enough [[concept.acidity|acidity]] to remain vivid. Rosé and clairet emphasise direct red fruit; Crémant freshness and mousse. [@source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles]

These are families of possibility, not tasting notes written in advance. Age, temperature, bottle development and serving moment also change what appears in the glass.
:::

:::detail{#stijlpatronen-en-grenzen parent="bordeaux-in-het-glas" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022"}
### Style has more than one voice

Merlot can provide softness, but a Merlot-led wine need not be soft. Cabernet Sauvignon can bring structure, but does not behave identically on every gravel soil. Sauvignon Blanc aroma depends on picking and cellar choices. Grape, soil, microclimate, yield, extraction, blending and maturation speak at the same time.

Bank rules are therefore useful opening hypotheses. The Médoc is associated with Cabernet Sauvignon and the Libournais with Merlot, but soils and plantings vary within both. [@source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022] Use the pattern to ask better questions, not to explain an untasted bottle.
:::

:::detail{#jaargang-en-maker parent="bordeaux-in-het-glas" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas source.civb-bordeaux-blending"}
### Vintage and maker shift the balance

Because Bordeaux has a variable oceanic climate, the growing season can strongly affect ripeness, acidity, tannin, disease pressure and yield. *Vintage* is not a regional report card, however: early and late sites, red and white grapes and different harvest decisions do not respond identically.

A producer can select, pick earlier or later, extract more or less firmly and adjust the blend to the year. “Good vintage” without appellation, wine type and producer is consequently too broad. Comparing one wine across years, or several producers within one year, is often more revealing.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#etiket-als-startpunt depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025"}
Treat “Bordeaux” on a bottle as the beginning of an investigation, not a tasting note. Find the more precise origin, wine type, producer and — if shown — vintage. A famous regional name then becomes information you can use to choose, compare and continue learning.
:::

## Contemporary developments — not a museum behind a label

Bordeaux carries abundant history but does not stand still. Producers work at very different scales and with different approaches to farming. Some seek lighter extraction or earlier drinkability; others continue to build for long maturation and ageing. Dry white, sparkling wine, clairet and less familiar origins receive renewed attention alongside classic red.

The most urgent change comes from climate. Warmer conditions can accelerate ripening, raise alcohol, lower acidity and increase drought stress, while wet periods and fungal risk remain part of an oceanic climate. Adaptation is not one intervention but a set of choices involving plant material, soil, canopy, timing and regulation. [@source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation]
:::

:::detail{#klimaat-en-aanpassing parent="bordeaux-vandaag" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025"}
### Changing without emptying origin of meaning

Research in Bordeaux examines phenology, drought response, grape choice, rootstocks and vineyard measures, among other subjects. [@source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation] The broad AOP specification has also admitted several adaptation varieties under limited conditions. [@source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2025] Such steps can buy time and spread risk; their long-term effect on regional style and adoption is still developing.

This creates a fundamental tension. An appellation preserves recognisable collective practice, yet must be able to change enough to keep producing credible wine under new conditions. Too little movement makes rules vulnerable; change that is too rapid can hollow out the origin's meaning. Bordeaux's future will not be decided by one “climate grape”, but by an ongoing balance between continuity and adaptation.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Understanding Bordeaux means seeing connections. Water and trade shaped the landscape; appellations organise origin and rules; classifications each tell their own institutional story; grape, year and producer form the finished wine. None of these layers can replace the others.
:::
