Entre-deux-Mers is the broad hill country between the Garonne and Dordogne. Its name literally means “between two seas”, though *mers* here points to the large tidal rivers. Anyone thinking only of fresh white PDO wine sees one fragment: the region is a mosaic of vineyards, woods, grain fields, villages, abbeys and numerous Bordeaux origins. Red wine can now carry the Entre-deux-Mers appellation too.
:::

:::section{#geografie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gironde-landscape-atlas-entre-deux-mers"}
## Overview — land in the fork of two rivers

The Garonne borders Entre-deux-Mers to the south and west, the Dordogne to the north. They meet in the Gironde to the west; eastward the area continues towards the departmental boundary. Numerous stream valleys cut the limestone plateau into ridges, slopes and small valleys.

This is a geographical region, not a synonym for the [[appellation.entre-deux-mers|Entre-deux-Mers PDO]]. Wines in the same landscape may carry other appellations. Even within an eligible commune, only delimited parcels are legally relevant.
:::

:::detail{#geen-eiland parent="geografie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gironde-landscape-atlas-entre-deux-mers"}
### “Between two seas” is not an island

The rivers direct movement, trade, drainage and views, but do not seal the region off. Bridges, roads and historic markets connect it with Bordeaux, Libourne and neighbouring areas. On a smaller scale, wooded ridges divide the catchments of tributaries. A short journey can consequently reveal a new pattern of vines, pasture, arable fields and woodland.
:::

## History — abbeys, farmland and Bordeaux's port

Medieval monasteries and lordships helped clear land, organise viticulture and shape trade routes. La Sauve-Majeure Abbey is a visible remnant of that world. Through the Garonne, Dordogne and Bordeaux's port, wines reached England and other markets, while the region itself remained mixed farmland.

The vineyard expanded greatly in the nineteenth century, before oidium, downy mildew and [[concept.phylloxera|phylloxera]] brought destruction and replanting. The twentieth century organised production around appellations, merchants and cooperatives. Entre-deux-Mers became an AOC in 1937 and was long best known as dry white wine.
:::

:::detail{#volume-en-identiteit parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
### Growth made the region important, not uniform

The official specification describes about 42,000 hectares of vines around 1850 and more than 70,000 around 1870, before phylloxera struck from 1876. Those figures show scale, not an unbroken quality line. Replanting radically changed vine material, business structures and the economy.

A 1924 judgment still connected Entre-deux-Mers with white and red wine. The 1937 AOC protected white, made explicitly dry from 1953. The recent return of red is therefore both an innovation and a reconnection with an older regional reality.
:::

:::section{#handel-en-instituties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
## Trade and institutions — rivers, merchants and cooperatives

The rivers took wine to Bordeaux's port; merchants carried it farther. Independent estates and cooperatives coexist in the modern region. Appellations protect collective origin rules, while producers shape their own parcels, blends and market positions.
:::

:::section{#landschap-klimaat-en-bodem depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gironde-landscape-atlas-entre-deux-mers source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
## Landscape, climate and soils — limestone hills with many faces

A limestone foundation gives much of the region its relief. Erosion exposed limestone and clay on slopes, while plateaux and valleys carry sand, loam, clay and gravelly deposits. Exposure and elevation influence sunshine, wind, frost and drainage. The region is neither a flat plain between rivers nor one soil type.

The Atlantic climate is mild and moist. River valleys may hold frost and mist; slopes move water and cold air differently. Woods and hedges break the wind and increase landscape diversity. In hot dry years, retaining water becomes as important as draining rain.
:::

:::detail{#bodem-en-keuze parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025 source.gironde-landscape-atlas-entre-deux-mers"}
### Place creates possibilities, not guaranteed flavour

Limestone and clay soils can offer water reserves; higher, free-draining sites may warm and dry more quickly after rain. This helps explain why white and red varieties coexist. Wine style and quality nevertheless also emerge from yield, root system, picking date, cellar work and vintage.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
## Grape varieties — white and red in a mixed landscape

For white wine, [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]], [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]] are familiar names, with Sauvignon Gris and several supplementary varieties. Sauvignon offers aroma and acidity, Sémillon texture and ripe fruit, and Muscadelle a floral accent.

Among reds, [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] lead, joined by Malbec and Petit Verdot. The exact combination varies with soil, producer and legal origin.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijlen-en-appellations depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
## Wine styles — white, red and more than one origin

Dry white Entre-deux-Mers is often light to medium bodied, citrusy and herbal, and intended to drink fresh. The PDO now also allows red. Many further red, rosé, white and sweet wines are produced under other Bordeaux appellations within the geographical region.

:::

:::section{#appellations depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
## Appellations — the region exceeds its best-known name

Red wine from the Entre-deux-Mers landscape historically did not need to carry that name, and might appear as Bordeaux or Bordeaux Supérieur. The new red category changes the offer; it does not rewrite every earlier label. Other parts of the region also have their own more specific protected names.
:::

:::section{#classificaties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
## Classifications — no central château hierarchy

Entre-deux-Mers has no official producer rank comparable with 1855 or the Graves Classification. Reputation grows through producer, location, cuvée and consistency; it must not be confused with appellation status.
:::

:::detail{#geen-centrale-classificatie parent="classificaties" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
### Producers share rules, not an historic ranking

Entre-deux-Mers has no official château hierarchy comparable with 1855 or the Graves Classification. Reputation comes through producer, location, cuvée, consistency and market rather than a central rank. Cooperatives and family estates are both essential parts of production.
:::

## Viticulture and winemaking — freshness and ripeness by parcel

Growers adapt pruning, canopy, ground cover and harvest to different slopes and soils. White wine depends on aromatic freshness; red requires skins and seeds to ripen without sacrificing acidity and water balance. Cellars range from rapid, protected fermentation for fruity whites to skin contact and maturation for reds.

:::

:::section{#in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-wine-styles"}
## In the glass — no single regional taste

Dry white may show citrus, green herbs and light texture; red ranges from supple plum fruit to more Cabernet-led structure. Other appellations within the region add rosé and sweeter styles. Producer, origin name and vintage are therefore better guides than treating geographical “Entre-deux-Mers” as a flavour label.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
## Contemporary developments — climate pressure and a new red identity

Hotter years, drought spikes and intense rain increase differences between sites. The economic question also remains: which style creates enough value to sustain farming? Extending the PDO to red is partly an attempt to connect regional production more closely with recognisable origin.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-bezoek depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
## Labels and terminology — always ask which Entre-deux-Mers

On a label, Entre-deux-Mers means the appellation; in a guidebook it may mean the much larger country. Read colour, producer, vintage and full origin line. For recent red wine, the vintage also helps establish which generation of rules applied.

Visitors find no continuous sea of vines, but a living cultural landscape. The alternation of Romanesque churches, villages, woods, fields and vineyards explains why no single photograph or flavour profile can summarise the region.
:::

:::key-idea{#kernidee depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gironde-landscape-atlas-entre-deux-mers source.inao-entre-deux-mers-specification-2025"}
Entre-deux-Mers is first a large rolling country between Garonne and Dordogne, and only then the name of one appellation. That distinction reveals its real richness: mixed landscape, long trading history, and both white and red wine.
:::
