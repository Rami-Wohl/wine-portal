:::summary{#medoc-in-het-kort depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-medoc-region source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas"}
The Médoc is a narrow world between two great bodies of water. Atlantic dunes and pine forest lie to the west; to the east, the Gironde opens into a broad tidal horizon. The famous vineyards chiefly follow that estuary side, where low gravel rises, clay, sand, ditches and stream valleys form a landscape far less uniform than the single word “Médoc” suggests.

Here Bordeaux built its most familiar red-wine image: Cabernet Sauvignon, châteaux, barrel cellars and historic classifications. Yet this is not merely a collection of grand façades. Its wine grew from water management, farming, port trade, capital and generations of labour. To understand the Médoc is not to memorise one flavour, but to read how place, season, grapes, producer and market meet in a bottle. [@source.civb-medoc-region] [@source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas]

## Overview — peninsula, wine region and protected names

The Médoc lies northwest of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]], on the peninsula between the Atlantic Ocean and the Gironde estuary. In wine, the name usually points to the vineyard zone along the Gironde's left bank. That is a cultural and landscape region, not a single protected designation.

Eight appellations sit within it. [[appellation.medoc|Médoc AOP]] and [[appellation.haut-medoc|Haut-Médoc AOP]] are two separate, broad origins. Six communal appellations complete the group: [[appellation.margaux|Margaux]], [[appellation.moulis-en-medoc|Moulis-en-Médoc]], [[appellation.listrac-medoc|Listrac-Médoc]], [[appellation.saint-julien|Saint-Julien]], [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]] and [[appellation.saint-estephe|Saint-Estèphe]]. “Médoc” can therefore mean the whole wine region or one particular AOP; context and label must tell you which. [@source.civb-medoc-region] [@source.inao-medoc-overview]
:::

:::detail{#regio-aop-en-linkeroever parent="overzicht" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview source.inao-haut-medoc-overview"}
### Three maps that do not quite coincide

The geographical Médoc also includes coast, forest, lakes, villages and farmland outside appellation vineyards. The wine region is narrower and mainly follows the estuary. Legal maps are more exact still: each AOP defines its participating communes and delimited vineyard parcels.

“Left bank” is not an appellation either. In Bordeaux conversation it often groups the Médoc with Graves and Pessac-Léognan, but it makes no legal promise about a bottle. Haut-Médoc does not mean “better Médoc”: it is a geographically different appellation in the southern half of the Médoc vineyard, within which the six communal names lie. [@source.inao-haut-medoc-overview]
:::

:::detail{#geen-eenvoudige-strook parent="overzicht" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022"}
### A useful simplification, while it remains open

On an overview map the Médoc resembles one long strip. On the ground, jalles — local watercourses —, marshes, villages, woods and soils of different ages cut across it. Holdings are fragmented too: one château may own parcels on several rises.

“Cabernet on gravel beside the estuary” is therefore a good first orientation and a poor final conclusion. Without verified Atlas geometry, this page does not invent appellation or soil boundaries. Landscape prose may explain space; it must not pretend to be surveying. [@source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas] [@source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022]
:::

## History — from difficult ground to a world name

Winegrowing in the Médoc did not begin with the celebrated eighteenth-century châteaux. Settlements, farming, ecclesiastical holdings and wine for local use and trade preceded their rise. Much of the low peninsula, however, was wet, difficult to cross and vulnerable to standing water. The later grands crus were built upon an older and rougher landscape.

From the seventeenth century, drainage, new investment and growing northern European demand enabled expansion. Bordeaux merchants, magistrates and wealthy families developed larger vineyards and estates that brought production, storage and name together. During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, some became international references. [@source.medoc-wine-council-history] [@source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history]
:::

:::detail{#handel-drainage-en-landgoederen parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.medoc-marsh-drainage-1957 source.boutoulle-bordelais-wine-trade-2000 source.medoc-wine-council-history"}
### Draining water, exporting wine

Dutch entrepreneurs and engineers played a documented part in draining areas of the Bas-Médoc. Dykes, ditches and main channels made land more usable, but they did not single-handedly “create” the whole wine region. Local communities maintained the system, while vineyards expanded chiefly on better-drained rises. [@source.medoc-marsh-drainage-1957]

At the same time, Bordeaux's port connected the Médoc to British, Dutch and other markets. Merchants selected, financed and sold wine; their interest in recognisable origins and consistent lots helped sharpen château identities. Water management and trade were two sides of one development: one made production possible, the other made reputation valuable. [@source.boutoulle-bordelais-wine-trade-2000]
:::

:::detail{#landschap-is-mensenwerk parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.lavaud-bordeaux-vineyard-landscape-2014 source.medoc-wine-council-history"}
### Classification, crises and recovery

The [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]] froze a nineteenth-century commercial reputation into five ranks. No uninterrupted triumph followed. Powdery and downy mildew and, above all, phylloxera forced replanting and new viticultural knowledge; wars, economic crises, fraud and changing markets altered ownership and production.

The twentieth-century AOC system gave origin and practices a legal frame. Meanwhile ditches, roads, hedges, woods, cellars and parcels kept changing. The Médoc is not a “natural” museum landscape, but a continuously maintained cultural landscape in which power, labour and ecology remain visible. [@source.lavaud-bordeaux-vineyard-landscape-2014]
:::

## Landscape, climate and soils — life between ocean and estuary

The Atlantic brings a temperate maritime climate: winters are generally mild, rain can fall in every season and harvest remains exposed to sudden changes in weather. The great Landes forest helps shelter the vineyards from direct ocean winds. On the other side, the Gironde is an immense body of water that can moderate temperature extremes near its banks.

The vineyards do not occupy high hills, but subtle elevations. These *croupes* can shed water more readily than the hollows between them. A difference of only a few metres matters in a wet climate: roots need moisture, but prolonged saturation restricts air and growth. [@source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas]
:::

:::detail{#grind-klei-zand-en-kalk parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022 source.inao-medoc-overview source.inao-haut-medoc-overview"}
### Gravel is famous; the mosaic matters more

River deposits from different periods left gravel, sand and loam. Gravelly rises drain quickly and warm relatively easily, which can help late-ripening [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]]. Elsewhere, clay and finer soils retain more water and may suit [[grape.merlot|Merlot]]. Limestone and marl also occur below ground and around the margins.

None of these materials pours a flavour directly into wine. Soil affects water, heat, nutrition and rooting; rootstock, drainage, season and management determine the vine's response. Official dossiers therefore describe several soil families, not a magic equation in which “gravel equals great wine”. [@source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022] [@source.inao-medoc-overview]
:::

:::detail{#jalles-croupes-en-water parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas source.medoc-marsh-drainage-1957"}
### Drainage is infrastructure and ecology

Jalles and ditches move rain and groundwater towards the estuary and divide groups of gravel rises. Their management touches vineyards, homes, wetland habitats and flood risk at once. Too little drainage can suffocate roots; overly rapid removal can reduce reserves during dry summers.

The historical reflex that “drier is better” is no longer enough. Climate adaptation calls for precise water management, organic matter, cover crops and attention to wet landscapes beyond the vine rows. Terroir does not end at a parcel boundary.
:::

:::section{#wijnstijlen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-medoc-region source.inao-medoc-overview source.inao-haut-medoc-overview"}
## Wine styles — red at the heart, not one recipe

The Médoc's international identity rests on still red wine. Cabernet Sauvignon often leads the blend, with Merlot and smaller portions of other varieties. Young wines may show blackcurrant, plum, cedar, spice, freshness and firm tannin; over time, tobacco, earth, leather and dried fruit may emerge.

This is a range, not a template. Wine from more clay-rich northern ground can feel different from Cabernet on a warm gravel rise beside the estuary. Yield, ripeness, extraction, oak, selection and vintage widen the differences. The Médoc can be recognisable without being uniform. [@source.civb-medoc-region]
:::

:::detail{#structuur-en-variatie parent="wijnstijlen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview source.civb-bordeaux-blending"}
### Structure is more than weight

In successful red Médoc, fruit, acidity, alcohol and tannin keep one another in motion. Cabernet can provide length and a firm spine; Merlot can fill the middle and make the wine accessible sooner. Barrel ageing may refine texture and add aroma, but new oak is not a requirement for regional identity.

“Powerful” does not automatically mean alcoholic or hard. Some wines persuade through precision and perfume, others through breadth or slow development. Producer and appellation offer more guidance than a single generic tasting sentence.
:::

:::detail{#wit-in-de-medoc parent="wijnstijlen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview"}
### An old practice gained a new name

Médoc estates had long made white wine, but could not sell it as AOP Médoc. A change approved in 2025 added still white wine to that appellation; European publication followed in 2026. [@source.inao-medoc-overview] This is a current legal development, not a reason to repaint the region's historic reputation.

The rule belongs to [[appellation.medoc|Médoc AOP]], not automatically to all eight appellations. Exact grapes, zones and production rules belong on the current appellation page. At regional scale, white remains a small, emerging family beside an overwhelmingly red tradition.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties source.inao-medoc-overview"}
## Grape varieties — Cabernet leads an ensemble

[[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] is the Médoc's emblem. It ripens late and can develop blackcurrant fruit, fresh acidity and abundant tannin on warm, freely draining sites. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] generally ripens earlier and may bring fruit, volume and softer contours. Their proportions change with soil, estate and year.

[[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]], [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]], [[grape.malbec|Malbec]] and [[grape.carmenere|Carménère]] play smaller but sometimes meaningful roles. Petit Verdot can add colour, tannin and spice in ripe seasons; Cabernet Franc may bring perfume and tension. Permitted does not mean planted everywhere, and typical does not mean compulsory.
:::

:::detail{#ras-is-geen-recept parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-grape-varieties source.civb-bordeaux-blending"}
### The blend begins in the parcel

A variety is chosen for its response to soil, water and ripening rhythm. Cabernet on fast-draining gravel may benefit in a wet year; Merlot on moisture-retaining clay may respond differently in drought. Vine age, [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]], planting density and harvest date further change the result.

Only then does the cellar compose the [[concept.assemblage|blend]]. Familiar varietal roles are not flavour buttons that guarantee “structure” or “softness”. Every lot first has to be ripe, sound and balanced.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-blending source.inao-medoc-overview"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — from parcel choice to blend

Style begins with pruning, yield, soil management, canopy and harvest timing. Varieties and parcels are often fermented separately. During skin contact the wine gains colour, aroma and tannin; the maker manages temperature and movement to build enough structure without needless hardness.

After fermentation, lots are tasted and combined. [[concept.assemblage|Blending]] is not a rescue operation here, but a way of bringing different ripening rhythms and parcels into one wine. Then comes [[concept.elevage|élevage]]: development before bottling, often partly in oak, yet with different balances of tank, new barrels and used barrels.
:::

:::detail{#selectie-extractie-en-elevage parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-blending"}
### More selection makes more wines, not automatic truth

Many estates select a [[concept.grand-vin|grand vin]] and direct other lots to a [[concept.second-wine|second wine]] or another cuvée. This can make the main wine more consistent and create an earlier-drinking style, but the terms are not universal quality guarantees.

Nor do [[concept.extraction|extraction]] and oak follow a mandatory Médoc standard. A ripe, powerful harvest may call for restraint; a lighter year demands different choices. The best technique is not the loudest, but the one that lets fruit, structure and origin speak together.
:::

:::section{#appellations depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-medoc-region source.inao-medoc-overview source.inao-haut-medoc-overview"}
## Appellations — eight ways to delimit origin

The eight appellations do not form a simple ladder from broad to better. [[appellation.medoc|Médoc]] covers the northern half of the vineyard zone, while [[appellation.haut-medoc|Haut-Médoc]] covers the southern half outside the six communal names. Both are full AOPs with their own geographical areas and rules. [@source.inao-medoc-overview] [@source.inao-haut-medoc-overview]

Within the southern area, roughly south to north, lie [[appellation.margaux|Margaux]], [[appellation.moulis-en-medoc|Moulis-en-Médoc]], [[appellation.listrac-medoc|Listrac-Médoc]], [[appellation.saint-julien|Saint-Julien]], [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]] and [[appellation.saint-estephe|Saint-Estèphe]]. The sequence helps orientation; it is not a ranking.
:::

:::detail{#medoc-en-haut-medoc parent="appellations" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview source.inao-haut-medoc-overview"}
### Broad names with places of their own

Médoc AOP lies mainly in the north, where gravel terraces alternate with more clay, sand and low ground. Haut-Médoc runs north from the edge of Bordeaux through the southern vineyard area. An estate outside a communal AOP can therefore make excellent Haut-Médoc; a broader name is not an automatic demotion.

The six communal appellations are not style boxes with hard walls either. They protect origin, while parcels and producers within one name continue to differ. Exact boundaries, permitted colours and current rules belong on the individual entity pages.
:::

:::detail{#zes-gemeentelijke-namen parent="appellations" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.civb-medoc-region source.inao-haut-medoc-overview"}
### Proximity without sameness

Margaux is large and internally varied; Moulis and Listrac sit farther inland; Saint-Julien, Pauillac and Saint-Estèphe continue north along the estuary corridor. Location affects soils, drainage and climate, but an appellation name alone never predicts the quality of a bottle.

The learning route should therefore move from region to appellation and then to producer, cuvée and vintage. The regional page provides the connections; child pages carry precise history, rules and internal differences.
:::

:::section{#classificaties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-classifications source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
## Classifications — three systems, three different questions

The [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]] is the best known. It orders historic classified growths into five ranks and, for red wine, chiefly comprises Médoc estates. A rank belongs to a particular château within a historical system; it is not a rank for the appellation and is not earned afresh each year. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]

The [[classification.crus-bourgeois-du-medoc|Crus Bourgeois du Médoc]] and [[classification.crus-artisans-du-medoc|Crus Artisans du Médoc]] also exist. They give visibility to different groups of businesses and operate by their own criteria and cycles. “Cru” becomes useful only when you know which system, validity period or vintage, and which producer is meant.
:::

:::detail{#1855-en-zijn-grenzen parent="classificaties" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
### A commercial moment that became landscape history

The 1855 list grew from existing market prices and reputations for the Paris Exposition. It did not measure soil in the modern sense and did not judge every producer that exists today. This explains both its historic power and its blind spots. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]

Classification influences attention, price, investment and expectations, but does not protect a bottle from vintage, winemaking or poor storage. It is an important historical layer above appellation and producer — not a summary of all quality in the Médoc.
:::

:::detail{#bourgeois-en-artisans parent="classificaties" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-classifications"}
### Recognition beyond the classified growths

Crus Bourgeois and Crus Artisans make clear that the Médoc contains many more businesses than the famous 1855 list. Their selection conditions, categories and time spans have changed over their histories. Any current claim must therefore be checked against the applicable official list.

Nor are these systems reserve classifications to which every non-1855 estate automatically belongs. Participation, scale and assessment differ. The reader's key rule is simple: treat a classification term as a precise reference, not as general decoration.
:::

:::section{#handel-en-instituties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.boutoulle-bordelais-wine-trade-2000 source.medoc-wine-council-history"}
## Trade and institutions — between château and world market

A Médoc wine travels through a network. The château grows, makes and often bottles; a [[concept.courtier|courtier]] brokers and gathers market knowledge; a [[concept.negociant|négociant]] buys and distributes to customers in many countries. These roles changed over time, but explain why producer reputation and the Bordeaux trade remain closely linked.

“Château” is place, business and wine name at once. [[concept.estate-bottling|Estate bottling]] strengthened the direct bond between bottle and producer in the twentieth century without making the trading network redundant. The Médoc is both agricultural region and brand system — and tension between the two belongs to its identity.
:::

:::detail{#reputatie-voorraad-en-prijs parent="handel-en-instituties" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.boutoulle-bordelais-wine-trade-2000"}
### Why one appellation contains such different prices

Price does not arise from production cost or tasting quality alone. Scarcity, historic rank, global demand, distribution, scores, vintage and the amount released all matter. Some top wines sell [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]] before bottling; others reach market through normal stock.

Market value should never be read as a simple terroir meter. A famous name may provide access to capital and selection, while a lesser-known estate can make compelling wine in the same appellation. Tasting and price answer different questions.
:::

:::section{#etiketten-en-begrippen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.inao-medoc-overview"}
## Labels and terminology — read the full name first

If “Médoc” appears as the appellation on a label, it means [[appellation.medoc|Médoc AOP]], not any wine from all eight appellations. If it says Pauillac or Margaux, that is the protected origin. Then look for producer, vintage, bottler and any classification.

A château picture, “grand vin” or the word [[concept.cru|cru]] creates no official rank on its own. “Mis en bouteille au château” tells you where the wine was bottled, not that it is classified or superior. A label becomes clearer once origin, producer, cuvée and classification are read as four separate layers. [@source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025]
:::

:::detail{#wit-en-actuele-regels parent="etiketten-en-begrippen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-medoc-overview"}
### Old rules of thumb can expire

For a long time, anyone taught that Médoc AOP was always red was correct. Under the 2025 change, the name can now appear on white wine that meets the new conditions. [@source.inao-medoc-overview] The date of a source therefore matters: wine law is durable, not immovable.

The safe method is not another absolute shortcut, but checking the complete appellation, colour and current specification. This page orients; the future AOP page must carry the exact white varieties and production rules.
:::

:::section{#in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-medoc-region"}
## In the glass — recognition without a checklist

Red Médoc often accompanies roast meat, mushrooms, lentils and other savoury, earthy food. Young tannic wine may benefit from air and from protein or fat at the table. Do not serve it too warm: cool room temperature keeps fruit and freshness clearer than a stuffy heated room.

A young, firm wine may open in a decanter; with old bottles, careful pouring chiefly helps leave sediment behind. Ageing potential varies enormously. Appellation, producer, cuvée, year, bottle size and storage together say more than “Médoc ages well”.
:::

:::detail{#proeven-met-context parent="in-het-glas" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-medoc-region"}
### Compare intelligently

Taste two Médocs side by side with one variable changed: the same vintage from Médoc and Haut-Médoc, two producers in one appellation, or young and developed bottles of the same wine. Note fruit, acidity, tannin, alcohol, oak impression and length before looking at reputation.

The classic profile then becomes a hypothesis rather than an answer. “Cedar” or “graphite” is a human aroma association, not proof that the wine came from a particular gravel rise.
:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.inao-medoc-overview"}
## Contemporary developments — ripeness is easier, balance is not

Warmer growing seasons can help late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon, but they also bring heat, drought, faster sugar accumulation and sunburn. The maritime Médoc remains vulnerable to wet springs, fungal pressure and rain near harvest. Climate change does not replace one problem with one new problem; it increases the need to decide season by season.

Producers work with cover crops, canopy, pruning dates, rootstocks, plant material, harvest timing and cellar selection. Rules move too: the recent white Médoc AOP and changes to growing requirements show appellations responding to farming, market and climate. [@source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation] [@source.inao-medoc-overview]
:::

:::detail{#water-biodiversiteit-en-arbeid parent="hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation source.gironde-estuary-landscape-atlas"}
### The future lies between the rows too

Water shortage can become sharper on fast-draining gravel; on low ground, drainage remains vital during wet periods. More organic matter, targeted ground cover and less bare soil may aid structure and biodiversity, yet sometimes compete for water. No measure is automatically right in every season.

Transition has a human dimension as well. Manual labour, safety, cost, technical knowledge and investment capacity differ among businesses. A credible future for the Médoc is not only about preserving a celebrated flavour, but about a viable landscape in which winegrowers, residents and estuary ecology all retain a place.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
The Médoc is neither a flavour recipe nor a ranking of châteaux. It is a wine landscape between ocean and estuary, built from eight appellations, varied soils, historic trade networks and continuing choices. Read a bottle in layers: **region → appellation → producer → cuvée → vintage**.
:::
