:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-history source.inao-sauternes-overview"}
South of Bordeaux, Graves and Sauternais follows the left bank of the Garonne, from vines enclosed by the city's suburbs to the quieter country around the Ciron. Gravel gave Graves its name, but does not explain the region alone. Sand, clay, limestone, woodland, river mist and centuries of trade made possible a remarkable range: red wine, dry white wine and some of the world's most celebrated botrytised sweet wines.

The name is primarily a useful geographical and historical umbrella. Labels carry more precise protected origins, including [[appellation.pessac-leognan|Pessac-Léognan]], [[appellation.graves|Graves]], [[appellation.sauternes|Sauternes]] and [[appellation.barsac|Barsac]].
:::

:::section{#geografie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-terroir source.inao-sauternes-overview"}
## Overview — one long region, several wine worlds

The region extends along the Garonne's left bank, south and southeast of Bordeaux. In the north, Pessac-Léognan's vineyards are almost enclosed by the city. Farther south, the landscape opens into villages, woodland and the vineyards of Graves. Around Sauternes and Barsac, the Garonne meets the smaller, cooler Ciron—a transition of particular importance to sweet wine.

“Graves and Sauternais” is not a separate PDO. It reveals connections between neighbouring areas but never replaces the legal origin on a bottle. Nor does every plot between Bordeaux and Langon automatically belong to one of these appellations.
:::

:::detail{#rivier-en-overgangen parent="geografie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022 source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
### River terraces do not follow appellation boundaries neatly

Over long periods the Garonne shifted and cut into the landscape, leaving old gravel terraces, younger deposits and erosion valleys side by side. Legal boundaries follow communes and approved parcels; geological formations sometimes cross them. An appellation map and a geological map therefore answer different questions.

The transition into sweet-wine country is also more gradual than a line suggests. The Ciron and its tributaries influence local humidity, but exposure and air movement help decide where morning mist lingers and then dries.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-history source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history"}
## History — Bordeaux's old vineyard

Long before the Médoc achieved its modern prestige, wines from Graves were closely tied to Bordeaux's port. Gravelly ground just outside the city supported recognisable estate wines; [[producer.chateau-haut-brion|Château Haut-Brion]] gained a London reputation under its own name in the seventeenth century. Trade, ownership and proximity to the city made this an early centre of quality wine.

That proximity later became a threat. Bordeaux expanded over northern vineyards, roads and railways divided the land, and diseases including [[concept.phylloxera|phylloxera]] forced replanting. The twentieth century supplied legal contours: Graves and Graves Supérieures were recognised in 1937, Sauternes and Barsac in 1936, and the northern quality centre became the separate Pessac-Léognan appellation in 1987.
:::

:::detail{#droog-en-zoet-in-de-handel parent="geschiedenis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-history source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
### Dry and sweet developed within the same trading world

This is not the history of one immutable style. Red claret, dry white wine and sweeter whites found different markets at different times. In the south, selective picking and demand for concentrated sweet wine encouraged increasingly precise work with [[concept.botrytis|noble rot]]. In the north, both red and dry white built estate reputations.

It is therefore misleading to see Graves only as red wine and Sauternais as an isolated sweet enclave. The contrast is real, but grapes, merchants, techniques and families remained part of a larger Bordelais network.
:::

:::section{#handel-en-instituties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history source.graves-winegrowers-history"}
## Trade and institutions — the city as neighbour and gateway

Bordeaux supplied quays, merchants, storage and access to international buyers. That infrastructure helped estate names and different wine styles find markets. Producer syndicates and the appellation system now organise collective origin, while château, merchant and individual cuvée retain separate roles.

## Landscape, climate and soils — gravel, water and shelter

Gravel is the famous image: stones and sand left by the Garonne in old terraces. Such soils drain rain readily and warm quickly. Yet the region is not one gravel sheet. Clay-rich subsoils can retain water, limestone rises nearer the surface in places, and lower zones contain sand and younger river deposits.

The Atlantic climate is generally mild, with forest sheltering the vineyards from the strongest ocean influence. Rain makes drainage and disease pressure important; hot dry periods make water reserves equally important. Around the Ciron, cold morning air over warmer Garonne water can form mist. When a drier afternoon follows, botrytis may concentrate the berries rather than merely spoil them.
:::

:::detail{#bodem-is-geen-smaakmachine parent="landschap-klimaat-en-bodem" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.brgm-bordeaux-geology-2022 source.graves-winegrowers-terroir"}
### A stone does not write a tasting note

Gravel often favours late-ripening Cabernet through warmth and drainage, while clay may provide water reserves and suit Merlot. These are working hypotheses, not automatic flavour rules. Root depth, rootstock, slope, season and soil management alter the result.

Even Sauternes' famous mist offers no guarantee. Noble rot requires ripe healthy fruit, appropriate fungal development and alternating moisture and dryness. Persistent rain may bring grey rot; a dry year may delay botrytis. The landscape creates possibilities, not certainty.
:::

:::section{#druivenrassen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-wines source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
## Grape varieties — two families in one region

For red wine, [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] form the core, joined by Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot and others. Cabernet can bring structure, blackcurrant and length on warm gravel; Merlot generally ripens earlier and often adds rounder fruit and volume.

For white wine, [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]] lead, with Muscadelle and sometimes Sauvignon Gris. Sauvignon commonly brings aroma and acidity; Sémillon can supply texture, waxy depth and susceptibility to noble rot. The same varieties can therefore become taut dry white or rich sweet wine.
:::

:::section{#wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-wines source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
## Viticulture and winemaking — three routes from the vineyard

Red wine ferments with the skins to acquire colour and tannin; separate parcels and varieties are then blended and frequently matured in barrel. Dry-white grapes are normally pressed quickly and protected from oxidation; barrel fermentation and fine-lees ageing may add texture, but are not a compulsory style recipe.

Sweet wine demands a different harvest rhythm. Pickers pass repeatedly through the same rows, taking only sufficiently concentrated berries. Tiny yields, slow fermentation and exacting selection explain part of both the intensity and the production cost.
:::

:::detail{#keuzes-per-stijl parent="wijnbouw-en-wijnmaken" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-graves-specification-2025 source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
### Ripeness does not mean the same thing for every wine

Red wine needs ripe skins and seeds without losing freshness. Dry white may prioritise aromatic tension before maximum sugar. For botrytised grapes, work starts only once ripe berries have lost water through fungal action.

These aims can coexist within one estate but require separate parcels, picking dates and cellar logistics. Appellation rules ultimately determine which route may be sold under which name.
:::

:::section{#appellations-en-classificaties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-graves-overview source.inao-sauternes-overview"}
## Appellations — read the name precisely

[[appellation.pessac-leognan|Pessac-Léognan]] protects red and dry white wine in the north. [[appellation.graves|Graves]] does so farther south. [[appellation.graves-superieures|Graves Supérieures]] is the historic name for white wine with residual sugar, but is in a legal transition towards inclusion in Graves. [[appellation.sauternes|Sauternes]] and [[appellation.barsac|Barsac]] protect selectively picked white wines with residual sugar.

:::

:::section{#classificaties depth="foundation" source_refs="source.bordeaux-graves-classification source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
## Classifications — two historic systems

The [[classification.graves|Graves Classification]] now applies to fourteen Pessac-Léognan estates and may concern red, white or both. The [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 Classification]] includes Château Haut-Brion among the red first growths and separately ranks the sweet growths of Sauternes and Barsac.
:::

:::detail{#geen-verticale-ladder parent="classificaties" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.bordeaux-graves-classification source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
### Origin and rank are separate axes

An appellation states where and under which collective rules a wine was made. A classification grants historical status to named estates or wines. Graves, Pessac-Léognan and Sauternes are therefore not an ascending quality ladder; red and white are not higher or lower steps either.

The two classifications are not interchangeable. Their dates, selection bases, colour categories and terminology differ. Château Haut-Brion is the striking overlap, not proof that the systems form one whole.
:::

## Wine styles — from cedar to candied citrus

Reds may combine blackcurrant, plum, cedar, tobacco and earthy notes with evident tannin. Dry white ranges from citrus-led and taut to broader, smoky and creamy. Botrytised sweet white may show apricot, honey, saffron, wax and candied citrus, yet should avoid heaviness through firm acidity.

:::

:::section{#in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.graves-winegrowers-wines source.inao-sauternes-overview"}
## In the glass — temperature and time follow style

Serve red lightly cool around 16–18°C, dry white cooler, and sweet white chilled but not icy. Powerful red and white examples can develop for years; concentrated Sauternes and Barsac may age for decades. Producer, vintage and bottle condition matter more than one fixed timetable.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-huidige-opgaven depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025 source.legifrance-graves-transition-2025"}
## Labels and terminology — read the legal name

Read the exact appellation first, then colour or style, producer, cuvée and vintage. “Graves and Sauternais” will not normally be the legal origin on a bottle. Graves Supérieures requires extra care on older and future labels: an initiated registration change may alter the appellation under which comparable wine is offered.

:::

:::section{#hedendaagse-ontwikkelingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inrae-bordeaux-climate-adaptation"}
## Contemporary developments — each wine type feels climate pressure differently

Climate change affects each style differently. Earlier ripening may help red grapes but pressure acidity and water balance; dry white depends on preserving freshness; botrytised wine relies on an increasingly uncertain window between humidity and drying. Soil care, harvest timing, plant material and selection are becoming still more important.
:::
