**Cru** is one of those wine words that sounds familiar yet acquires meaning only once you know where you are. In Bordeaux it may refer to a wine estate, in Burgundy to a closely delimited origin within the appellation structure, and in Champagne to a wine-growing commune. *Cru* by itself is therefore not a universal seal of quality.

Always read the complete phrase: *grand cru*, *premier cru*, *cru classé* and *cru bourgeois* do not belong to one worldwide ladder. Each region and system asks its own question—about place, producer, origin or rank.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-in-context depth="foundation" source_refs="source.bivb-burgundy-aoc-levels source.champagne-cru-faq source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
## Meaning — a word that changes scale

In broad use, *cru* points to a recognisable unit of winegrowing: something named as a distinct origin, vineyard, estate or wine. The word itself does not settle which unit is meant. Regional rules and customs supply that meaning.

You therefore cannot treat two bottles bearing *grand cru* as if they held the same kind of rank. First establish whether the phrase forms part of an appellation name, a classification of sites, a rank for producers or a historical designation. Only then does it convey useful information.
:::

:::detail{#vier-vragen parent="betekenis-in-context" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.bivb-burgundy-aoc-levels source.champagne-cru-faq source.gcc1855-classification-overview"}
### Four questions prevent most confusion

For any cru term, ask: **where**, **what**, **which system** and **which period**? Where establishes the regional vocabulary. What separates parcel, commune, estate and wine. The system tells you whether a formal classification or appellation is involved. The period matters because lists and rules can change while historical terms continue to circulate on labels and in books.

The word is not a conclusion, but a prompt to look more precisely.
:::

:::section{#bordeaux depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
## Bordeaux — often the estate, sometimes a rank within a system

In [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]], *cru* is often associated with a château or estate and the wine released under that name. The [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 Classification]] ranked named crus in classes; it did not rank every individual parcel in the [[region.medoc|Médoc]]. A *cru classé* is therefore not the same kind of object as a classified Burgundian climat. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]

Terms such as *cru bourgeois* and *cru artisan* likewise become precise only within their own recognition systems: [[classification.crus-bourgeois-du-medoc|Crus Bourgeois du Médoc]] and [[classification.crus-artisans-du-medoc|Crus Artisans du Médoc]]. A château, its [[concept.grand-vin|grand vin]] and its classification status are related but separate layers of information.
:::

:::detail{#saint-emilion-valkuil parent="bordeaux" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023"}
### In Saint-Émilion, Grand Cru can form part of the origin name

[[appellation.saint-emilion-grand-cru|Saint-Émilion Grand Cru]] is a protected appellation with its own production requirements. The additional phrases *Grand Cru Classé* and *Premier Grand Cru Classé*, by contrast, denote ranks in the separate [[classification.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion Classification]]. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-grand-cru-overview] [@source.saint-emilion-grand-cru-specification-2023]

An important reading rule follows: every classified wine from [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]] falls within Saint-Émilion Grand Cru, but not every bottle of Saint-Émilion Grand Cru carries a classification rank. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022] *Grand Cru* in the appellation name is not, by itself, the rank *Grand Cru Classé*.
:::

:::section{#bourgogne depth="foundation" source_refs="source.bivb-burgundy-aoc-levels"}
## Burgundy — place comes first

In Burgundy, the emphasis falls on delimited places. A Premier Cru is a precisely bounded *climat* within a Village AOC; its label gives the commune name together with the name of that climat. A Grand Cru generally forms an AOC in its own right, with the climat name taking centre stage on the label. [@source.bivb-burgundy-aoc-levels]

Here the cru designation therefore speaks primarily about the wine's level of origin. The maker still matters, but a producer may make wines from several crus and one cru may be worked by several producers. Cru and domaine are not the same thing.
:::

:::detail{#plaats-voor-producent parent="bourgogne" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.bivb-burgundy-aoc-levels"}
### One place can have several makers

Fragmented ownership and separate bottlings give Burgundy a different reading order from the Bordeaux reflex. First identify appellation, commune and any named climat; then ask who made and bottled the wine. Two producers may put the same cru on their labels without making identical wines.

The place name defines origin and rules, not an automatic flavour or quality in every bottle. Vintage, viticulture, winemaking and producer decisions still help shape what reaches the glass.
:::

:::section{#champagne depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-cru-faq"}
## Champagne — a cru is a wine-growing commune

In Champagne, the scale shifts again: a cru corresponds to a wine-growing commune. It does not name one château or one individual vineyard. The Comité Champagne counts 319 crus within the appellation. [@source.champagne-cru-faq]

When a Champagne label or historical text mentions a Premier Cru or Grand Cru, keep that communal scale in mind. It is fundamentally different from both a Bordeaux estate and a Burgundian climat.
:::

:::detail{#historische-schaal parent="champagne" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.champagne-cru-faq"}
### The old percentage scale survives in the words

The historical *échelle des crus* assigned communes percentages from 80 to 100, originally tied to grape prices. From 1952, *Grand Cru* was reserved for communes rated at 100 per cent and *Premier Cru* for those rated from 90 to 100 per cent. INAO abolished the percentage scale in 2010, but communes holding the historic designations were allowed to retain them. [@source.champagne-cru-faq]

The terms therefore survive even though the mechanism that once expressed their rank in percentages is no longer the current system. Old tables should not be read as a present-day price scale.
:::

## Reading a label — begin with the complete name

Start with the exact appellation and region. Then establish what the cru term denotes: a place, commune, estate, wine or classification rank. Next identify producer and bottler, and for a classification check the edition or validity period to which the designation belongs; [[concept.vintage|vintage]] is a separate layer of information again.

This turns an impressive word into useful information. *Grand cru* may be a formal and meaningful designation, but its meaning does not travel unchanged from one region to another. Even within Bordeaux, an appellation and a classification can use nearly identical words to say different things.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Cru never says enough without context. Identify the region and system first; only then can you know whether the word denotes an estate, delimited site, commune, appellation or classification rank.
:::
