A vine grows in several environments at once. It stands within a region's climate, experiences this season's weather, occupies a particular site, and carries leaves and bunches with their own microclimate. These scales are connected but not interchangeable. **Climate, weather, site and microclimate** therefore teaches you to follow how heat, light, water, moisture and wind actually reach the plant rather than attaching one label to a vineyard. [@source.wmo-climatological-normals] [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wmo-climatological-normals source.oiv-climate-variability-2015"}
## Overview

**Weather** is the short-term state and sequence of the atmosphere: a hot afternoon, three wet weeks, a night frost or a windy flowering period. **Climate** describes the distribution of such conditions across a long series of years. It includes not only averages but seasonal rhythm, variability and the probability of extremes. A climate normal uses a fixed thirty-year period under WMO practice, allowing places and periods to be compared on a recognizable basis. [@source.wmo-climatological-normals]

A [[concept.vintage|vintage]] is not a synonym for weather or climate. It is the outcome of one [[concept.grapevine-phenology|growing cycle]] in which weather acts within a longer climate pattern upon a particular cultivar, parcel and farming system. **Site** adds the concrete setting: elevation, slope, aspect, terrain form, nearby water, shelter and spatial relationship with the surroundings. **Microclimate** zooms in further on conditions around a row, leaf, flower or bunch.

Each word therefore answers a different question. Climate helps frame expectations across years; weather explains what happens at a particular moment; site explains why nearby parcels can receive different conditions; microclimate describes what plant tissue itself experiences.
:::

:::detail{#schaalnamen-zijn-gereedschap parent="overzicht" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sturman-high-resolution-vineyard-climate-2017 source.oiv-climate-variability-2015"}
### Scale terms are tools, not natural boundaries

Wine literature uses terms such as macroclimate, mesoclimate, topoclimate and microclimate. They help zoom from region to parcel and bunch, but authors do not always draw their boundaries in exactly the same place. A “mesoclimate” may mean a wine region, valley or individual vineyard; “microclimate” may refer to a parcel, canopy or a few centimetres around a berry.

The scale itself therefore matters more than the label. Always ask: what area, height, period and plant organ does an observation describe? Atmospheric models on kilometre-scale grids can reveal regional patterns, while local topography and canopies create variation over tens of metres or centimetres. [@source.sturman-high-resolution-vineyard-climate-2017]
:::

## Structure and relationships — five drivers across four scales

The environment reaches the vine mainly through five connected drivers:

- **heat and cold** govern the speed and limits of biological processes;
- **light and radiation** supply energy for photosynthesis and warm leaf, soil and berry;
- **precipitation and available water** replenish soil reserves and affect the plant differently according to timing and intensity;
- **humidity, dew and wetness duration** alter evaporative demand and conditions for disease development;
- **wind and air movement** transport heat and moisture, dry surfaces and may cool, intensify evaporation or cause damage.

No driver acts alone. A sunny day can raise both photosynthesis and leaf or berry temperature; wind can cool while increasing water demand; rain can replenish soil water yet carry a very different risk near flowering or harvest than during dormancy. The effect emerges from combination, timing and the state of the [[concept.vine-as-living-system|vine]].
:::

:::figure{#omgeving-op-vier-schalen depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.climate-weather-site-microclimate.environmental-scales"}
**From region to bunch — the same atmosphere, experienced ever more locally**

1. **Region and weather pattern:** sun, cloud, precipitation and moving air form a broad background in which years, seasons and weather situations differ.
2. **Site:** modest differences in elevation, slope, aspect, water and shelter redistribute radiation, wind, moisture and cold air within that broader pattern.
3. **Vineyard and canopy:** row direction, spacing, leaf volume and openness determine where sun and shade fall and how air moves through the row; a sensor in open ground cannot automatically describe all that variation.
4. **Leaf and bunch zone:** differences of only a few centimetres in exposure can alter leaf and berry temperature, air movement and moisture around a bunch. A local logger therefore answers a different question from a regional weather station.

The plate orders four scales rather than four fixed landscape types. Slope and water are examples of local modifiers; their presence or position is not a condition for viticulture.
:::

## How it works — from atmosphere to plant response

Temperature influences winter dormancy, budbreak, flowering, growth, respiration and the pace of [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|berry development and ripening]], among other processes. This does not mean that one higher average temperature accelerates everything. A warm winter, hot afternoon and warm night affect different processes; the same number of warm days can also produce different outcomes before flowering and during ripening. [@source.van-leeuwen-climate-adaptation-2017]

Water likewise follows a route. Precipitation is first atmospheric input; infiltration, runoff, soil storage, roots and evaporative demand then determine what the plant can take up. Humidity affects how much more water vapour air can hold, while wind disturbs the boundary layer around a leaf. Water balance, drought and irrigation receive their own full treatment; the essential point here is that “much rain” and “enough water for the vine” do not measure the same thing.
:::

:::detail{#warmte-en-licht parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.hunter-row-orientation-microclimate-2020 source.van-leeuwen-climate-adaptation-2017"}
### Heat and light travel together but are not identical

A thermometer measures temperature; a radiation sensor measures received energy. On a clear day a sunlit berry can become warmer than the surrounding air, while a shaded bunch nearby remains cooler. Leaves need light for photosynthesis, but intense radiation combined with high temperature and restricted water supply can also increase stress or sunburn.

Sun path, row direction, leaf angle, canopy density and soil reflection distribute direct and diffuse radiation through the day. Research into different row orientations shows that the two sides of a canopy do not simply receive “equal sun”: time, hemisphere, season and architecture shift the pattern. The practically relevant aim is therefore not maximum exposure, but suitable exposure for this climate, cultivar and developmental stage. [@source.hunter-row-orientation-microclimate-2020]
:::

:::detail{#vocht-lucht-en-wind parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-climate-variability-2015 source.wmo-instruments-observation-guide"}
### Rain, humidity, wetness duration and wind each tell a different story

Rain gauges record how much precipitation falls, not how long a leaf or bunch remains wet. Dew formation, fog, canopy density, shade and air movement can prolong wetness without additional rain. Relative humidity also depends on temperature: the same amount of water vapour produces higher relative humidity in cooler air.

Wind removes moisture and heat and can make surfaces dry faster. At the same time, strong dry wind can raise evaporative demand; very strong wind can damage shoots, leaves or berries. A sheltered place is consequently not universally favourable or unfavourable. Speed, duration, temperature, moisture and the vine's stage all matter.
:::

## Conditions and variation

A regional average may include two parcels that rarely share the same minimum temperature, wind or sunshine duration. Elevation generally changes the thermal context but also exposure to wind and radiation. Slopes affect runoff, erosion, mechanisation and the angle at which sunlight arrives. On clear, still nights, cold dense air can move into lower ground and collect in hollows.

These factors do not operate in a fixed hierarchy. A higher parcel may be cooler but also sit above a pool of cold air; a slope may receive more morning or afternoon sun yet face more wind or erosion. Local modelling and measurement are necessary precisely because coarse regional data capture such patterns only in part. [@source.sturman-high-resolution-vineyard-climate-2017]
:::

:::detail{#expositie-water-en-relief parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sturman-high-resolution-vineyard-climate-2017"}
### Aspect, water and relief gain meaning only in context

**Aspect** is the direction a slope faces. In the Northern Hemisphere, an equator-facing slope often receives more direct sun than the opposite face; the comparison reverses in the Southern Hemisphere. Latitude, slope angle, season, cloud and time of day determine how large the difference actually is.

Large water bodies can use their thermal inertia to moderate seasonal and daily temperature ranges near their shore. They may also influence local wind, fog or humidity. Rivers, small ponds and oceans do not act on the same scale, however, while distance, relief and prevailing wind determine whether an effect reaches a parcel. “Near water” is therefore a research question rather than a complete climate explanation.
:::

:::detail{#microklimaat-in-de-loofwand parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.pena-vineyard-canopy-temperature-2020 source.hunter-row-orientation-microclimate-2020"}
### The canopy creates an environment within the environment

A dense canopy intercepts light, slows wind and may retain moisture longer. A more open canopy admits more radiation and air but can also heat or expose bunches more strongly. Between these extremes lie countless combinations of leaf density, shoot position, row spacing, bunch position and sun angle.

In one field study, daily minimum and maximum temperatures within a Chardonnay canopy differed significantly from a standard weather station 410 metres away; west-facing bunch zones also differed from east-facing ones. The precise values belong to that location and setup, but the mechanism is widely relevant: measurement position determines which environment a temperature series describes. [@source.pena-vineyard-canopy-temperature-2020]
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs — choose the scale before the intervention

At establishment, a grower fixes many long-lived choices: parcel, cultivar, rootstock, row direction, spacing and training system. Later, pruning, shoot positioning, leaf removal, ground cover, irrigation and harvest timing can shift performance within those boundaries. Each intervention commonly alters more than one driver. More openness may improve air circulation and increase sun exposure; shelter may limit wind damage and slow drying.

A decision therefore starts with a concrete question. Is the issue regional suitability, frost in a hollow, water supply for a parcel, heat on the afternoon side of a row, or wetness around a compact bunch? Only then do the appropriate measurement and management follow. The forthcoming pages on water, soils, canopy and hazards develop those choices separately.
:::

## Global context

Maritime areas often have a different seasonal rhythm and smaller thermal extremes than comparable latitudes far inland. High sites can offset heat but, depending on location, also bring wind, radiation, short seasons or frost risk. Dry regions may have little disease pressure from prolonged wetness yet be strongly constrained by water supply; humid regions may receive enough rain while cloud, fungal risk and inaccessible wet ground create other problems.

The calendar is not globally uniform either. Northern and Southern Hemisphere seasons are opposed, while tropical or subtropical viticulture may use managed dormancy and more than one growth cycle. The OIV therefore recommends comparable variables while adapting reference periods to hemisphere, phenology and, in tropical conditions, the actual growth cycle. [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015]
:::

## Evidence and limits — measurement means choosing a scale

A standard weather station provides comparable measurements of air temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation and radiation when instrument, shielding, height and surroundings follow a known protocol. A logger in the canopy provides local information, but leaves, sunlight, sensor housing and position can make it sample another part of the system. Both are valuable when question and method are known. [@source.wmo-instruments-observation-guide]

A map or gridded climate product fills space around points, but every grid cell is a modelled summary. A monthly mean can conceal a night frost, heatwave or run of wet flowering days. A precipitation total says little about its distribution among events. Record variable, unit, sensor position, time step, reference period and spatial resolution before comparing series.
:::

:::detail{#indices-vereenvoudigen parent="meten-en-modelleren" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.oiv-climate-variability-2015 source.wmo-climatological-normals"}
### An index simplifies deliberately

Growing Degree Days, mean growing-season temperature, the Huglin Index, Cool Night Index and drought indices summarize series for a particular purpose. They use different formulas, thresholds, periods and sometimes latitude or water-balance adjustments. Their values therefore cannot be interchanged or compared outside their definitions without explanation. [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015]

A climate normal is also a reference rather than a timeless property of a place. The WMO uses successive recent thirty-year periods for current normals and retains a fixed historical period for long-term change. In a changing climate, the baseline against which an anomaly is stated must therefore remain visible. [@source.wmo-climatological-normals]
:::

:::detail{#weer-klimaat-en-attributie parent="meten-en-modelleren" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.ipcc-ar6-wgi-chapter-11 source.van-leeuwen-climate-adaptation-2017"}
### One event and a changing climate are different questions

A late frost, heatwave or downpour is a weather event. Climate change is studied through long records, physical mechanisms and models that assess changes in means, variability, timing and the probability of extremes. **Event attribution** can estimate how human influence altered the probability or intensity of some event types; confidence depends on definition, scale, observations and model quality. [@source.ipcc-ar6-wgi-chapter-11]

Changes in viticulture also often arrive in combination: earlier phenology can move a sensitive stage into another part of the year while heat, drought or heavy rainfall themselves change. An adaptation question therefore gains more from local trends, plant response and multiple risks than from declaring that one particular season “was caused by climate change”.
:::

## Significance for wine

Environment acts through the vine and the decisions around it. Temperature and light shift phenology, photosynthesis, respiration and berry development; water and humidity alter growth, evaporation and disease pressure; wind and topography redistribute those conditions. The harvested fruit carries that history, after which vinification adds another sequence of choices. [@source.van-leeuwen-climate-adaptation-2017]

Climate and site information are consequently highly useful, chiefly as causal context within the larger story of [[concept.terroir|terroir]]. When encountering a “cool climate”, “sunny slope”, “maritime influence” or “airy vineyard”, ask four questions: at what scale, over which period, through what mechanism and under what management? Those phrases then become the beginning of understanding rather than a direct prediction of taste or quality.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
A vine does not grow in one climate number. Time, relief, water, shelter and canopy repeatedly redistribute the atmosphere; the plant responds to those concrete conditions before becoming raw material for wine.
:::
