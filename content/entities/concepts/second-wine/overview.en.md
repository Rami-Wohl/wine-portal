A **second wine** is a separate wine released by an estate alongside its [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]]. The name indicates its place in the range, not a universal second quality tier. It may be made from different parcels, younger vines, another [[concept.assemblage|blend]], or lots that are not selected for the principal wine after tasting.

This does not make a second wine a diluted Grand Vin, nor automatically a bargain with exactly the same style. Origin, varietal proportions, selection and [[concept.elevage|maturation]] may differ — and each château organizes the relationship in its own way.
:::

## Meaning and use — second within one wine estate

“Second” means that the wine sits alongside and below the same producer's principal selection. The word does not compare two arbitrary wines from an appellation. A famous château's second wine is not therefore ranked above a neighbour's Grand Vin.

The two wines often share a family resemblance: they come from the same estate, are made by the same team and share part of a vineyard and cellar philosophy. The second wine nevertheless has its own name and its own [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]]. At [[producer.chateau-figeac|Château Figeac]] it is Petit-Figeac; at [[producer.chateau-mouton-rothschild|Château Mouton Rothschild]], Le Petit Mouton; at [[producer.chateau-latour|Château Latour]], Les Forts de Latour.
:::

:::detail{#geen-universeel-recept parent="betekenis-en-gebruik" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-les-forts source.chateau-mouton-wines source.chateau-figeac-wine source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### Four producers, four selections

Mouton particularly uses grapes from younger vines for Le Petit Mouton, harvesting and fermenting them separately. Les Forts de Latour may contain both parcels outside the Enclos and lots from potential Grand Vin parcels that are assigned elsewhere after blending tastings. Petit-Figeac comes exclusively from its own estate, but the choice between the two wines is made only during blending. [@source.chateau-mouton-wines] [@source.chateau-latour-les-forts] [@source.chateau-figeac-wine]

Dedicated parcels are another possibility: since 2017, Arômes de Pavie has been drawn largely from old vines specifically allocated to it. [@source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025] “Second wine” therefore describes a function; only the producer tells us how that function is filled with grapes.
:::

## Selection and style — earlier to approach does not mean simple

Second wines are often selected with a more approachable style and a shorter time horizon in mind. Less emphatic [[concept.tannin|tannin]], more immediate fruit or a different proportion of [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] can make the wine inviting sooner. These are common outcomes, not a fixed definition or a guarantee of taste.

In each [[concept.vintage|vintage]], the technical team assesses which parcels and barrels fit each wine. A lot may be excellent yet lack the structure, fragrance or line of development sought for the Grand Vin. Selection is therefore also composition: not merely placing “the best barrels” at the top, but building several coherent wines.
:::

:::detail{#geen-afvoerladder parent="selectie-en-stijl" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-les-forts source.chateau-mouton-wines"}
### Not every barrel moves one rung down

A simple pyramid — Grand Vin, then second wine, then everything discarded — misrepresents practice. Some parcels are followed with a particular wine in mind; other lots change destination only after tasting. An estate may also make a third wine, a branded cuvée or a wine under another appellation.

The hierarchy must not therefore be read backwards as a parcel map. A bottle called a second wine does not prove that all its grapes came from young vines or that every barrel was once a Grand Vin candidate. The precise route must be investigated by producer and, where relevant, by year.
:::

## Label and choice — look for the complete wine name

The words *second wine* or *deuxième vin* need not appear prominently on the label. Identify the bottle by its full name, then check appellation, [[concept.vintage|vintage]], producer and bottler. These details say more about its formal identity than its place in the producer's range. [@source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025]

Do not compare a second wine only with the Grand Vin. Ask what the same appellation and price range offer elsewhere. The shared château name provides useful context about team and origin, but price, ageing potential and quality remain properties of the specific wine and vintage.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
A second wine is one producer's second selection, not “second-rate wine”. Understand it as a distinct cuvée related to the Grand Vin — a relationship whose rules each estate defines for itself.
:::

:::caveat{#naam-is-geen-recept depth="advanced" variant="simplification" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-les-forts source.chateau-mouton-wines source.chateau-figeac-wine source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
The words *second wine* do not reveal the parcels, vine ages or barrels from which it came. Statements such as “always young vines” or “always rejected Grand Vin lots” incorrectly turn a producer-specific choice into a general rule.
:::
