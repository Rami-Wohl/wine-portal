:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-grape-phylloxera"}
Grape **phylloxera** is a tiny insect that lives on grapevines. Feeding on the roots of European vines made it notorious: in the nineteenth century, the pest forced vineyards to replant on a vast scale. The lasting response was not to abandon familiar grapes, but to graft them onto more resilient [[concept.rootstock|rootstocks]]. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]
:::

:::section{#insect-en-schade depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-grape-phylloxera"}
## A small insect, a major root problem

Phylloxera is not a fungal disease or a fault in the cellar. The insect feeds on grapevines. It can form galls — small swellings — on leaves, but for susceptible European *Vitis vinifera* vines the gravest damage occurs underground. Feeding injures young roots; impaired root function weakens the vine and can eventually kill it. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]

That distinction matters when you see a photograph. A galled leaf shows a visible part of the life cycle, not the root damage central to the historical crisis.
:::

:::detail{#blad-en-wortelvormen parent="insect-en-schade" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.osu-grape-phylloxera"}
### Leaves and roots do not show the same injury

The life cycle varies with the vineyard, vine material and phylloxera population. Some populations are found chiefly on roots; others also produce conspicuous leaf galls. A lack of obvious galls therefore says little about what is happening underground. Conversely, a gall alone does not prove that an entire vineyard will be lost. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]
:::

## The crisis that changed vineyards

Phylloxera reached Europe from North America. European vines on their own roots were often vulnerable. From the second half of the nineteenth century, the pest caused devastation; [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] too had to rebuild large parts of its vineyard. This is a chapter in the history of European winegrowing as a whole, not only of one famous region. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera] [@source.boagri-bordeaux-specification-2026]

The solution changed the plant below ground, not necessarily the grape growing above it. [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] could remain Cabernet Sauvignon while standing on different roots.
:::

:::section{#enten-als-antwoord depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-grape-phylloxera source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks"}
## Grafting as a response

In **grafting**, a grower joins a top section that bears the desired grapes to a rootstock that forms the root system. Rootstocks with North American ancestry can tolerate root phylloxera damage better than many European vines on their own roots. Familiar varieties can therefore still be grown where the pest is present. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]

That is no magical shield for the whole vine. Rootstock choice must also fit soil and growing conditions. The selection is explained further under [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]].
:::

:::detail{#resistentie-heeft-grenzen parent="enten-als-antwoord" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.osu-grape-phylloxera"}
### Resistance is not absolute immunity

Not every rootstock tolerates every phylloxera population equally well. Resistant roots also do not automatically protect leaves or defend against other pests. Replanting history therefore has no single universal recipe: plant material, local populations and monitoring still matter. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Phylloxera proved so destructive because it injured susceptible roots. Grafting onto suitable resistant rootstocks allowed many European grape varieties to continue growing.
:::
