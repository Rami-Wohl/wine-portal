Grape **phylloxera** is a minute insect that changed the wine world underground. When it crossed from North America into Europe in the nineteenth century, susceptible *Vitis vinifera* root systems collapsed. Grafting European grape varieties onto more resilient American [[concept.rootstock|rootstocks]] provided the answer. A modern vine consequently often consists of two plants: familiar grapes above ground and different roots below. [@source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020]
:::

## Biology and root damage

*Daktulosphaira vitifoliae* feeds on grapevine sap. On susceptible European vines, its most damaging form lives on the roots. Feeding makes young root tips swell and hook into **nodosities**; attacks on older roots can cause larger swellings and areas of dead tissue. Soil fungi can enter through these wounds. The vine finds it progressively harder to absorb water and nutrients, loses vigour and yield, and may die after several years. [@source.inrae-ephytia-phylloxera] [@source.uc-ipm-grape-phylloxera]

Above ground the early signs can look unremarkable: weak growth, short shoots and symptoms resembling water or nutrient shortage. By the time a declining patch becomes obvious in a vineyard, the cause has already been active among the roots.
:::

:::detail{#levenscyclus-en-verspreiding parent="biologie-en-wortelschade" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.osu-grape-phylloxera source.uc-ipm-grape-phylloxera"}
### Life cycle and spread

Phylloxera has several forms and a life cycle that varies among vine species and insect populations. Young crawlers can move through soil; infested roots, plant material, earth, tools and machinery help the pest travel between vineyards. Some populations also form leaf galls. On *Vitis vinifera*, however, the root form carries the historic and economic weight. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera] [@source.uc-ipm-grape-phylloxera]
:::

:::detail{#waarom-amerikaanse-wortels-verschillen parent="biologie-en-wortelschade" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020"}
### Co-evolution and vulnerability

North American vines and phylloxera evolved alongside one another over a long period. Many of those *Vitis* species contain root damage more effectively than European *Vitis vinifera*. Genetic research traces the European invasion most probably to insects adapted to *Vitis riparia* along the upper Mississippi. The same study explains why “American resistance” is not one simple trait: the interaction depends on both vine and phylloxera population. [@source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020]
:::

## History — a vineyard crisis without precedent

Faster transport of live plants enabled an accidental crossing in the nineteenth century. Dying vines were reported in France's Gard from 1863; researchers subsequently recognised the insect as the cause. The pest then spread through European viticulture and onwards around the world. Genetic reconstruction indicates that many invasions beyond Europe descend from these European populations. [@source.inrae-vine-disease-history] [@source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020]

The crisis was agricultural, economic and social at once. INRAE estimates that half of the French vineyard was destroyed. Harvests disappeared, land lost its purpose and entire regions had to decide whether replanting remained viable. The vineyard landscape changed as well: some marginal sites never returned, planting material was replaced, and knowledge of propagation and soils acquired new urgency. [@source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020]
:::

:::detail{#van-bestrijden-naar-herbouwen parent="negentiende-eeuwse-crisis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020 source.inrae-vine-disease-history"}
### From fighting the pest to rebuilding vineyards

The first response sought a treatment capable of saving existing vines. The insect's scale and underground life made such measures local, expensive or temporary. The decisive insight was more fundamental: preserve the European variety as the upper plant but replace its vulnerable root system. Grafting spread increasingly from around 1880. Reconstruction then required suitable American plant material, specialised knowledge and years before new vineyards produced mature crops. [@source.inrae-vine-disease-history] [@source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020]
:::

:::section{#enten-en-onderstammen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-grape-phylloxera source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks"}
## Grafting and rootstocks

In **grafting**, the fruit-bearing upper plant — the scion — is joined to a separate rootstock. The scion may be [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]], [[grape.riesling|Riesling]] or another *Vitis vinifera* variety; the rootstock supplies roots that tolerate phylloxera better. The grape retains its identity while the plant below ground is biologically different. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]

This combination became the standard response wherever root phylloxera is present or poses a credible introduction risk. Selection now extends far beyond resistance to one insect: a [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]] must also suit soil, lime, water availability, vigour and other soil pests. [@source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks]
:::

:::detail{#blijvend-systeem parent="enten-en-onderstammen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks source.awri-phylloxera"}
### A lasting system of plant material and quarantine

Phylloxera did not disappear once vineyards had been replanted. It still lives in many wine regions, while resistant or tolerant rootstocks keep damage manageable. Nurseries therefore supply controlled graft combinations and wine regions regulate movements of soil, machinery and planting material. Australia shows why this matters: much of its vineyard is own-rooted, while infested zones coexist with officially protected exclusion zones. [@source.awri-phylloxera]
:::

:::detail{#grenzen-van-resistentie parent="enten-en-onderstammen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.uc-ipm-grape-phylloxera source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020"}
### Resistance remains a living relationship

A rootstock need not repel every phylloxera individual to support a vine sustainably. Tolerance, local insect populations, root type and soil conditions interact. California's replanting after the once widely used AXR#1 rootstock failed also showed that a solution with *Vitis vinifera* in its parentage can prove vulnerable to a more aggressive population. Selection, monitoring and regional experience therefore remain part of the system. [@source.uc-ipm-grape-phylloxera]
:::

:::section{#franc-de-pied depth="foundation" source_refs="source.uc-ipm-grape-phylloxera source.francs-de-pied-association"}
## Franc de pied — vines on their own roots

An [[concept.franc-de-pied|own-rooted or franc-de-pied vine]] grows directly on its own roots, without a graft union or separate rootstock. It may be an old survivor, but it can equally be a young vine deliberately planted on its own roots. The term describes propagation and plant structure; age and wine quality do not automatically follow from it. [@source.francs-de-pied-association]

Own-rooted *Vitis vinifera* can persist where the insect remains absent, where strict quarantine prevents introduction or where very sandy soils strongly inhibit the pest. Such local conditions can also occur within affected countries. “Franc de pied” is consequently a parcel or plant characteristic, not evidence that an entire region is free of phylloxera. [@source.uc-ipm-grape-phylloxera]
:::

:::detail{#waar-eigen-wortels-standhouden parent="franc-de-pied" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.wines-greece-pdo-santorini source.sag-chile-quarantine-pests-2025 source.awri-phylloxera"}
### Three different phylloxera-free contexts

**Santorini** is an island case: the Greek wine organisation describes its sandy, low-clay volcanic soil as the reason phylloxera never affected the vineyard, allowing old vines to persist on their own roots. **Chile** uses a national phytosanitary status: the official 2025 quarantine list records *Viteus (Daktulosphaira) vitifoliae* as absent from the national territory. **Australia** works at zone level: the insect occurs in parts of Victoria and New South Wales, alongside Phylloxera Exclusion Zones maintained through biosecurity measures. [@source.wines-greece-pdo-santorini] [@source.sag-chile-quarantine-pests-2025] [@source.awri-phylloxera]

These are snapshots with different definitions. “Never reached”, “officially absent” and “not detected within a protected zone” each mean something different and require a dated source at the right geographic scale.
:::

:::detail{#hedendaagse-beweging parent="franc-de-pied" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.francs-de-pied-association source.rvf-liber-pater-profile"}
### The contemporary franc-de-pied movement

An international producer association seeks to preserve knowledge of own-rooted vines, local varieties and their growing practices, and is developing its own certification mark. This is a heritage and producer initiative rather than a universal legal wine category or proof of superior flavour. [@source.francs-de-pied-association]

In [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]], [[producer.liber-pater|Liber Pater]] is an outspoken contemporary example: the [[appellation.graves|Graves]] estate plants old Bordeaux varieties on their own roots, and its owner chairs the association. Such planting is a deliberate project within a region where phylloxera may be present, not an indication that Bordeaux is phylloxera-free. [@source.francs-de-pied-association] [@source.rvf-liber-pater-profile]
:::

## Lasting consequences for the wine world

Phylloxera changed more than the root system. The crisis accelerated international cooperation, research into vine species and rootstocks, specialist nurseries and phytosanitary controls. It also rearranged vineyards: not every site or variety returned, while replanting forced choices about variety, rootstock, density and business model.

The modern vineyard carries that history every day. The graft union at the foot of a vine, the choice of a particular rootstock and rules governing movements of soil or machinery are not footnotes to a finished disaster. They are the living system that allows viticulture to coexist with phylloxera. [@source.inrae-phylloxera-genomics-2020] [@source.awri-phylloxera]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Phylloxera made a vine's roots a decisive viticultural choice. Grafting preserved many *Vitis vinifera* varieties; own-rooted vines persisted in carefully distinguished local, island, national and protected-zone contexts.
:::
