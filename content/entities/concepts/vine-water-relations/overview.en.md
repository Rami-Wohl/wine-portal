Water keeps a grapevine alive, but it never reaches the vine directly from a rain gauge. It must enter the soil, remain within reach of active roots and travel through the plant to transpiring leaves. Meanwhile, warm, dry or windy weather continually changes demand. **Vine water relations** describe this entire exchange—and explain why controlled limitation differs from harmful drought stress. [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020] [@source.chaves-grapevine-deficit-irrigation-2010]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020 source.chaves-grapevine-deficit-irrigation-2010"}
## Overview

A vineyard **water balance** compares inputs with storage, movement and losses. Rain and irrigation supply water. Some is intercepted by the canopy or runs off; some infiltrates and is stored temporarily in the root zone. Roots take up a share of that water, the vine transports it upwards, and leaves release most of it as vapour. Evaporation from soil and plant together is called **evapotranspiration**.

Several terms describe different parts of this story. **Soil moisture** says where water is held in the soil. **Available water** is the portion roots can reach and extract under the prevailing conditions. **Vine water status** describes the state of the plant itself. A **water deficit** occurs when supply and uptake do not fully match demand; **drought stress** is the physiological strain that follows. These often move together, but they are not interchangeable.

A wet winter may build a deep reserve and still be followed by a hot, windy summer. Conversely, a moderate rainfall total may suffice when rain infiltrates well, the root zone is deep and atmospheric demand remains limited. The pathway therefore matters more than one rainfall or irrigation total.
:::

:::detail{#vraag-en-aanbod parent="overzicht" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020"}
### Demand and supply meet inside the plant

The atmosphere does not pull equally hard on the water pathway every day. Warm, dry, moving air can accept more water vapour than cool or humid air. The difference in vapour pressure between leaf and air is commonly called **vapour pressure deficit**, or VPD. High VPD raises potential evaporative demand, but actual transpiration also depends on light, leaf area, wind, stomata and hydraulic supply from soil and roots.

A vine in moist soil may therefore come under temporary strain on a hot afternoon, while one in drier soil may keep functioning under lower demand. [[concept.climate-weather-site-microclimate|Weather and microclimate]] shape demand; soil, roots, plant tissues and management determine how much of it can be met.
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020"}
## Structure and relationships — the pathway from soil to atmosphere

Water moves along a continuous gradient through soil, root, xylem, leaf and atmosphere. Evaporation from leaves helps pull the water column upwards through xylem under tension. Roots must remain in contact with moist pores; conducting tissue must function; leaves must regulate their loss. A strong restriction in one part slows the entire pathway.

That pathway carries more than water. Dissolved minerals travel from the root zone, transpiration helps cool leaves, and adequate supply supports cell turgor, shoot growth and berries. The link to [[concept.vine-as-living-system|the vine as a living system]] is direct: water is simultaneously transport medium, raw material, cooling mechanism and part of the structural firmness of young tissues.
:::

:::detail{#wortelzone-is-meer-dan-diepte parent="opbouw-en-samenhang" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020"}
### A root zone is more than a depth measurement

A soil may hold much water yet release only a portion to the vine. Texture, structure, pore distribution, organic matter, stone content and depth affect storage and movement; compaction, oxygen shortage or an impermeable layer reduce effective rooting volume. The distribution of living fine roots matters as well. A deep taproot in a drawing tells less than where active roots actually meet moisture.

Cultivar, age and [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]] influence root architecture and response, but always within a particular soil and season. The forthcoming soil hub develops these properties; the essential point here is that “deep-rooted” or “water-retentive soil” never predicts vine water status by itself.
:::

:::figure{#waterroute-in-beeld depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.vine-water-relations.soil-plant-atmosphere-pathway"}
**The soil–plant–atmosphere pathway**

1. **Input:** rain and drip irrigation represent two possible sources. Their presence does not reveal how much reaches the roots.
2. **Infiltration and storage:** water enters pores, may move sideways or across the surface, and remains available to different degrees around soil particles.
3. **Root uptake:** fine, active roots take up most water where contact, oxygen and water potential permit.
4. **Xylem transport:** water moves upwards through xylem into shoots and leaves. The arrows model a system; they are not visible pipes with a constant pumping rate.
5. **Leaf exchange:** water vapour exits through stomata, while carbon dioxide can enter through those same openings for photosynthesis.
6. **Atmospheric demand:** radiation, temperature, humidity and wind together influence how strongly the surroundings promote evaporation.
:::

## How it works — stomata guard a costly gateway

The underside of many leaves carries **stomata**: adjustable pores through which carbon dioxide enters and water vapour leaves. Open stomata support photosynthesis and evaporative cooling, but cost water. When supply falls behind or atmospheric demand rises, the plant narrows the opening. This reduces loss and protects its water status.

Protection has a cost. Less open stomata also restrict carbon dioxide uptake; photosynthesis and growth decline, while leaves lose part of their evaporative cooling. As deficit increases, expansion of young tissues commonly slows first, followed by stronger constraints on gas exchange and production. Prolonged severe stress may cause loss of turgor, leaf fall, damaged water transport, inadequate reserve formation and eventually vine death. [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020]
:::

:::detail{#matige-beperking-is-geen-recept parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chaves-grapevine-deficit-irrigation-2010"}
### Moderate limitation is a condition, not a quality recipe

A temporary, moderate limitation can restrain excessive shoot growth and alter the balance between canopy and fruit. That may be useful for some combinations of cultivar, stage, climate and production goal. The same shortage at another moment may harm fruit set, leaf function, berry growth or ripening. Deficit-irrigation trials consequently show different effects on yield and berry composition according to timing, severity, cultivar, rootstock and environment. [@source.chaves-grapevine-deficit-irrigation-2010]

“Water stress” is not a seal of merit. A vigorous, balanced vine may still find sufficient water through a dry period; a weak vine on a wet site may take up little because its roots are damaged or oxygen-starved. The relevant question is what limitation the plant experiences at what time, not whether the vineyard looks dry.
:::

:::detail{#hydraulische-grenzen parent="werking" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020"}
### Water potential, signals and hydraulic limits

Researchers describe the driving force through **water potential**: water moves along a potential gradient from soil through plant to the much drier atmosphere. As soil dries while evaporation continues, the xylem water column comes under greater tension. Stomata respond through intertwined hydraulic and chemical signals; the hormone abscisic acid is involved, but not as one simple on–off switch.

At very negative potentials, gas bubbles can interrupt conduction in xylem vessels: cavitation and embolism. Leaves and petioles may lose conduction before perennial wood, sacrificing vulnerable outer parts, but extensive hydraulic damage remains dangerous. Older classifications of cultivars as strictly *isohydric* or *anisohydric* are too coarse. Genotypes differ, yet their behaviour lies on a continuum and shifts with environment, stage and measurement method. [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020]
:::

## Conditions and variation — timing changes meaning

A vine uses water throughout the active season, but not always for the same purpose. Around budbreak it supports new shoots and leaves. Before and during flowering and fruit set, growth and stress influence how many flowers become berries. Cell division and expansion in the first phase of berry growth are sensitive to supply; later, sugars, acids, aroma compounds and phenolics do not all change at the same pace. After harvest, healthy leaves may still build carbohydrate reserves for the next year.

A short early deficit can therefore act differently from the same reading during ripening. Duration and recovery matter: one hot afternoon is not a multi-week drought. Cultivar, [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]], rooting volume, crop load, leaf area and prior growth also alter the response. Water must always be read alongside [[concept.grapevine-phenology|phenology]] and [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|berry development]].
:::

:::detail{#teveel-water-en-te-weinig-lucht parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-waterlogged-vineyards"}
### Too much water can mean too little air

Roots respire and require oxygen. When a root zone remains saturated, water fills its pores and slows oxygen supply. Root function, nutrient uptake and shoot growth can decline; persistent conditions may kill roots. Drainage depends on soil structure, compaction, groundwater, relief and drainage options, not merely on rainfall total. [@source.awri-waterlogged-vineyards]

Abundant water may also support strong vegetative growth. A denser canopy changes light, airflow and humidity around clusters and can amplify other growing problems. That effect runs through growth and microclimate: rain does not automatically dilute every berry, and irrigation does not automatically create a dense canopy.
:::

:::detail{#waterkwaliteit-en-zout parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.zhou-tsang-grapevine-salt-tolerance-2021"}
### Water quality belongs in the water balance

Irrigation water can carry dissolved salts. As water evaporates, inadequate drainage or leaching may allow sodium and chloride to accumulate in the root zone, especially in dry regions. This makes uptake harder through osmotic stress and may cause ion damage when uptake is high. Scion–rootstock combinations differ in exclusion and tolerance, but no choice replaces knowledge of water, soil and the long-term balance. [@source.zhou-tsang-grapevine-salt-tolerance-2021]

Additional leaching can move salts below the active root zone, but requires more water and adequate drainage. This creates a real trade-off among plant health, soil, water quality and scarce resources—not a problem solved by “more efficient dripping” alone.
:::

:::figure{#vier-waterstanden depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.vine-water-relations.water-status-contrast"}
**Four water states in the same vine**

1. **Saturated root zone:** persistently filled pores displace soil air. Roots receive too little oxygen for normal respiration, and uptake may fall despite abundant water.
2. **Functional balance:** supply and root uptake support current demand, without implying one universal optimum or maximum growth.
3. **Moderate limitation:** shoot growth and stomata respond before the plant visibly collapses. Outcome depends on timing, duration, cultivar, soil and production goal; the panel does not mean “better grapes”.
4. **Severe, prolonged stress:** loss of turgor, leaf damage and restricted berry growth show that protection and recovery have limits.
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs — irrigation starts with an objective

Irrigation is controlled water supply, not one fixed technique. It may establish young vines, bridge a vulnerable stage, prevent severe damage, stabilize yield or provide most seasonal water in an arid climate. A reasoned decision asks: how much water is already stored, what does the plant currently demand, which stage is vulnerable, how evenly will water be distributed, and which ecological and legal boundaries apply?

Choosing not to irrigate is context-dependent too. Rainfed farming may fit where winter storage, summer rain or deep root zones carry demand. Elsewhere, the same principle may cause repeated loss, failed establishment or exhaustion of old vines. The OIV therefore places water use within plot monitoring, suitable plant material and systems, efficient application and protection of a shared resource. [@source.oiv-sustainable-water-viticulture-2018]
:::

:::detail{#irrigatiestrategieen parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-sustainable-water-viticulture-2018 source.chaves-grapevine-deficit-irrigation-2010"}
### Strategy describes purpose and timing; technique describes delivery

**Supplementary irrigation** adds to rainfall when storage or a critical stage requires it. **Full irrigation** provides a large share of seasonal need in very dry systems. **Deficit irrigation** deliberately replaces less than estimated full demand; **regulated deficit irrigation** changes the intended limitation by developmental stage. *Partial root-zone drying* alternates wetted parts of the root zone in an attempt to direct plant responses, but its outcome and practical advantage are context-dependent.

Drippers, microsprinklers and other systems determine where and how quickly water arrives. They do not constitute a strategy by themselves. A precise drip system can be scheduled badly; a less localized method can serve another purpose in a particular soil or for frost protection. Uniformity, clogging, pressure, energy, evaporative loss, root distribution and maintenance all belong in the assessment.
:::

:::detail{#efficientie-heeft-een-schaal parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.oiv-sustainable-water-viticulture-2018 source.zhou-tsang-grapevine-salt-tolerance-2021"}
### Efficient water use requires a chosen scale

“More grapes per litre” may describe crop-level efficiency, but not how much water a catchment loses. Water counted as a loss on one plot may recharge groundwater or be reused downstream; more efficient application can also encourage expansion or more intensive use. Pumping energy, source reliability, ecological flows, other users and salt disposal all belong in a sustainability assessment.

Precision sensors and variable application can reveal spatial differences, but cannot repair an unclear objective. First define which condition is to be avoided or reached, at what scale and with what uncertainty. Technology can then reduce guesswork.
:::

## Global context

In Mediterranean climates, winter rain may fill storage while the dry summer brings gradual limitation. In desert viticulture across parts of Argentina, Australia, South Africa, Spain and the western United States, controlled supply makes winegrowing possible in many places. Cooler or wetter regions may instead face saturation, erosion and large year-to-year variation. Tropical systems can use water to organize growth and rest around managed pruning cycles.

These examples do not form a simple map of an “old world” that is dry-farmed and a “new world” that is irrigated. Every country contains multiple climates, soils, water rights and production systems. Irrigation may be prohibited, restricted, licensed or widely permitted, and rules may change during scarcity. A current legal claim therefore belongs to the relevant region or appellation and a dated official source.

Water is shared as well. A vineyard competes or cooperates with households, ecosystems, food crops, industry and downstream users. A complete account includes source, storage, energy, quality, timing, return to the system and impacts beyond the plot boundary.
:::

## Evidence and limits — which part of the system is being measured?

A soil-moisture probe tracks water at one point and depth. A pressure-chamber reading on a leaf or shoot estimates plant-tissue **water potential**. Stomatal conductance describes gas exchange in sampled leaves; sap flow tracks transport; leaf or canopy temperature may signal reduced evaporative cooling. Carbon isotopes in grape sugar provide a retrospective, integrated seasonal signal. Water-balance models combine weather, soil and crop in a calculated estimate. [@source.van-leeuwen-vine-water-status-2009]

No method replaces all others. Soil sensors miss roots outside the sampled volume; a leaf measurement is a snapshot; thermal images also respond to radiation, wind and canopy structure; a seasonal signal arrives too late to schedule yesterday’s irrigation. Good diagnosis combines a clear question, appropriate scale, standardized timing and repeated observation.
:::

:::detail{#drempels-zijn-methodespecifiek parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020 source.van-leeuwen-vine-water-status-2009"}
### A threshold belongs to a method, moment and context

Leaf water potential before dawn samples a different part of the system from a midday reading on an exposed leaf or a covered leaf equilibrated with the shoot. Values also relate to atmospheric demand, cultivar, stage, soil heterogeneity, root distribution and protocol. A universal threshold table may therefore suggest more certainty than the experiment supports.

The same applies to models and remote sensing. They can reveal patterns missed by isolated readings, but require calibration and ground observations. The most useful result is often not an absolute label such as “mild” or “severe”, but a trend: how does this plot respond under comparable conditions, and what changes after rain, irrigation or heat?
:::

## Significance for wine

Water influences the raw material through the plant. It changes shoot and leaf growth, light and air around clusters, fruit set, berry size, photosynthesis, ripening and reserve formation. Skin-to-juice ratio and concentrations of sugars, acids, phenolics and aroma precursors may consequently shift. Direction and magnitude are not fixed: stage, intensity, duration, cultivar, rootstock, crop load, climate and winemaking combine to shape the outcome. [@source.chaves-grapevine-deficit-irrigation-2010]

Treat a claim such as “drought concentrates wine” as the start of an investigation. Was the berry smaller because growth was moderately restricted, because it shrivelled or because development failed? Did the leaves keep functioning? Was this a red cultivar valued for skin compounds, a white wine with different priorities for freshness and yield, or another production goal entirely? And was vine water status actually measured?

Within [[concept.terroir|terroir]], water is a powerful route linking climate, soil, plant material and human action. That is precisely why one dry summer, one irrigation event or one soil name can never predict flavour or quality on its own.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Water stress is not an ingredient that a grower can simply dose. Water moves through one connected soil–plant–atmosphere system; only by reading supply, demand, plant response, timing and purpose together can limitation be understood as manageable, useful, harmful or life-threatening.
:::
