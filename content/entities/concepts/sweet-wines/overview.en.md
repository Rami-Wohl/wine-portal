Sweet wine brings sugar into the glass. Very different choices can lie behind it: allowing grapes to ripen longer, removing water from berries, interrupting fermentation or adjusting sweetness later. Understanding a sweet wine means following both the grape and fermentation: concentrated grapes provide a different starting point, while subsequent processing determines how much sugar remains in the wine.
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dwi-wine-taste-categories source.oiv-fermentation-interruption"}
## Overview

Sweetness connects a broad family of wines. Some are still, unfortified wines; others combine sugar with [[concept.sparkling-wines|bubbles]] or [[concept.fortified-wines|added alcohol]].

Sugar in the finished wine differs from sugar in grapes at harvest. A harvest term such as [[concept.spatlese|Spätlese]] also provides different information from a description of final sweetness. This page follows the journey from raw material to residual sugar; the individual technique pages explain each step further.
:::

## Structure and relationships

Four questions organize the process: how much sugar is in the must, how much yeast converts, what is adjusted afterwards, and how the result remains stable. During [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]], sugar becomes alcohol and carbon dioxide. When yeast and fermentable sugar remain, preparation for [[concept.bottling|bottling]] also needs attention.

## How it works

**Concentration** changes must before fermentation. Drying reduces water volume. Noble rot promotes water loss while also changing berry metabolism. For German Eiswein, grapes are harvested and pressed frozen, leaving some water behind as ice. [@source.dwi-praedikate] Each route has its own conditions and flavour development.

**Retaining sugar** means leaving part of the available sugar unfermented. A maker can interrupt fermentation through cooling and physical yeast removal; in Port, adding spirit during fermentation brings about interruption. [@source.oiv-fermentation-interruption] [@source.ivdp-port-vinification]

**Adjusting sweetness** can also occur later. In the traditional sparkling route, dosage follows removal of the yeast sediment. Local rules determine which operations are allowed for a particular wine. [@source.champagne-dosage]
:::

:::detail{#concentratie-en-restsuiker parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### The same raw material can lead to a different result

Must sugar describes the beginning of fermentation; residual sugar describes what remains afterwards. Dried grapes can produce dry wine with substantial alcohol when much sugar ferments, or sweet wine when more is retained. The process name appassimento therefore helps explain the raw material. The subsequent fermentation course explains sweetness.
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021 source.blanco-ulate-noble-rot-2015"}
## Conditions and variation

Grape condition remains essential during concentration. Temperature, humidity, time and fruit health govern drying and changes within the berry. Noble rot involves a different course from harmful grey rot; the [[concept.botrytis|botrytis page]] explains that distinction.

Highly concentrated must makes yeast's work harder. Fermentation behaviour therefore belongs in the explanation of the wine, alongside harvest timing and grape treatment. A higher measured concentration of a compound can also result from less water, changed metabolism or both.
:::

:::section{#keuzes-en-afwegingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-microbiological-stability source.oiv-fermentation-interruption"}
## Decisions and trade-offs

A maker retaining sugar also chooses subsequent treatment. Cooling can halt fermentation, but remaining yeast may become active again later. Microorganism removal, hygienic work and appropriate bottling preparation need to suit the wine. [@source.awri-microbiological-stability]

Concentration meanwhile calls for selection, time and management of fruit health. Greater water loss means less juice; the aim is usable raw material for the intended wine. Comparing products therefore benefits from the combination of raw material, fermentation course and stabilization, beyond the word 'sweet' alone.
:::

:::section{#wereldwijde-context depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dwi-praedikate source.masaf-pantelleria-specification-2024 source.ivdp-port-vinification source.wosa-wine-styles"}
## Global context

- **Germany:** Eiswein shows concentration through frozen pressing; Spätlese shows that a harvest term and sweetness provide different information. [@source.dwi-praedikate]
- **Italy:** [[concept.passito|Passito]] connects wine with dried grapes. Pantelleria also has a fortified passito liquoroso variant. [@source.masaf-pantelleria-specification-2024]
- **France:** [[concept.vin-de-paille|Vin de paille]] leads into different local dried-grape traditions.
- **Portugal:** Port demonstrates sugar retention by fortification during fermentation. [@source.ivdp-port-vinification]
- **South Africa:** Noble Late Harvest provides a local context for wine from botrytis-affected grapes. Local label conditions belong to that category. [@source.wosa-wine-styles]

The routes make the family recognizable; individual origins and wine names add grapes, rules and the maker's choices.
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dwi-wine-taste-categories source.german-wine-law-pradikate-2026 source.champagne-dosage source.eu-wine-sugar-labelling-2025"}
## Evidence and limits

A sugar analysis, sweetness description and harvest term answer different questions. German taste categories for still wine use sugar and, for certain categories, its relationship with acidity. Sparkling wine uses its own sweetness classification. [@source.eu-wine-sugar-labelling-2025; Annex III A–B] Read figures with the applicable category and rules.

Spätlese describes a regulated harvest/must context. The designation alone does not determine final sweetness. [@source.german-wine-law-pradikate-2026] Perceived sweetness also calls for sensory assessment: acidity, alcohol and other properties belong to the context in the glass.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dwi-wine-taste-categories source.dwi-praedikate source.champagne-dosage"}
## Significance for wine

Read a sweet wine through three pieces of information: **grape treatment, fermentation course and sugar in the finished product**. Add fortification and bubbles separately. This makes an unfortified dried-grape wine, sweet Port and sparkling wine with dosage understandable within the same family.

For a particular bottle, origin, vintage and technical information are the next step. A familiar wine name offers direction; the combination of information describes the sweet wine actually in front of you.
:::
