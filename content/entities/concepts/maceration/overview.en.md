Maceration keeps the liquid and solid parts of grapes in contact. Juice takes up compounds from material including skins and seeds. This contact is an important key to colour, structure and style—not only in red wine. [@source.oiv-maceration]
:::

:::section{#sap-en-schillen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-maceration"}
## Juice and skins

Grape skin is not a useless wrapper to discard after picking. It contains colour compounds, [[concept.tannin|tannins]], and aroma compounds or their precursors. Seeds and any included stems can also contribute. Keeping juice or young wine with those solids gives the winemaker access to another part of the grape beyond its juice alone.

**Maceration** describes contact; [[concept.extraction|extraction]] describes the transfer of compounds it enables. [[concept.fermentation|Fermentation]] is different again: yeast turns sugar into alcohol. These processes often overlap in red wine, but maceration can also take place before or after alcoholic fermentation. [@source.oiv-maceration]
:::

## Red, rosé and white

Red wine is generally fermented with dark grape skins. This explains why skin contact is central: it brings colour and tannin into the wine. For rosé, shorter contact may be chosen before separating juice from skins. Rosé can also be made by direct pressing; it is not a fixed number of hours on a stopwatch.

Many white wines are made by pressing first, then fermenting the juice. A winemaker may nevertheless deliberately allow white grapes to macerate before pressing, for example to include certain flavour and mouthfeel compounds. Longer fermentation of white grapes with their skins can produce amber or orange wine: wine made from white grapes with extended skin contact, not from oranges. [@source.awri-skin-contact]
:::

:::detail{#witte-inweking parent="rood-rose-en-wit" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-skin-contact"}
### White skin contact needs its own judgement

Skin contact can add structure to white wine, but also bitterness or a drying mouthfeel. Fruit health and ripeness, temperature, duration and protection from unwanted oxidation all matter. The same tool can serve different aims: a limited preparation of juice or an emphatically skin-fermented style. Orange is a style choice, not an automatic indication of organic farming or fault-free wine.
:::

:::section{#tijd-en-beweging depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wre-cap-management"}
## Time and movement in the tank

During red fermentation, released carbon dioxide pushes skins upwards to form a floating **cap**. When it sits above the juice, not all skins have equally good contact. The winemaker may push it down or pump liquid from the bottom of the vessel over it. [@source.wre-cap-management]

French terms appear frequently in cellar descriptions: *pigeage* means punching down, while *remontage* means pumping over. They explain how contact is renewed; they are not quality seals. At a chosen stage, liquid is drained and the remaining grape solids are pressed.
:::

:::detail{#voor-tijdens-en-na parent="tijd-en-beweging" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-cold-maceration-red source.awri-extended-maceration-2019"}
### Before, during and after fermentation

In a *cold soak*, grapes are kept cool for a period of skin contact before fermentation. The aim is to release certain compounds without starting the usual fermentation course. The technique requires healthy fruit, oxidation management and microbial control; cooling alone does not make the contents sterile. [@source.oiv-cold-maceration-red]

During fermentation, temperature and alcohol level change, altering extraction conditions. In **extended maceration** after fermentation, skins and seeds remain with the young wine. The choice is used with grapes including [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]], Pinot Noir, Shiraz and Nebbiolo, but not according to one regional or variety-specific recipe. [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019]
:::

:::detail{#koolzuurmaceratie parent="tijd-en-beweging" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-carbonic-maceration"}
### Carbon dioxide is different from cooling

[[concept.carbonic-maceration|Carbonic maceration]] begins with whole grapes in a closed tank with a carbon dioxide-rich atmosphere. The grapes are subsequently crushed and pressed, and the separated juice continues fermenting. It is not another term for cold soak, nor a method for retaining carbon dioxide as bubbles in the finished wine. The full technique and its variants belong on their own concept page. [@source.oiv-carbonic-maceration]
:::

:::section{#grenzen-van-contact depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-extended-maceration-2019"}
## Why longer is not automatically better

Longer maceration may change tannin structure, but does not guarantee more colour, softer tannins or greater ageing potential. Grapes, ripeness, vessel and subsequent winemaking help determine whether extra contact suits the intended style. In [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] this matters for Cabernet-led reds; the same judgement also belongs in Piedmont, Burgundy and Australian cellars. [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019]

The winemaker tastes and follows the batch. A calendar date can schedule an operation, but cannot independently say when a wine has enough structure.
:::

:::detail{#niet-lineaire-overdracht parent="grenzen-van-contact" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-extended-maceration-2019"}
### Contact is not an unlimited tap

Compounds are not merely dissolved: they may also react, bind to grape material or later precipitate. Measured colour compounds can therefore decline during longer contact while tannins evolve differently. After fermentation, the batch also needs appropriate protection against unwanted oxidation and microbial development. The right endpoint follows the wine and its purpose, not the assumption that every additional day adds something. [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Maceration determines which parts of the grape work with the juice. Its duration and management help create a style; they do not rank wines from simple to good.
:::
