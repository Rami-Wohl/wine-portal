## Overview — four routes from the grape

For many white wines, juice is separated from grape solids first and fermentation then takes place mainly in the liquid. Red wine instead ferments with black grape skins. Rosé offers several ways of separating pink juice early; in skin-fermented white wine, white grape skins remain during fermentation. The defining distinction combines grape colour, contact and the point of separation. [@source.awri-amber-wine] [@source.ifv-rose-production]

This page follows still wine from prepared grapes to the separation of young wine and grape solids. [[concept.grape-reception-must-preparation|Grape reception and must preparation]] explain the starting point; [[concept.elevage|maturation]] covers what happens to the young wine afterwards. [@source.oiv-pressing] [@source.oiv-maceration]
:::

## Structure and relationships — contact, conversion and separation

Four terms provide a guide. [[concept.maceration|Maceration]] is contact between liquid and grape solids. [[concept.extraction|Extraction]] is the transfer of compounds during that contact. In [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]], yeast converts sugar; [[concept.pressing|pressing]] releases liquid from grapes or pomace under pressure. Contact, extraction and fermentation can take place together in a red-wine vat. [@source.oiv-maceration] [@source.oiv-pressing]

The same operation therefore gains a different place and purpose. Early pressing allows juice to ferment off skins. Later pressing ends the chosen period with grape solids, even if the liquid still needs to finish fermenting afterwards. The route gives the sequence; the individual concepts explain how each operation works. [@source.awri-whole-bunch-fermentation]
:::

:::section{#werking depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-skin-contact source.ifv-rose-production source.awri-amber-wine source.oiv-pressing"}
## How it works — when do skins stay in?

**White:** white grapes go to the press, sometimes after short maceration. Juice preparation is followed by fermentation off skins. Brief contact before pressing and an entire fermentation on skins are different decisions. [@source.awri-skin-contact]

**Rosé:** black grapes can be pressed directly, releasing some colour as they are pressed. Another route begins with short maceration and then separates the pink juice. In both cases, the separated liquid continues fermenting without the mass of skins. Fruit choice and contact are matched to the intended rosé. [@source.ifv-rose-production]

**Red:** black grape skins stay in the vat during fermentation. At the chosen endpoint, wine is drained and the remaining solids are pressed. Free-run wine and press wine can be assessed separately. [@source.oiv-pressing]

**Skin-fermented white:** white grapes ferment with their skins and are pressed later. This is often called amber or orange wine. Contact adds a different extraction and texture dimension to white grapes; the eventual colour varies with variety and process. [@source.awri-amber-wine]
:::

:::detail{#saignee parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-saignee source.ifv-rose-production"}
### Saignée connects two batches

In *saignée*, part of the juice is drained from a vat of black grapes. That juice can ferment separately as rosé. Relatively more skins remain per unit of liquid in the original vat, changing extraction conditions for the red wine. The decision therefore affects two batches at once. When grapes are harvested for red wine, the rosé inherits that ripeness decision too; harvesting a parcel specifically for rosé allows a different choice. [@source.awri-saignee] [@source.ifv-rose-production]
:::

:::figure{#vier-routes depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.winemaking-routes.four-routes"}
**The pressing point in four routes**

1. **White:** white grapes → pressing → the separated juice ferments.
2. **Direct-pressed rosé:** black grapes → pressing → the pink juice ferments. Short maceration before separation is another option.
3. **Red:** black grapes → fermentation on skins → draining and pressing at the chosen contact endpoint.
4. **Skin-fermented white:** white grapes → fermentation on skins → draining and pressing.

The plate simplifies the main sequence. Sorting, juice preparation, optional short maceration and further maturation are outside the image. After pressing, the liquid can finish fermenting if needed.
:::

## Conditions and variation — the condition of the fruit

An intact berry initially keeps its juice inside; crushing makes it available immediately. Whole bunches bring stems into the vat too. They alter contact and drainage and can release compounds into the wine. The proportion of bunches, their condition and their position in the vat therefore matter. [@source.awri-whole-bunch-fermentation]

Fruit health and ripeness help determine how much contact is desirable. For white skin contact, phenolic extraction and protection against oxidation are relevant considerations. [[concept.carbonic-maceration|Carbonic maceration]] adds a different start: intact berries undergo changes in an oxygen-poor atmosphere, alongside or before yeast fermentation in released juice. [@source.awri-skin-contact] [@source.ifv-carbonic-maceration]
:::

:::detail{#bes-en-tros parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-whole-bunch-fermentation source.ifv-carbonic-maceration"}
### A whole bunch describes the material

Destemming and crushing are separate operations: a detached berry can remain intact. ‘Whole bunch’ mainly describes what enters the vat; ‘carbonic’ describes conditions and transformations in intact fruit. During whole-bunch fermentation, punching down and the weight of fruit can open some berries while others remain intact. A single whole-bunch percentage captures that changing distribution only partly. [@source.awri-whole-bunch-fermentation] [@source.ifv-carbonic-maceration]
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs — managing the intended contact

A floating cap forms during fermentation on skins. Punching down and pumping over bring liquid and solids together again; heat distribution and oxygen input also depend on how the operation is performed. The explanation of [[concept.extraction|extraction]] covers cap management in more detail. The winemaker chooses intensity and timing to suit fermentation progress. [@source.awri-cap-management-2023]

Pressing time is a second important decision. Longer contact can alter phenolic composition and mouthfeel, while the desired duration varies with grapes and season. Tasting and measurement help select the endpoint. Free-run wine and press fractions can subsequently be combined through [[concept.assemblage|blending]], after their contributions have been assessed. [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019] [@source.oiv-pressing] [@source.awri-amber-wine]
:::

:::section{#wereldwijde-context depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ifv-rose-production source.awri-amber-wine source.beaujolais-vinification-2020"}
## Global context — shared questions, different applications

France's IFV distinguishes rosé planned as a wine in its own right from rosé drawn from a red saignée batch. Australia's AWRI investigated skin fermentation with [[grape.chardonnay|Chardonnay]] alongside other white-wine treatments. Together, these examples show why both the harvest decision and the processing route matter. [@source.ifv-rose-production] [@source.awri-amber-wine]

The semi-carbonic practice described by Inter Beaujolais shows a limit of the simple red-wine route: fermenting juice at the bottom produces CO₂ around intact berries higher in the vat. After draining and pressing, the collected liquid can complete fermentation. ‘Red’ therefore does not fully describe how every berry passed through fermentation. [@source.beaujolais-vinification-2020]
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-extended-maceration-2019 source.awri-amber-wine"}
## Evidence and limits — a route explains part of the result

A comparison is strongest when grapes from the same batch are divided between treatments. Even then, the outcome depends on the chosen duration, temperature, equipment and subsequent handling. Research on extended maceration shows, for example, that colour compounds and tannins can develop differently. The sequence of operations therefore explains more than colour alone, but predicts the eventual taste only partly. [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019]

Skin-fermented white is used here as a description of the process. The style can involve different vessels, yeasts and protective measures; amber colour is one possible outcome within that range. [@source.awri-amber-wine]
:::

:::detail{#stijl-en-normdefinitie parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.oiv-white-wine-maceration source.awri-amber-wine"}
### A standard's definition has its own scope

In I.4.9, the OIV describes *white wine with maceration* using exclusively white grapes and at least one month of maceration. That defines the scope of this particular OIV category. AWRI's broader technical discussion of amber wine starts from fermentation on skins and covers different contact regimes. A particular wine is therefore described here by its process first; a formal category or labelling claim additionally requires checking the applicable standard and jurisdiction. [@source.oiv-white-wine-maceration; I.4.9] [@source.awri-amber-wine]
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-skin-contact source.awri-amber-wine"}
## Significance for wine — explaining colour and structure

Knowing the route helps explain possible sources of colour, [[concept.tannin|tannin]], bitterness and texture. White grapes can produce a distinctly drying mouthfeel through skin contact, while juice separated early from black grapes can yield a much paler wine. Variety, fruit condition and subsequent maturation together determine how those possibilities appear in the glass. [@source.awri-skin-contact] [@source.awri-amber-wine]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Follow the grape parts: when do juice and skins meet, when do they ferment, and when are they separated? Those three questions make the main route readable.
