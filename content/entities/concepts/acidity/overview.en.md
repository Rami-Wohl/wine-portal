Acidity gives wine momentum: it refreshes a sip and keeps sweetness and richness in balance. That role matters in crisp whites as well as red, sweet and sparkling wines. A good wine draws its balance from the way acidity fits everything else.
:::

:::section{#zuur-proeven depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-acidity-2026 source.wset-mouthfeel-2026"}
## Recognising acidity

Take a sip and notice what happens after swallowing or spitting. Acidity stimulates saliva; your mouth waters. Pronounced acidity can make a wine feel lively and taut. Balance requires enough fruit, sweetness or structure as a counterweight, whether the wine feels taut or soft.

Acidity differs from bitterness, alcoholic warmth and the drying grip of [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. A completely dry wine can still have high acidity: *dry* here describes the absence of noticeable sweetness, not a drying mouthfeel. It helps to name these sensations separately before judging the whole. [@source.wset-mouthfeel-2026]
:::

:::section{#van-druif-tot-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-wine-composition source.awri-berry-ripening"}
## From grape to wine

Tartaric and malic acid are the main acids supplied by the grape itself. Malic acid is broken down during ripening; warmer conditions favour that breakdown. Variety, growing season and picking date therefore help determine the winemaker's starting point. Cooler conditions favour acid retention, rather than guaranteeing one fixed acid profile. [@source.awri-berry-ripening]

The cellar changes that starting point. [[concept.fermentation|Alcoholic fermentation]] also produces other acids. [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|Malolactic fermentation]] converts malic acid into lactic acid and reduces acidity. The bottle consequently contains an acid profile shaped step by step through ripening and winemaking. [@source.wset-wine-composition]
:::

:::detail{#rijping-is-geen-enkele-schuif parent="van-druif-tot-wijn" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-berry-ripening"}
### Ripening means more than accumulating sugar

During ripening, sugar, acids, aromas and berry structure do not all change at the same rate. Choosing a harvest date is therefore not a race towards the lowest acidity or highest sugar. The intended wine requires those changes to make sense together. Tartaric and malic acid also follow different paths: loss through dilution and breakdown by the plant are different processes. [@source.awri-berry-ripening]
:::

:::section{#ph-en-titreerbaar-zuur depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-acidity-ph"}
## pH and titratable acidity

A technical sheet may give two different acidity measurements. **pH** describes the activity of free hydrogen ions: lower pH means a more strongly acidic environment. **Titratable acidity**, often shortened to *TA*, is determined by measuring how much base is needed to reach a specified endpoint. It is often expressed in grams per litre as tartaric acid equivalents; that does not mean all the measured acid is tartaric acid.

Wine technical sheets sometimes call TA “total acidity”. That differs from chemically determining and adding up every individual acid. The values cannot be converted directly into one another. Two wines can have the same TA but different pH. TA helps describe the amount of neutralisable acid; pH matters for microbial stability, colour reactions and the effectiveness of sulfur dioxide, the protective substance also familiar as sulfites. Neither replaces tasting. [@source.awri-acidity-ph]
:::

:::detail{#buffering-en-neerslag parent="ph-en-titreerbaar-zuur" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-acidity-ph source.awri-deacidification"}
### Why the measurements do not move together

Wine contains a mixture of acids and their salts. This mixture buffers: it absorbs part of an acid or base addition, so the pH response depends on composition. Precipitation of potassium hydrogen tartrate, a tartaric acid salt, also shifts the equilibrium. TA can fall while the direction of the pH change depends on the starting conditions. “Less measured acidity” therefore does not always mean “higher pH”. [@source.awri-acidity-ph]

Cellar trials consequently assess both the measurements and the sensory result. There is no universal addition for each desired pH step. [@source.awri-deacidification]

## Balance and ageing potential

In a sweet wine, acidity counterbalances sugar; in a dry wine the same amount of acidity has less shelter. Fruit, alcohol and mouthfeel also influence how sharp or harmonious the whole seems. A refreshing wine need not taste *sour* in the everyday negative sense.

Acidity helps wine retain freshness during development. Ageing potential also requires sufficient flavour intensity and appropriate structure. A lean, unbalanced young wine therefore needs more than high acidity to develop with interest. [@source.wset-tasting-guide-2018]
:::

:::section{#kelderkeuzes depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-deacidification source.wset-acidity-2026"}
## Shaping acidity in the cellar

A winemaker may allow or prevent malolactic fermentation, bring batches together by [[concept.assemblage|blending]], or add acid or partly neutralise it. These choices serve a wine style and a stable wine; applicable rules differ between production contexts. An allowed technique alone says nothing about quality.

The target follows the wine, not a worldwide ideal value. Pronounced acidity in a young sparkling base has a different role from acidity in a soft red intended for immediate drinking. Laboratory measurements, small trials and assessment of the whole belong together. [@source.awri-deacidification]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Acidity brings freshness and cohesion. Assess its amount and character alongside sweetness, fruit, alcohol and structure.
:::
