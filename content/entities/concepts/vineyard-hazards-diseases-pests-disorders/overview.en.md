## Overview

Vineyard risks fall into four useful groups. **Weather hazards and other abiotic threats** have non-living causes, including frost, hail, heat, sunburn, drought and prolonged oxygen shortage in saturated soil. **Infectious diseases** arise from pathogens: true fungi, oomycetes, bacteria, viruses and phytoplasmas. **Pests** are animals that cause damage, including insects, mites and nematodes; some are also vectors that move pathogens between plants. **Physiological disorders** are disruptions of growth or development without an infectious agent, for example through water, temperature or nutrient problems.

A **symptom** is the plant's response, such as yellowing, tissue death (necrosis), wilting or stunting. A **sign** is part of the causal organism itself, such as fungal growth, spores or a visible insect. **Injury or damage** is the consequence for leaf area, roots, wood, crop or fruit. The same yellowing can fit root injury, water stress, a virus or restricted nutrient uptake; one cause may in turn affect several organs. This page therefore teaches a way of looking, not diagnosis by colour.
:::

## Structure and relationships

Infectious disease requires at least a susceptible vine, a present pathogen and conditions in which infection succeeds. Time belongs in the system too: young tissue may be more vulnerable than older tissue, rain may disperse spores and symptoms may appear weeks after infection. [[concept.grapevine-phenology|Developmental stage]] therefore helps determine which organ is at risk and what a missed window will mean later.

Non-infectious injury follows a different route through the same plant. Frost kills vulnerable cells; hail wounds leaves, shoots and berries; excess radiation and heat can scorch fruit; prolonged saturation restricts oxygen around roots. [[concept.vine-water-relations|Water status]], [[concept.vine-nutrition-soil-management-vineyard-floor|root zone and nutrition]] and [[concept.pruning-training-canopy-crop-load|canopy]] affect the severity of those events. Injured tissue may later become more vulnerable to rot, so categories may follow one another in practice without becoming the same cause.

Pests connect several parts of the system. [[concept.phylloxera|Phylloxera]] feeds on roots and caused a historic crisis; certain leafhoppers transmit the bacterium behind Pierce's disease; mealybugs and scale insects spread certain leafroll viruses. [@source.uc-ipm-pierces-disease] [@source.uc-ipm-grape-virus-diseases] Injury from the vector and disease from the organism it carries must be understood separately.
:::

### From survival to renewed infection

Many pathogens pass through a cycle: survive between seasons, reach suitable tissue, enter or grow on its surface, reproduce and disperse to new tissue. Routes differ sharply. Powdery mildew grows largely on the surface and its secondary spores can spread without free water; downy mildew is an oomycete and is much more closely tied to moisture and leaf wetness. *Xylella fastidiosa*, the bacterium behind Pierce's disease, is introduced into xylem by sap-feeding insects. [@source.cornell-grape-powdery-mildew-2025] [@source.cornell-grapes-101-downy-mildew-2021] [@source.uc-ipm-pierces-disease]

An infection can be present before the first symptoms appear. “Clean today” can therefore mean nothing is visible yet, not that infection did not occur. Conversely, an old scar can remain after active risk has passed. Monitoring follows weather, phenology, block history and new tissue, rather than relying on what one walk happens to reveal.
:::

## How it works

The principal damage pathways can be separated. Weather may freeze, bruise, dehydrate or overheat cells, or deprive roots of oxygen. Fungi and oomycetes use plant tissues as resources and injure leaves, inflorescences, berries or wood. Bacteria may colonise vascular or other tissues. Viruses use living plant cells to replicate and chiefly travel in infected planting material or through specific vectors. Animals chew, suck, bore or damage roots; nematodes may also transmit viruses.

A global foundation register begins with several major groups. **Powdery mildew** (*Erysiphe necator*) produces grey-white growth on leaves and bunches and can cycle secondarily without free water. **Downy mildew** (*Plasmopara viticola*) is an oomycete, not a true fungus; oily leaf spots and white sporulation beneath the leaf fit wet infection periods. **Black rot** can leave infected berries shrivelled into hard mummies; diseased clusters left behind provide inoculum for the next season. [@source.umn-diseased-grape-clusters] **Botrytis** may cause destructive grey rot, while [[concept.botrytis|noble rot]] is desirable only under a particular sequence of conditions and with suitable fruit.

Perennial trunks can be affected by a complex of **trunk diseases**; symptoms may appear intermittently and pruning wounds provide an entry point for several agents. [@source.uc-ipm-grape-eutypa-dieback] **Leafroll, fanleaf and red-blotch viruses** may affect growth and ripening and remain within the plant. [@source.uc-ipm-grape-virus-diseases] Bacterial diseases are equally context-dependent: in **Pierce's disease**, *Xylella fastidiosa* colonises the xylem and particular sharpshooters transmit it. [@source.uc-ipm-pierces-disease]

Among pests, [[concept.phylloxera|root phylloxera]] is the historic key example. Elsewhere or in other seasons, grape moths, mealybugs, scales, leafhoppers, mites and nematodes may matter more. Significance does not depend on numbers alone: a vector may matter epidemiologically at low density, while visible feeding can sometimes remain chiefly cosmetic.
:::

### Chronic injury, latency and resistance

An annual leaf spot and an infection of wood, roots or vascular tissue operate on different timescales. Chronic problems may affect reserves, sap flow and shoot growth for years while producing obvious above-ground symptoms only in some seasons. With viruses, bacteria and trunk diseases, removing infected planting material may matter more than treating visible leaves.

Control itself also changes the system. Repeated use of products with the same mode of action removes susceptible organisms while resistant variants become relatively more common. Alternating or combining mode-of-action groups, restricting use to necessary windows and employing non-chemical measures can slow that process, but they do not make an incorrectly chosen product suitable. [@source.oiv-plant-protection-good-practices-2018]
:::

## Conditions and variation

Within [[concept.climate-weather-site-microclimate|climate, weather, site and microclimate]], timing often matters more than the hazard's name. **Winter cold** can injure permanent wood and buds; **spring frost** attacks emerged, water-rich tissue and may reduce potential crop in one night. **Hail** causes mechanical wounds whose severity depends on stone size, wind, duration and developmental stage. Around flowering it may impair fruit set; later, berries and foliage are struck directly. [[concept.coulure|Coulure]] and [[concept.millerandage|millerandage]] are not infectious diseases either: they describe different outcomes of disrupted flowering, fertilisation and berry development.

**Heat and sunburn** are related but not identical. Air temperature affects the whole vine, while an exposed berry can become considerably hotter than the air. Sudden exposure after heavy leaf removal, intense radiation and limited cooling increase sunburn risk. [[concept.vine-water-relations|Drought]] restricts sap flow and cooling; prolonged **waterlogging** instead restricts oxygen around roots. Both can inhibit growth and leaf function, but they require different responses. [@source.bernardo-grapevine-abiotic-stress-2018] [@source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021]

In disease, inoculum — infectious material already present —, susceptible tissue and weather together determine pressure. Conditions favourable to one organism need not favour another. Dense canopies dry slowly and hinder inspection and spray coverage, whereas very open canopies may suddenly expose berries to heat and radiation. Variety, clone, rootstock, vine age and block history shift risk further.
:::

### Pattern in space and time is diagnostic information

Damage after a frost night often follows low pockets where cold air settled; hail may cut a narrow track through a region; waterlogging follows drainage and soil profile. Infection may begin around contaminated planting material, an inoculum source or vector habitat and then expand. A nutrient or salt problem may instead follow soil or irrigation patterns.

Sequence matters too: which organ was affected first, did the problem appear suddenly or gradually, on one variety or all, after rain, heat, an intervention or arrival of a vector? No pattern is conclusive by itself, but it narrows the search much more effectively than an isolated close-up photograph.
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs

**Integrated pest management**, or IPM, combines prevention, observation and targeted measures in a repeatable decision cycle:

- prevent where possible through healthy planting material, suitable variety and rootstock, hygiene, drainage and a manageable canopy;
- track weather, phenology, block history, traps, models and field observations;
- determine which organism or non-living cause is plausible and how large the actual risk is;
- choose a cultural, biological, mechanical, genetic or chemical measure suited to the target and timing;
- limit human and environmental exposure, follow the label and local law, and manage resistance risk;
- check whether the measure worked and record both observation and intervention. [@source.oiv-plant-protection-good-practices-2018]

Prevention contains genuine trade-offs. An airier canopy may reduce wetness duration and infection pressure, but excessive or late leaf removal may increase sunburn. Frost sprinklers require sufficient water and correct uninterrupted operation; wind machines use a warmer layer of air above the cold air around vines during calm radiation frost. [@source.uga-vineyard-frost-protection] Hail nets, insurance and distributing blocks across sites address different parts of risk at different costs. Resistant varieties and [[concept.rootstock|rootstocks]] can alter vulnerability but do not remove the need to monitor.
:::

:::detail{#behandelen-is-geen-synoniem-voor-spuiten parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-plant-protection-good-practices-2018 source.cornell-grape-powdery-mildew-2025 source.uc-ipm-grape-virus-diseases source.uc-ipm-grape-eutypa-dieback source.uc-ipm-european-grapevine-moth source.umn-diseased-grape-clusters"}
### Treatment means more than spraying

Sanitation removes sources such as mummified berries or infected pruning wood where relevant to the disease. Canopy management can improve drying and access. Mating disruption can interfere with particular moths. [@source.uc-ipm-european-grapevine-moth] Natural enemies and biocontrol may form part of a programme; certified planting material helps keep systemic disease out. A fungicide acts only on the organisms in its spectrum and within its legally authorised use.

Timing is often more decisive than visible severity. Protectants must be present before or around infection risk; a chiefly contact-active product requires coverage; a systemically infected vine does not become virus-free by treating its leaves. The aim is not maximum intervention, but sufficient, demonstrably effective action with the least practicable collateral harm.
:::

:::detail{#resistentie-en-meerjarige-sanering parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.oiv-plant-protection-good-practices-2018 source.uc-ipm-pierces-disease source.uc-ipm-grape-virus-diseases source.awri-phylloxera"}
### Resistance and sanitation require multi-year planning

Modes of action should be managed as scarce tools. Repeated use of the same code increases selection pressure even when trade names differ. A programme therefore considers the target organism, cross-resistance, number of applications, mixture partners and local guidance. These rules change with registrations and demonstrated resistance; an international overview can never replace the local label.

With vector-borne and systemic diseases, the best decision may be to test, remove affected plants, manage vector sources and replant clean material. That feels less immediate than a foliar treatment but better matches where the organism lives. Quarantine around phylloxera and other regulated organisms also protects areas where establishment has not occurred or remains limited.
:::

## Global context

Every wine region has a different risk profile. Cool continental areas may put winter cold and spring frost first. Humid growing seasons often increase pressure from downy mildew, black rot and bunch rots; warm dry regions may instead prioritise heat, sunburn, drought and salt. Hail is highly local and erratic. A warm climate is not disease-free: powdery mildew does not require continuously wet leaves, while vectors or irrigation may introduce risks of their own.

History and biogeography remain visible. European *Vitis vinifera* proved highly vulnerable to North American [[concept.phylloxera|phylloxera]], after which grafting onto resistant American rootstocks became an important response. [@source.inrae-ephytia-phylloxera] Australian wine regions limit spread through zones and controls on moving planting material and machinery, among other measures. [@source.awri-phylloxera] [[concept.botrytis|Botrytis]] offers a different contrast: the same species may produce unwanted grey rot or, under rare suitable conditions and with selective harvesting, fruit for noble sweet wine. “Good” or “bad” therefore belongs not to the organism alone but to purpose, timing and context.
:::

## Evidence and limits

A reliable diagnosis builds evidence in layers: symptom and any sign; organ and variety affected; distribution within and among blocks; speed and timing of appearance; weather and interventions; presence of vector or inoculum; and, where needed, microscopy, culture or molecular laboratory testing. For Pierce's disease, UC IPM explicitly notes that other problems can look similar and laboratory testing is appropriate when doubt remains. [@source.uc-ipm-pierces-disease]

The schematic illustration helps explain these evidence steps; diagnosis requires observations of the actual plant. Forecast models are locally calibrated aids: they estimate risk from weather and biology but cannot see a hidden drainage failure, faulty sensor or new infection focus. Thresholds, authorised products, protective equipment and withholding periods differ among countries and change over time. For an actual intervention, consult recognised local extension, current regulation and the product label.
:::

:::detail{#onzekerheid-hoort-bij-de-diagnose parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.uc-ipm-pierces-disease source.bernardo-grapevine-abiotic-stress-2018 source.uc-ipm-grape-virus-diseases"}
### Uncertainty belongs in diagnosis

Stressors often coincide. Drought, heat and radiation may occur together; root injury can restrict water uptake; a wounded berry may later rot. A trial or observation that isolates one factor does not therefore transfer automatically to the field. Strong claims name the scale, season, variety, starting condition and measured outcome.

Absence of evidence also has limits. No visible insect does not exclude earlier vector transmission; no fungal growth does not exclude latent infection; a negative test may mislead after poor sampling. Good decisions make that uncertainty explicit and schedule reassessment where necessary.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.bernardo-grapevine-abiotic-stress-2018 source.vaczy-noble-grey-rot-2024 source.umn-diseased-grape-clusters source.uc-ipm-grape-eutypa-dieback"}
## Significance for wine

Vineyard injury affects wine through concrete intermediate steps. Lost leaf area may restrict assimilation and ripening; damage to flowers or young berries reduces yield; root and wood injury may weaken multi-year growth and reserves; split or rotten berries change the microbial and chemical condition of the harvest. Sorting out or rejecting diseased fruit connects harvest with cellar decisions. [@source.umn-diseased-grape-clusters]

This does not form a simple quality scale. A small crop after hail does not automatically make better wine, and stress is not a synonym for concentration. Outcome depends on severity, timing, recovery, ripeness, contamination and selection. The useful chain is therefore **cause → vine response → affected organ → grape composition and integrity → harvest and cellar decision**. A claim about wine is justified only when each link has been observed or supported.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
A visible problem is the start of a question, not the end of a diagnosis. Reading cause, conditions, spread and developmental stage together makes interventions more precise and prevents every yellow leaf, insect or rain event from receiving the same answer.
:::
