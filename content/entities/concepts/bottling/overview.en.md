:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-pre-packaging source.awri-sulfur-dioxide"}
Bottling transfers finished wine from tank or cask into a clean, sealed package. It may look like a final logistical operation, but it is technically critical: clarity, microbiological stability, dissolved gas, oxygen pickup, fill height and closure all help determine how the wine survives the move into bottle.
:::

:::section{#klaar-voor-verpakken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-pre-packaging source.awri-microbiological-stability"}
## When wine is ready for bottling

Before bottling, the winemaker checks whether composition, clarity and stability suit the intended wine. Residual fermentation, unwanted microorganisms, unstable proteins or crystal formation can cause visible or sensory change after sealing. Necessary preparation therefore differs from wine to wine.

[[concept.clarification-and-fining|Clarification or fining]], cold treatment and filtration are possible tools, not an automatic checklist. A wine that is stable without strong treatment may follow a shorter route; a wine with residual sugar or microbiological risk needs more control.
:::

:::section{#schone-overdracht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-pre-packaging source.awri-microbiological-stability"}
## A clean transfer into bottle

Tank, hoses, filter, filler, bottle and closure form one chain during bottling. Cleaning and hygiene must prevent a stable wine from being contaminated again immediately before sealing. Suitable filtration can remove microorganisms, but only when the equipment and wider bottling environment are also controlled.

Fill height and temperature need to be aligned because wine and trapped gas may expand or contract after bottling. The chosen closure must be applied correctly and suit both bottle and intended storage.
:::

:::section{#zuurstof-rond-botteling depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-sulfur-dioxide"}
## Oxygen during filling and closing

Wine can take up oxygen while it is pumped, filtered, filled and closed. The headspace between wine and closure also contains gas. Winery teams limit that pickup through calm closed transfer, correctly adjusted equipment and, where appropriate, inert gas.

The wine's protective condition is checked again around bottling. [[concept.sulfur-dioxide|Free sulfur dioxide]] can decline as it reacts following oxygen pickup, so the condition after the operation matters more than the tank value alone. [@source.awri-sulfur-dioxide]
:::

:::section{#verpakking-en-sluiting depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-pre-packaging"}
## Bottle, closure and later development

Glass protects wine from liquid loss and major gas exchange, but closure and package remain part of the storage system. Natural cork, technical cork and screwcap each have production and performance characteristics; choice and correct application matter more than a simple quality hierarchy.

After bottling, wine needs time to settle after the mechanical operation and adjust within its sealed package. Subsequent bottle development then belongs to wine, closure and storage conditions together.
:::

:::section{#estate-bottling depth="foundation"}
## Bottling and estate bottling

The technical bottling process differs from [[concept.estate-bottling|bottling at the château or estate]]. The latter term concerns where and under whose responsibility wine was bottled. A mobile line may, for example, travel to an estate; the technical quality controls still require the same kind of attention.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
Careful bottling helps preserve intended clarity, freshness and stability. Oxygen pickup, contamination or a poorly matched closure may emerge only weeks or months later, when correction is no longer possible.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Bottling is the last opportunity to protect wine actively before the package takes over. From then on, wine, fill height, closure and storage need to function as one system.
:::
