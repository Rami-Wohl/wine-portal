:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-pre-packaging source.awri-microbiological-stability"}
Bottling takes wine from the cellar into a sealed package. That transition starts before the filling machine: the winemaker assesses what could still change, selects treatment where needed and checks whether protection remains adequate after filling and sealing. Clarity, stability and a correctly applied closure answer different questions.
:::

:::section{#klaar-voor-verpakken depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-pre-packaging source.awri-pre-bottling-specifications"}
## Overview

Wine is ready for packaging when its composition and preparation suit the intended style and storage. **Clarity** describes what you see now; **stability** concerns changes that could occur later. Clear wine may still contain microorganisms, unstable proteins or substances capable of forming crystals.

The chosen [[concept.assemblage|blend]] is the starting point for the final assessment. Earlier results for individual lots do not replace checking the finished wine. This page connects that assessment with bottling; [[concept.clarification-and-fining|clarification and fining]] and [[concept.sulfur-dioxide|sulfur dioxide]] have their own explanations of the mechanisms.
:::

:::section{#schone-overdracht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-microbiological-stability source.oiv-filtration-wine"}
## Structure and relationships

Wine, tank, hoses, any filters, filling machine and package form one chain. Tank preparation, removal of unwanted material and hygiene during filling must work together. [[concept.cellar-hygiene-wine-faults|Microbial control]] includes protection against new contamination. Removal has little value if dirty equipment contaminates the wine again afterwards.

Protection therefore combines several measures. Filtration can retain particular material, SO₂ helps control unwanted microbial activity and oxidative change, and clean handling limits new contamination. None makes the other checks redundant.
:::

:::section{#werking depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-microbiological-stability source.awri-heat-stability source.awri-cold-stabilisation"}
## How it works

Three stability questions deserve separate attention:

| Stability | Possible change | Targeted preparation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microbial | Refermentation or spoilage | Hygiene, protection, filtration |
| Protein | Protein haze | Protein test; bentonite if needed |
| Tartrate | Crystals | Test; targeted stabilisation |

Residual sugar can feed refermentation, while remaining malic acid can allow bacterial conversion. Dry wine is therefore not automatically microbiologically stable. Protein haze forms from unstable grape proteins; tartrate crystals form from dissolved constituents. These are different causes: passing one test does not show that all three risks are controlled.

:::

:::detail{#tartraat-en-temperatuur parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-cold-stabilisation"}
### Crystal risks do not all respond alike

Cold treatment can make potassium hydrogen tartrate crystallise so it can then be removed. Other techniques inhibit crystal formation or reduce the ions involved. Calcium tartrate responds differently to temperature; cold treatment is not a general solution for it. The test must therefore match the intended risk. [@source.awri-cold-stabilisation]
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-microbiological-stability source.awri-gas-adjustment source.awri-sulfur-dioxide"}
## Conditions and variation

Residual sugar, pH, microbial condition and intended storage influence preparation. The same amount of free SO₂ does not provide identical microbial protection at different pH levels. A decision therefore starts with the wine and its risks, not a fixed dose for every bottle.

Dissolved gas also belongs in that assessment. Oxygen can promote unwanted change, whereas carbon dioxide can be part of the intended style. Wine without visible bubbles need not be free of dissolved CO₂.

:::

:::detail{#koolzuurgas-en-stijl parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-gas-adjustment"}
### Gas management also changes perception

Dissolved CO₂ can enhance freshness and prickling, but can also emphasise hardness in a red wine, for example. Its solubility depends on temperature. Driving gas out can also carry away volatile aroma compounds, so a technical adjustment calls for tasting as well. [@source.awri-gas-adjustment]
:::

:::section{#keuzes-en-afwegingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-fining-agents source.oiv-filtration-wine source.awri-pre-bottling-specifications"}
## Decisions and trade-offs

During **settling**, suspended particles sink and clear wine can be racked away. In **fining**, a selected aid binds particular constituents that are subsequently separated. In **filtration**, a medium retains particles as wine passes through it. These techniques can complement one another, but are not a compulsory sequence.

The decision concerns how much intervention a demonstrated problem requires without unnecessarily removing character. Bench trials help establish that choice. 'Unfiltered' alone means neither that a wine is unstable nor that it has greater quality.

:::

:::detail{#filterdoel parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-filtration-wine"}
### A filter has a purpose

The OIV distinguishes clarifying filtration from filtration intended to remove microorganisms. The word 'filtered' therefore says too little about microbial control. The equipment and its application must be capable of meeting the intended objective; a clear outflow does not prove that. [@source.oiv-filtration-wine]
:::

:::section{#zuurstof-rond-botteling depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-sulfur-dioxide source.awri-total-package-oxygen"}
## Oxygen during filling and sealing

Oxygen can enter wine during transfer and filling. The headspace beneath the closure also contains gas. Correctly adjusted equipment and appropriate inert gas use limit pickup. Oxygen reactions can reduce available free SO₂, making checks after transfer important.

:::

:::detail{#totale-verpakkingszuurstof parent="zuurstof-rond-botteling" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-total-package-oxygen"}
### Measure wine and headspace

**Total package oxygen (TPO)** combines dissolved oxygen and headspace oxygen, determined immediately after packaging. Measuring only the tank therefore misses part of the load. Oxygen subsequently released from or transmitted through a closure is an additional contribution during storage. [@source.awri-total-package-oxygen]
:::

:::section{#verpakking-en-sluiting depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-screw-cap-closures source.awri-total-package-oxygen"}
## Bottle and closure

Bottle, fill height, temperature and closure must suit one another. Headspace allows expansion, while the oxygen it contains also needs control. Filling higher to eliminate all gas space is therefore not a general solution.

A closure's name tells you less than its specification and application. With screw caps, the sealing liner is among the factors determining gas transmission. A correctly applied closure supports the storage objective; the appearance of a cork or cap is not proof of quality. Later bottle development also depends on the wine and storage conditions.
:::

:::section{#wereldwijde-context depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-filtration-wine source.awri-screw-cap-closures"}
## Global context

The international OIV description groups filtration by treatment objective. Australian AWRI publications then show how many practical choices exist within screw caps alone. New Zealand winemakers and Clare Valley Riesling producers contributed to renewed closure trials from the late 1990s.

These examples help distinguish country, wine style and technique. A regional practice may explain a choice, but does not replace assessing the particular wine and package.
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-heat-stability source.awri-pre-bottling-specifications"}
## Evidence and limits

A stability test examines a defined risk under specified conditions. A protein test does not predict refermentation, for example. Visual clarity and a laboratory result therefore provide complementary evidence, not interchangeable guarantees.

:::

:::detail{#test-en-monster parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-heat-stability source.awri-pre-bottling-specifications"}
### Result, method and sample belong together

In a heat test, heating, cooling and the threshold for measured turbidity affect the assessment. Compare results with knowledge of the method. The sample must also be identified: the final blend and packaged wine may differ from an earlier tank sample. Retained samples help investigate later changes.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-pre-bottling-specifications source.awri-microbiological-stability"}
## Significance for wine

Read a technical sheet as a starting point for specific questions: which risk was examined, which treatment followed, and what was checked after packaging? 'Cold stabilised', 'filtered' and 'bottled under screw cap' each answer only part of that story.

In the glass, haze can prompt investigation, but appearance alone does not identify its cause. Understanding a connection with [[concept.oxidation|oxidation]] or microbial change also requires the production and storage context.
:::

:::section{#estate-bottling depth="foundation"}
## Bottling and estate bottling

The technical process differs from [[concept.estate-bottling|bottling at the château or estate]]. That term concerns the place and responsibility for bottling. It does not describe which stability tests, filters or closures were used.
:::

:::in-the-glass{#waarom-in-het-glas depth="foundation"}
Careful preparation and packaging help preserve intended clarity, freshness and stability. A clear bottle alone does not tell you which treatment was needed or how the wine will behave later.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Stability is a set of targeted questions. Match treatment to the risk and check the whole transition from the finished wine to its sealed package.
:::
