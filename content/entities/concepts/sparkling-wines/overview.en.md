Sparkling wine brings carbon dioxide into the glass. That gas can arise during fermentation in a bottle or tank, or be added from outside. Different production routes therefore lie behind the same visible bubbles. Fermentation location, yeast contact and final sweetness help explain those differences.
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-sparkling-production source.oiv-carbonated-wines"}
## Overview

Carbon dioxide remains in wine under pressure and emerges as bubbles when it is opened and poured. Part of the family starts with still base wine that ferments again; another route continues an existing fermentation in the bottle. Carbonation uses added gas.

Sparkling describes the bubbles. [[concept.sweet-wines|Sweetness]] follows from other choices and can vary. This page compares sparkling routes; the [[concept.traditional-method|traditional method]] retains its detailed process explanation.
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation source.champagne-dosage"}
## Structure and relationships

For a fermentation route, wine or must, sugar and yeast provide the starting point. During [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]], yeast makes alcohol and CO₂. A closed, pressure-resistant vessel retains gas. The producer then decides on lees contact, sediment removal, sweetness and packaging.

In the traditional route, [[concept.liqueur-de-tirage|tirage]], [[concept.second-fermentation|second fermentation]], [[concept.lees-ageing|lees ageing]], [[concept.remuage|riddling]], [[concept.disgorgement|disgorgement]] and [[concept.dosage|dosage]] form successive steps. Tirage feeds the new fermentation; dosage adjusts the wine after sediment removal. [@source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation] [@source.champagne-dosage]
:::

:::section{#werking depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-sparkling-production source.oiv-carbonated-wines source.limoux-ancestral-method source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation"}
## How it works

| Route | Where do bubbles originate? | What happens to sediment? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional method | Second fermentation in the bottle later sold | Riddling and disgorgement remove sediment |
| Tank method | Fermentation in a closed pressure tank | Separation before bottling while retaining pressure |
| Ongoing fermentation | An existing fermentation continues in the bottle | Implementation depends on the product and local route |
| Carbonation | CO₂ is introduced from outside | An additional fermentation is not the source of the added gas |

The OIV describes bottle and tank routes and distinguishes carbonated wine separately. [@source.oiv-sparkling-production] [@source.oiv-carbonated-wines] A process name explains the gas source. Raw material and subsequent treatment remain necessary to understand the wine.
:::

:::detail{#voortgezette-gisting-en-transfer parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.limoux-ancestral-method source.oiv-sparkling-production"}
### Fermentation timing and sale bottle are separate questions

For Limoux Méthode Ancestrale, fermentation begins in must and continues after bottling. This differs from starting a new fermentation of already fermented base wine. The local example explains the principle; the name elsewhere may encompass another implementation. [@source.limoux-ancestral-method]

With **transfer**, wine ferments in bottles but moves under pressure into another vessel for further treatment and sediment separation. Bottle fermentation then means something different from sale in the same bottle. [@source.oiv-sparkling-production]
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-lees-contact-2020 source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation"}
## Conditions and variation

Base wine, fermentation and time on the lees each contribute to development. Contact with yeast cells can change aroma and texture; [[concept.autolysis|autolysis]] explains part of that development. Contact duration and conditions matter. [@source.awri-lees-contact-2020]

A route involving extended lees contact can therefore produce a different result from one with little contact, even when both involve bottle fermentation. Bread-like aromas are one possible development, rather than evidence from which every sparkling route can be identified in the glass.
:::

:::section{#keuzes-en-afwegingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-dosage source.oiv-sparkling-production source.awri-microbiological-stability"}
## Decisions and trade-offs

The main choices concern fermentation vessel, intended lees development, sediment separation and sugar in the finished wine. Tank and bottle organize fermentation and treatment differently. Comparing methods means examining these operations rather than assigning them a fixed quality ranking.

Dosage concerns sweetness after disgorgement. [[concept.bottling|Bottling]] also concerns retaining the intended gas condition and microbiological stability. Intended fermentation for bubbles and unwanted refermentation in finished wine require different judgements.
:::

:::section{#wereldwijde-context depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation source.limoux-ancestral-method source.wosa-wine-styles"}
## Global context

**Champagne** demonstrates the traditional route: second fermentation and sale in the same bottle. **Limoux Méthode Ancestrale** demonstrates ongoing fermentation. These French examples reveal a difference in fermentation timing. [@source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation] [@source.limoux-ancestral-method]

In **South Africa**, Cap Classique uses the traditional bottle route. The local style description also distinguishes tank fermentation and carbonation. The principles of sparkling routes thus travel, while each name has its own origin and conditions. [@source.wosa-wine-styles]

The individual wine adds grapes, origin, rules and variation within a name to this process picture.
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-dosage source.oiv-carbonated-wines source.eu-wine-sugar-labelling-2025"}
## Evidence and limits

A method term describes production; an origin name identifies a particular product within its own rules. Sweetness words provide further information. In Champagne's classification, **extra dry** is sweeter than **brut**, despite what 'dry' might suggest in ordinary language. [@source.eu-wine-sugar-labelling-2025; Annex III A]

External CO₂ is a technical distinction from gas produced by fermentation. That distinction alone tells little about grape variety, aroma, origin or the care taken in making the wine. Read these together with the applicable product description.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.champagne-dosage source.oiv-sparkling-production"}
## Significance for wine

Ask about **gas origin, fermentation vessel, lees contact and final sweetness**. A mention of bottle fermentation partly answers the first questions; dosage and ageing information complete the description.

This makes comparison possible beyond a single familiar name. The sparkling route explains production; origin and the maker's choices complete the wine's individual style.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Follow the gas, then the lees and sugar. Sparkling wines share their bubbles, while different routes can produce them.
:::
