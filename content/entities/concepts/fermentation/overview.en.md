Must, yeast population and cellar management work together: a good start, a fast fermentation and a fully fermented wine are three different things. ## Overview — from sugar to wine

Yeast is a single-celled microorganism. During fermentation it converts sugars in grape must into **ethanol**, the alcohol in wine, and **carbon dioxide**, or CO₂. By-products also contribute to the wine's character. In still wine much of the gas escapes; a bubbling vessel does not automatically herald a sparkling wine. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation]

The conversion generates heat. An active fermentation can therefore warm itself, which in turn affects its rate. The winemaker monitors this interaction and can intervene through cooling. This page covers the biological conversion and its management; [[concept.grape-reception-must-preparation|grape reception and must preparation]] explain how the starting material reaches the vessel. [@source.awri-fermentation-temperature]
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-yeast-choice source.awri-yan source.uc-davis-problem-fermentations"}
## Structure and relationships — must, yeast and vessel

Must supplies both sugar and a living environment. Available nutrients, acidity and inhibitory substances influence how yeast develops. The population consists of living cells: their numbers, the species and strains present, and their ability to keep functioning all matter. Abundant sugar therefore does not guarantee a trouble-free fermentation. [@source.uc-davis-problem-fermentations]

The vessel allows observation and intervention, such as measuring temperature, taking samples and cooling. The winemaker relates those measurements to the starting material and the yeast chosen. Strains differ in nutrient requirements and aroma production, among other characteristics; a choice that works in one must needs fresh assessment in another. [@source.awri-yeast-choice] [@source.awri-yan]

## How it works — growth and sugar conversion

At the start, yeast adapts to the must and the population can grow. Sugar conversion gets under way and accelerates. Later, growth declines while existing cells can still consume sugar actively. Towards the end the environment becomes more demanding: less sugar remains and progressively more alcohol has accumulated. [@source.uc-davis-problem-fermentations]

A fermentation curve thus shows different phases. A declining rate near the end does not itself establish a fault; the question is whether the remaining sugar is still being converted at an appropriate rate. Measuring cell numbers alone cannot answer that question. [@source.mouret-fermentation-strategy-2021]
:::

:::detail{#groei-en-activiteit parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.mouret-fermentation-strategy-2021 source.uc-davis-problem-fermentations"}
### A stable cell count can still ferment actively

In the stationary phase the population no longer shows net growth, but its metabolism continues. A substantial part of sugar conversion can take place in this phase. Growth and fermentation rate are therefore different quantities. Later, cells must remain functional under increasing alcohol stress; membrane composition and earlier nutrient and oxygen provision play a part. A vigorous start consequently does not tell the whole story about the final stages. [@source.mouret-fermentation-strategy-2021] [@source.uc-davis-problem-fermentations]

## Conditions and variation — yeast, nutrients and temperature

With **inoculation**, the winemaker adds a selected yeast culture. In **spontaneous fermentation**, the existing population starts without such a culture. Yeasts may come from both grapes and the winery. Spontaneous describes the choice of start, rather than an absence of measurements or subsequent management. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation] [@source.awri-yeast-choice]

Temperature affects activity and heat production. In red wine it also affects [[concept.extraction|extraction]] of compounds from skins and other solids. Warmer can mean faster, but extreme temperatures and sharp fluctuations can stress yeast. There is no single best temperature for all musts and wine styles. [@source.awri-fermentation-temperature]

Alcoholic fermentation proceeds largely without oxygen, yet a managed oxygen supply can support yeast at certain stages. This calls for a different assessment from prolonged exposure of wine to air. Amount, timing and fermentation condition matter; [[concept.oxidation|oxidation]] remains a separate process to monitor. [@source.awri-aeration-ferments]
:::

:::detail{#een-gezonde-gisting parent="gist-en-temperatuur" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-yan source.awri-nutrient-management-2022"}
### Matching nutrition to must and timing

**YAN** means *yeast assimilable nitrogen*: nitrogen yeast can use, from ammonium and assimilable amino acids. It is not the same as all nitrogen in the grape. Requirements depend on yeast strain, sugar concentration and fermentation conditions, among other factors. Measurement before fermentation helps assess the starting situation; a sample taken during fermentation no longer shows how much YAN was initially available. [@source.awri-yan]

A nitrogen addition is not a complete nutrient strategy either. Diammonium phosphate, or DAP, supplies inorganic nitrogen; complex yeast nutrients may also contain other components. Early feeding can support population growth, while later feeding may affect the activity of existing cells without producing the same growth. Unnecessarily large additions can lead to a fast, hot fermentation or unused nutrients. Dose and timing therefore require an informed assessment, rather than a fixed recipe for every tank. [@source.awri-nutrient-management-2022] [@source.awri-yan]
:::

:::detail{#spontaan-is-geen-herkomstbewijs parent="gist-en-temperatuur" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-yeast-choice source.awri-natural-ferment-sequencing-2017"}
### Populations change during fermentation

A spontaneous fermentation may start with several yeast species; later, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* often dominates as alcohol rises. A DNA sample captures one moment in that development. Identifying a species also differs from distinguishing strains within that species; commercial strains can occur in a winery too. [@source.awri-natural-ferment-sequencing-2017]

A species list therefore does not independently explain a wine's sensory outcome or [[concept.terroir|expression of origin]]. That requires investigation of the relationship between population, fermentation course and finished wine. Both spontaneous and inoculated starts need assessment of what the yeast actually does. [@source.awri-yeast-choice]
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs — the intended endpoint

For dry wine, fermentable sugar is largely consumed. Alcohol production depends partly on the available sugar and its conversion. A sugar-rich harvest can therefore produce a dry wine; alcohol content alone does not tell you how much residual sugar remains. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation]

For a sweet wine, the winemaker may deliberately interrupt fermentation, for example through cooling and removal of yeast by suitable filtration. Fermentable sugar then remains. Protection against unwanted refermentation is part of that choice; cooling alone does not make the condition permanently stable. This planned endpoint differs from an unintentionally stuck fermentation. [@source.oiv-fermentation-interruption]

Regular temperature and density measurements help track progress. A final check of remaining glucose and fructose then establishes whether those fermentable sugars are still present. The disappearance of visible bubbles does not replace that check. [@source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013] [@source.awri-residual-sugar-measurement]
:::

:::detail{#meten-in-plaats-van-bellen-tellen parent="einde-van-de-gisting" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-tss-measurement source.awri-residual-sugar-measurement"}
### The method determines what the result means

A hydrometer measures density; sample temperature and the corresponding correction matter. Compare successive values using the same measurement procedure. For residual sugar, an enzymatic analysis can specifically determine glucose and fructose. A measurement of total reducing sugars can also include non-fermentable sugars such as pentoses and therefore give a higher result. Naming the measured quantity is essential: two results labelled ‘sugar’ need not describe the same thing. [@source.awri-tss-measurement] [@source.awri-residual-sugar-measurement]
:::

## Global context — compare the must and the method

For white wine, juice clarification can remove nutrient-rich solids; for red wine, skins can continue releasing nutrients during fermentation. Australia's AWRI discusses these differences in nutrient management. Sugar concentration also changes the challenge for yeast. A nutrient decision therefore belongs to a particular must and working method, rather than to a country or grape name alone. [@source.awri-nutrient-management-2022]

UC Davis compares inoculated and uninoculated fermentations of the same Grenache must. Their curves differ, but both reach a dry endpoint. The example shows why a fixed calendar cannot adequately assess every fermentation. Conversely, AWRI aroma recommendations drawn from [[grape.chardonnay|Chardonnay]] research should not automatically be applied to every wine: the research context limits the conclusion. [@source.uc-davis-problem-fermentations] [@source.awri-yan]
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013 source.awri-restart-fermentation-2020"}
## Evidence and limits — slowing requires diagnosis

A sluggish fermentation still consumes sugar; a stuck fermentation comes to an unintended halt while fermentable sugar remains. The same flattening curve can have different causes: temperature, nutrient shortage, high sugar concentration, alcohol stress or inhibitory substances. [[concept.acidity|Acidity]] and [[concept.sulfur-dioxide|sulfur dioxide]] may also be relevant to that assessment. There is no universal alcohol level at which every yeast stops. [@source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013]

The winemaker combines the trajectory with samples and information about earlier interventions. When a problem occurs, remaining sugar, alcohol and yeast viability are among the factors assessed. Blindly warming the wine or adding nutrients does not address every cause. General diagnosis of wine spoilage extends beyond this fermentation check. [@source.awri-restart-fermentation-2020]
:::

:::detail{#herstart-en-aanpassing parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-restart-fermentation-2020"}
### Restarting requires adaptation

A restart culture enters an environment unlike fresh must: alcohol is already present, nutrient conditions have changed and the remaining sugar may contain a relatively high proportion of fructose. AWRI's rescue procedure therefore acclimatises the chosen yeast gradually before it must take over the main batch. Tracking sugar consumption determines progress. Such a procedure is a targeted response to an investigated cause, rather than a guarantee that extra yeast will rescue every stuck tank. [@source.awri-restart-fermentation-2020]
:::

:::detail{#aroma-gevormd-en-behouden parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.mouret-fermentation-strategy-2021"}
### Aroma produced differs from aroma retained

For volatile compounds, both yeast production and losses from the liquid with escaping gas matter. Temperature affects both sides. A higher concentration in the finished wine therefore does not by itself prove that yeast produced more of that compound. Research into aroma production requires a broader mass balance than a final sample alone; sensory significance then needs its own assessment. [@source.mouret-fermentation-strategy-2021]
:::

## Significance for wine — conversion alongside other cellar processes

Fermentation connects grape sugar with alcohol, remaining sweetness and part of aroma formation. Yeast strain helps determine the result alongside must and conditions. Choosing a spontaneous or selected start therefore cannot tell you on its own how the wine will taste. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation] [@source.awri-yeast-choice]

[[concept.maceration|Skin contact]] and extraction explain transfer from grape solids. [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|Malolactic conversion]] is a bacterial process that changes acidity. A [[concept.second-fermentation|second alcoholic fermentation]] uses sugar and yeast again; in Champagne the gas produced is retained in the bottle. Further [[concept.elevage|maturation]] may include [[concept.lees-ageing|lees contact]], with sediment containing yeast cells among other material. Each process works differently, even when their timing in the cellar overlaps. [@source.oiv-microbiological-deacidification] [@source.champagne-bottling-second-fermentation] [@source.awri-lees-contact-2020]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Following fermentation means distinguishing growth, sugar conversion and the intended endpoint. The winemaker manages a living population; measurements connect what yeast does with the wine being made.
:::
