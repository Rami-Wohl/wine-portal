:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation"}
Alcoholic fermentation is the step in which grape juice becomes wine. Yeast uses the grape's sugars to produce alcohol, carbon dioxide and other compounds that contribute to wine's character. Harvest supplies the material; fermentation gives it a new life. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation]
:::

:::section{#van-suiker-naar-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation source.awri-fermentation-temperature"}
## From sugar to wine

Ripe grape juice contains sugar. When yeast—a single-celled microorganism—ferments it, ethanol, the alcohol in wine, is produced. Carbon dioxide is also released, appearing as bubbles or foam. In still wine this gas escapes; bubbling must does not mean the finished wine will be sparkling. *Must* is grape juice, potentially with skins and other grape solids, before or during fermentation.

Fermentation also produces heat. A fermentation vessel is therefore more than somewhere to store juice: the winemaker follows what happens inside and may use cooling to manage temperature. Walk through a cellar during harvest and you see equipment; behind it, a living population is at work. [@source.awri-fermentation-temperature]
:::

## Yeast and temperature

A winemaker may add selected yeast or allow existing yeasts to begin the process. The latter is called **spontaneous fermentation**: no selected culture is added to initiate fermentation. Those yeasts may come from both grapes and the cellar; spontaneous does not mean the wine is made without attention or intervention. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation]

Yeast choice and temperature help shape fermentation and wine style. Different strains may perform differently and produce different aroma compounds. Yeast is more than an anonymous engine for making alcohol, but neither can a packet of yeast make every grape taste the same. [@source.awri-yeast-choice]
:::

:::detail{#een-gezonde-gisting parent="gist-en-temperatuur" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-yeast-choice source.awri-fermentation-temperature"}
### Supporting a living population

Yeast needs more than sugar: it requires usable nutrients, including nitrogen. Juice, yeast and conditions need to suit one another. Insufficient nutrition can slow or stop fermentation, and requirements differ between strains. [@source.awri-yeast-choice]

Temperature affects yeast activity, heat production and—in red wine—[[concept.extraction|extraction]], the transfer of compounds from grape solids. Warmer fermentation can increase the rate of fermentation and certain extraction processes, but is not a universal recipe for more flavour. The winemaker chooses an appropriate course, not the thermostat's maximum setting. [@source.awri-fermentation-temperature]
:::

:::detail{#spontaan-is-geen-herkomstbewijs parent="gist-en-temperatuur" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-yeast-choice"}
### More than one wild yeast

A spontaneous fermentation may begin with several yeast species and later be dominated by Saccharomyces yeasts. That is more precise than the romantic picture of one unique vineyard yeast. Spontaneous fermentation does not itself prove stronger [[concept.terroir|terroir]] expression, just as selected yeast does not prove that a wine loses its origin. Both decisions need to be judged through the process and finished wine, not a label term alone. [@source.awri-yeast-choice]
:::

:::section{#einde-van-de-gisting depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013 source.oiv-fermentation-interruption"}
## When is fermentation finished?

For dry wine, fermentable sugar is largely consumed. Sugar left behind is called *residual sugar*. A winemaker may deliberately interrupt fermentation to retain sweetness; an unintentionally stuck fermentation is a different situation. Sweet wine is not simply a failed dry wine. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation]

Deliberate interruption may involve cooling and removing yeast through suitable filtration. The remaining juice or wine still contains fermentable sugar and therefore needs protection against unwanted refermentation; cooling alone is no permanent guarantee. [@source.oiv-fermentation-interruption]

Alcohol production depends partly on available sugar and fermentation progress. A sugar-rich harvest and a dry finished wine can go together: the sugar need not remain in the glass. Conversely, alcohol alone does not tell you how much residual sugar a wine contains.
:::

:::detail{#meten-in-plaats-van-bellen-tellen parent="einde-van-de-gisting" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013"}
### Measure rather than count bubbles

The winemaker follows temperature and the decline of sugar-related measurements, among other indicators, and checks remaining sugar. Fewer visible bubbles cannot reliably replace measurement. Excessively high or low temperatures, nutrient shortage, high sugar, alcohol stress or inhibitory substances may affect progress. There is therefore no single alcohol level at which every yeast automatically stops under all conditions. [@source.awri-stuck-fermentations-2013]
:::

:::section{#andere-processen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation"}
## Related processes, different jobs

[[concept.maceration|Maceration]] is contact between juice or wine and grape skins: it does not itself create alcohol. In red wine it generally accompanies fermentation; many white wines ferment after their skins have been removed.

[[concept.malolactic-fermentation|Malolactic conversion]] turns malic acid into lactic acid, rather than repeating sugar fermentation. A [[concept.second-fermentation|second alcoholic fermentation]], by contrast, uses sugar and yeast again. Some sparkling methods retain the resulting carbon dioxide. Further [[concept.elevage|maturation]] follows, potentially including [[concept.lees-ageing|lees contact]], with sediment containing yeast cells among other material.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Fermentation produces alcohol; managing the yeast helps shape the wine. Skin contact, acid conversion and maturation each have a different job, even when they take place in the same cellar.
:::
