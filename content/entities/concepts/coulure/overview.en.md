With **coulure**, an unusually large share of flowers or very young fruitlets falls during or shortly after flowering. The bunch consequently remains conspicuously sparse and yield may decline sharply. The condition is often mentioned alongside [[concept.millerandage|millerandage]], but the distinction matters: coulure is chiefly about loss, whereas millerandage concerns berries that remain but develop unevenly. [@source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
## Overview

A grapevine always sheds some flowers and newly set fruit after flowering. That is normal selection. Coulure describes abscission severe enough that an inflorescence retains relatively few berries. The French word evokes flowing or falling away; *shatter* is often used in English.

There is no fixed berry count at which coulure begins. Cultivars differ in flower number, natural set and bunch architecture. Coulure therefore describes departure from the starting point and normal response of that vine, not every naturally loose bunch. [@source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010]
:::

:::section{#werking depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009"}
## How it works

After capfall, pollination, fertilisation and early fruit growth must follow one another successfully. If a flower is not fertilised, seed and fruit development fails to start adequately, or the young fruit is not sustained, an abscission layer forms and it drops. With coulure, this affects a larger share than in ordinary partial fruit set.

The shed organs leave empty positions along the rachis. Those that remain may continue to grow normally. Coulure is therefore neither a fungal disease nor the name for small berries: severe loss is the defining feature. The whole chain is explained under [[concept.flowering-fruit-set-yield|flowering, fruit set and yield formation]].
:::

:::detail{#meerdere-routes-naar-uitval parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009 source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
### Several routes can lead to the same sparse bunch

Poor pollen germination, incomplete fertilisation, weak seed development and limited assimilate availability can all restrict retention of young fruit. Strong competing shoot growth around flowering may also be relevant. These routes cannot be read from the final bunch silhouette alone.

Coulure is consequently an observed outcome, not a complete diagnosis. Two equally sparse bunches may have arisen through different mechanisms and require different explanations.
:::

:::figure{#bloem-tot-coulure depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.flowering-fruit-set-yield.flower-to-set-outcomes"}
**Coulure within the route from flower to young bunch**

1. **Closed flower:** the green flower cap encloses the reproductive organs.
2. **Capfall:** stamens and pistil become exposed for pollination.
3. **Route to fertilisation:** a pollen tube may grow towards the ovules.
4. **Young berry:** after successful development, the ovary begins to swell.
5. **Ordinary partial fruit set:** some young berries remain and grow evenly.
6. **Coulure:** an above-normal share of flowers or fruitlets drops, leaving empty positions on the bunch framework.
7. **Millerandage for comparison:** fruit remains partly attached but differs strongly in development and size.

The image shows the recognisable distinction between loss and uneven retention. The physiological route that caused coulure must be inferred from observations of flowering, vine condition and circumstances.
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009 source.plantgrape-merlot"}
## Conditions and variation

Susceptibility differs by cultivar. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] is a familiar example, but even within one cultivar the outcome varies by clone, site and year. Cool conditions may delay flowering and pollen-tube growth; cloud may constrain carbon supply; imbalance in water, nutrition, leaf function or shoot growth may place additional strain on young fruit. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

“Poor weather during flowering” is therefore not a complete explanation. Temperature, light, rain and wind do not always coincide, and their effect depends on the stage of the flowers and condition of the vine.
:::

:::section{#toepassing-en-keuzes depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010 source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009"}
## Application and decisions

Sound assessment starts before loss occurs: how large were the inflorescences, how evenly did capfall progress, and how were shoots growing? After set, berry number, empty positions and distribution within representative bunches are recorded. Weather and management records then provide context for the pattern.

Management addresses the likely weak link and future seasons. A balanced canopy, suitable water and nutrient provision, and cultivar or clone choice may influence risk, but no intervention corrects every cause or flowering season.
:::

:::detail{#beheer-is-contextafhankelijk parent="toepassing-en-keuzes" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
### Cause and scale come before intervention

One thin bunch is not yet a vineyard problem. A grower compares rows, shoot positions and years, and asks whether the pattern coincides with frost damage, excessive vegetative growth, a short weather window or a susceptible cultivar. Only then is it possible to decide whether pruning, canopy work, nutrition, water management or plant material offers a useful future lever.
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
## Evidence and limits

Research may use a coulure index: the share of flowers that did not become retained berries. This requires the initial flower number. A low berry count without that denominator is ambiguous, because an inflorescence with few flowers may have a normal set percentage and still produce a small bunch.
:::

:::detail{#tellen-zonder-schijnzekerheid parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
### Counting without false precision

The result changes with measurement date and categories. A very young fruitlet may still drop; a small retained berry may later be classified under millerandage. Comparisons become dependable only when sampling, timing and definitions match. Coulure as an end result also does not prove which step—pollination, fertilisation, seed development or early assimilate supply—was decisive.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
## Significance for wine

Severe coulure reduces berries per bunch and often total yield. The remaining berries have more space and may grow differently from those in a compact bunch, but the effect on ripening also depends on leaf area, water, bunch number and the rest of the season. The term chiefly helps explain **why** a site carries little fruit; by itself it does not predict the resulting wine.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Coulure is unusually severe loss around fruit set. A sparse bunch is the visible outcome; its cause must be sought across flower number, flowering progress, vine condition and circumstances together.
:::
