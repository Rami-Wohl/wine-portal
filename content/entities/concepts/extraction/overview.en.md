:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-maceration"}
Extraction is the transfer of compounds from solid material into juice or wine. In a fermentation vessel, these come mainly from skins, seeds and any stems; wood may also release compounds later. The task is to choose what the wine needs, not to take as much as possible from the grape. [@source.oiv-maceration]
:::

:::section{#wat-overgaat depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-maceration"}
## What is transferred?

In red wine, extraction contributes colour and [[concept.tannin|tannins]], compounds that can produce a drying or grippy mouthfeel. Aroma compounds and their precursors can also be released from grape material. The winemaker is therefore working on several properties together: a dark wine is not automatically the most tannic wine.

[[concept.maceration|Maceration]] is contact between liquid and grape solids; extraction is what passes into the liquid. Pressing applies pressure to separate liquid from solids. It can affect composition, but extraction also happens without pressing, during skin contact itself. [@source.oiv-maceration]
:::

:::section{#sturen-in-de-kuip depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wre-cap-management"}
## Managing the tank

During red [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]], a cap of skins floats above the liquid. A pump-over—*remontage*—returns juice or wine from the bottom of the tank over the skins. A punch-down—*pigeage*—pushes skins into the liquid. Both renew contact and help change distribution within the vessel. [@source.wre-cap-management]

The question is not just how often work takes place, but how intensely, when and with which grapes. A fruit-led red may benefit from a different approach to a wine with a pronounced tannin structure. Neither is inherently better.
:::

:::detail{#tijd-temperatuur-en-alcohol parent="sturen-in-de-kuip" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-fermentation-temperature source.awri-extended-maceration-2019"}
### Time, temperature and alcohol interact

Transfer is influenced by contact time, temperature and changing alcohol level. Compounds do not all dissolve equally easily under the same conditions. Warmer fermentation may increase the rate of certain extraction processes; for seed-derived compounds, the final amount may also change. “Warmer means more” is therefore too blunt an explanation. [@source.awri-fermentation-temperature]

Extra contact after fermentation may also differ from extra movement during it. Available compounds and their accessibility vary between grapes and seasons. The winemaker therefore judges the batch itself, rather than treating a fixed schedule as a flavour guarantee. [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019]
:::

## Colour, bitterness and mouthfeel

Colour, bitterness and astringency are different perceptions. Seeing a very dark wine does not tell you how supple it feels. Grapes supply different colour and structural compounds, and cellar choices do not affect them all in the same way.

White wine also involves extraction. Skin contact can change flavour and mouthfeel, but excessive or unsuitable contact can introduce unwanted bitterness or astringency—the drying sensation in the mouth. Extraction is not a synonym for powerful red wine. [@source.awri-skin-contact]
:::

:::detail{#kleurstof-is-geen-tanninemeter parent="kleur-en-mondgevoel" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-fermentation-temperature source.awri-extended-maceration-2019"}
### Different compounds, different curves

Colour compounds do not necessarily reach their extraction maximum at the same time as tannins. Nor does the amount dissolved tell the whole story: binding, reaction and precipitation change what remains measurable and perceptible later. A decline in free colour compounds is not automatically a decline in all persistent red colour. [@source.awri-fermentation-temperature] [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019]

This limits the simple picture in which every pump-over adds a fixed dose of colour and tannin. Extraction and further evolution influence one another; they are not independent sliders.
:::

:::section{#van-pers-tot-hout depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-maceration source.oiv-oak-maturation"}
## From press wine to wood

After draining a red fermentation vessel, the remaining material is pressed. The resulting *press wine* may differ in composition from free-run wine. The winemaker can assess lots separately and use [[concept.assemblage|blending]]—combining selected wines—to decide what suits the whole. Press wine is not waste, but neither must it always be included.

During [[concept.elevage|élevage]], the management of young wine towards bottling, extraction from wood may contribute aroma and structure. That is a different source from grape skins. A wine with little skin contact can therefore still acquire oak influence; a concentrated red need not see new wood. [@source.oiv-oak-maturation]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Good extraction is appropriate extraction. It supports fruit and intended structure; more colour, tannin or movement does not itself prove quality.
:::
