A glass of wine brings together grapes, fermentation and cellar work. Water and alcohol form its liquid base; acids, sugars, pigments, aroma compounds and larger molecules give the mixture its properties. Understanding what is present starts with three questions: where does a substance come from, what happens to it, and which other substances does it interact with?
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-wine-composition source.awri-flavour-precursors"}
## Overview — a mixture with a history

**Wine composition** describes the substances present in wine, their amounts and their forms. Grapes supply the starting material. [[concept.fermentation|Fermentation]] produces new substances; [[concept.extraction|extraction]] transfers constituents from solid grape parts into the liquid. Ageing and treatment change the mixture further.

This page connects the families of substances. [[concept.acidity|Acidity]], [[concept.tannin|tannin]], [[concept.methoxypyrazines|methoxypyrazines]] and [[concept.volatile-thiols|volatile thiols]] each have a page for their detailed explanation. Chemical analysis describes wine from a different perspective than a tasting note: aroma, taste and texture emerge when the whole is experienced.
:::

## Structure and relationships — the main families of substances

Use this map to place a constituent in the whole. These groups have different roles and sometimes overlap: a mannoprotein, for example, contains both sugar chains and protein. [@source.wine-polysaccharides-review-2019; §3]

| Constituent | Origin and role | Explore further |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Mainly from grapes; the largest part of the liquid base | Water and ethanol together form the medium containing other substances |
| Ethanol | Produced by sugar fermentation; contributes to alcohol content and mouthfeel | [[concept.fermentation]] |
| Acids | Grapes supply tartaric and malic acids among others; transformations alter the acid profile | [[concept.acidity]] |
| Sugars | Grape glucose and fructose are largely fermented or partly retained | [[concept.sweet-wines]] |
| Phenolic compounds | Include tannins and pigments from grape parts; oak can also contribute phenols | [[concept.tannin]] and [[concept.extraction]] |
| Aroma compounds and their precursors | Come from grapes or are formed and changed during winemaking | [[concept.methoxypyrazines]] and [[concept.volatile-thiols]] |
| Polysaccharides | Larger sugar chains from grapes and yeast; interact with other wine constituents | [[concept.lees-ageing]] |
| Proteins | Particularly relevant to white wine stability; some can form haze | [[concept.bottling]] |
| Minerals and salts | Dissolved elements such as potassium and calcium; involved in pH and crystal formation | Acidity and stability analysis |
| Dissolved gases | CO₂ from fermentation or addition; oxygen enters during wine handling among other routes | [[concept.sparkling-wines]] and [[concept.oxidation]] |

Phenols are a broad family: tannins and red grape pigments, the anthocyanins, belong to different groups. [@source.uc-davis-wine-tannin] [@source.uc-davis-wine-anthocyanins] Minerals here are dissolved chemical constituents, rather than pieces of rock in wine. Their presence and origins are investigated analytically. [@source.uc-davis-wine-minerals]

**Glycerol** is an alcohol distinct from ethanol and is also produced through yeast activity. A glycerol concentration alone cannot explain a wine's texture; ethanol, phenols and larger molecules also belong to that question. [@source.uc-davis-wine-glycerol]
:::

## How it works — carrying over, transforming and developing

**Grapes supply a starting point.** Juice contains water, sugar and acids; solid parts supply phenols and aromatic material among other constituents. [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|Berry development and ripeness]] help explain why that supply differs at harvest. Methoxypyrazines are an example of aroma compounds that can already be present in grapes. Many important Sauvignon thiols, by contrast, are released from precursors during fermentation. [@source.uc-davis-wine-pyrazines] [@source.uc-davis-volatile-thiols]

**Microorganisms change the supply.** Yeast converts sugar into ethanol and CO₂ and also produces substances including glycerol. [@source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation] [@source.uc-davis-wine-glycerol] [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|Malolactic fermentation]] changes the acid profile further. This is a separate process from alcoholic fermentation. [@source.awri-mlf-style-2020]

**The cellar route continues to matter.** Extraction, [[concept.lees-ageing|lees contact]], blending and other treatments alter the substances and forms that remain. During lees contact, constituents of yeast cells can be released into wine. [@source.awri-lees-contact-2020] Wine is therefore more than a diluted or concentrated reflection of grapes.
:::

:::detail{#vrij-en-gebonden parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-sulfur-dioxide source.awri-acidity-ph"}
### Chemical form helps determine activity

A total concentration does not always reveal how much of a substance is active at a given moment. [[concept.sulfur-dioxide|Sulfur dioxide]] exists in free and bound forms. Within the free fraction, the proportion of the molecular form important for antimicrobial action depends on pH. The same total SO₂ concentration can therefore represent a different level of protection. [@source.awri-sulfur-dioxide]

pH describes the acidity of the chemical environment; titratable acidity describes the amount of acid measured by a particular titration. The balance of acids and salts matters. Measuring both gives more insight than treating either figure as a substitute for the other. [@source.awri-acidity-ph]
:::

:::figure{#druif-gisting-en-rijping depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.wine-composition.grape-fermentation-and-ageing" source_refs="source.oiv-alcoholic-fermentation source.awri-lees-contact-2020"}
**Three moments in a changing mixture**

1. **Grapes and juice:** the raw material already contains water, sugar, acids and other constituents; juice and solid parts offer different supplies.
2. **Fermentation:** yeast converts some of the sugar; gas can escape through the airlock while new substances form in the liquid.
3. **The subsequent cellar route:** wine is shown above settled lees. Contact with those lees is one possible ageing choice that further influences composition.
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-gas-adjustment source.awri-cold-stabilisation source.awri-heat-stability source.wine-polysaccharides-review-2019"}
## Conditions and variation — the mixture shapes what happens

Temperature and pressure influence how much gas remains dissolved. Alcohol content and other wine constituents also alter solubility. Dissolved CO₂ in still wine and effervescence in sparkling wine therefore belong to the same physical question under different conditions. [@source.awri-gas-adjustment]

Stability likewise involves substances and conditions together. Potassium and tartaric acid can form potassium hydrogen tartrate, which precipitates as crystals. Some proteins can cause haze, especially visible in white wine. Crystal stability and protein stability each require their own checks. [@source.awri-cold-stabilisation] [@source.awri-heat-stability]

Polysaccharides can interact with tannins, proteins and other particles. Their origin and molecular structure matter alongside their amount. More of one group therefore does not invariably produce the same mouthfeel or stability. [@source.wine-polysaccharides-review-2019; §§1–3]
:::

:::section{#keuzes-en-afwegingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-fermentation-temperature source.awri-lees-contact-2020 source.awri-heat-stability"}
## Decisions and trade-offs — one intervention affects several parts

Harvest selects the raw material; pressing and skin contact help determine which parts reach the wine. During fermentation, more than sugar changes. Temperature also influences yeast activity and the transfer of colour and tannin compounds in red wine. Fermentation and extraction explain these choices in detail. [@source.awri-fermentation-temperature]

Lees contact can release larger molecules while changing other interactions in the wine. The maker therefore assesses how the batch develops, rather than pursuing just one desired concentration. [@source.awri-lees-contact-2020]

Before bottling, the question becomes whether the chosen mixture can maintain its condition. Protein treatment, for example, may be needed to limit later haze; a stability test investigates the particular wine. [[concept.bottling|Bottling]] connects those checks with gas management, microbial stability and packaging. [@source.awri-heat-stability]
:::

## Global context — the same map, different proportions

**Sauvignon Blanc, including wines from New Zealand**, illustrates the role of aroma precursors and thiols. These substances also occur in other grape varieties. The general mechanism therefore belongs to more than one country or variety. [@source.uc-davis-volatile-thiols]

In [[concept.fortified-wines|fortified wines]], alcohol addition, retained sugar and ageing each help explain a different aspect. For [[concept.sweet-wines|sweet wines]], the first question concerns the sugar route; for [[concept.sparkling-wines|sparkling wines]], it concerns the origin and retention of CO₂. The family pages connect those questions with particular wines.

**Australian research** shows why a reference value needs a defined scope. An AWRI overview used approximately 10,000 red and white wines analysed between 2016 and 2020, plus a separate survey of 60 commercial wines for glycerol. That distribution is not a universal standard for every country or wine type. [@source.awri-wine-composition-survey-2021] In an AWRI lees-contact trial with Chardonnay from 2019, malolactic conversion also occurred. The observed texture could therefore not be attributed to lees contact alone: the grape, batch and procedure remain necessary context for understanding the result. [@source.awri-lees-contact-2020]
:::

## Evidence and limits — each figure answers a question

A technical sheet becomes more useful when the measured property, unit and sampling point are clear:

| Information | What it helps describe | What is still needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alcohol in % vol | The volume proportion of alcohol in wine | Other constituents and an assessment of mouthfeel |
| Glucose and fructose in g/L | The measured amount of these sugars | Method, wine category and tasting context |
| pH | The acidity of the chemical environment | Acid profile and titratable acidity |
| Titratable acidity, with its unit | The result of a specified acid titration | The reporting basis and pH |
| Free and total SO₂ | Different fractions of the protective substance | pH, wine history and sampling point |
| Stability test | Behaviour under the conditions tested | The particular instability investigated |

pH and titratable acidity have no fixed, predictable relationship. A high or low figure gains meaning alongside the other measurement. [@source.awri-acidity-ph] Similarly, a successful protein test answers a different question than a test for tartrate crystals. [@source.awri-heat-stability] [@source.awri-cold-stabilisation]

Some familiar explanations overstate the role of one constituent. Glycerol is measurably present, but that does not establish that its concentration causes a creamy texture. Tasting and chemical measurement complement each other. [@source.uc-davis-wine-glycerol]
:::

:::detail{#wat-een-meetmethode-telt parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-residual-sugar-measurement source.awri-tss-measurement"}
### Two sugar figures can count different things

An enzymatic assay can specifically measure glucose and fructose. Some classical reducing-sugar methods also count other reducing sugars, including pentoses that are not considered fermentable. Those results are therefore not directly interchangeable. [@source.awri-residual-sugar-measurement; Reaction/titration; Enzymatic assay]

Brix and related must scales use density or refractive-index measurements with calibration and temperature correction. This differs from specifically measuring the glucose and fructose remaining in wine. Record whether the sample is grape juice, fermenting must or finished wine, and which method was used. [@source.awri-tss-measurement]
:::

## Significance for wine — read from raw material to glass

Start with **origin**: what did the grape supply and what arose through fermentation or cellar treatment? Then ask about **state**: what amount and chemical form are present at the sampling point? Connect that with **conditions** such as pH, temperature, gas management and ageing.

A tasting note offers the next perspective. Aromatic and non-volatile constituents interact; a single chemical name cannot fully describe the experience. [@source.awri-flavour-precursors] A technical sheet thus helps frame focused questions about wine. Judging quality additionally requires an assessment of the whole.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Follow constituents through their history: grapes, fermentation and the subsequent cellar route. Their amounts, forms and surrounding mixture explain more than a list of substances on its own.
:::
