A vineyard does not live by the calendar on the wall. Growers look at the vine itself: closed buds, the first green tissue, falling flower caps, softening berries and changing leaves. **Phenology** is the language of these recurring developmental stages. It reveals where the grapevine is in its annual cycle — and therefore which opportunities and vulnerabilities matter at that moment. [@source.awri-grapevine-growth-stages] [@source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology source.awri-grapevine-growth-stages"}
## Overview

Each year the grapevine follows a recognisable route: winter dormancy, renewed sap flow, budburst, shoot growth, flowering, fruit set, berry growth, véraison, ripening, harvest, leaf senescence and a return to rest. These words describe what can be observed on the plant. They do not impose a fixed date or suggest that every bud or berry changes at once. [@source.awri-grapevine-growth-stages]

Phenology therefore gives growers and researchers a shared reference point. “10 May” means something different in a warm and a cool year; “50% flowering” identifies a comparable stage. From such an observation, they can discuss frost exposure, disease susceptibility, vineyard work and progress towards harvest much more precisely.

The cycle also has two tracks. The **vegetative track** includes roots, shoots, leaves and wood. The **reproductive track** runs from inflorescence formation inside a bud to flowers, berries and ripe grapes. Both unfold together and connect this year’s crop with next year’s growth and fruitfulness. This interaction builds on the [[concept.vine-as-living-system|grapevine as a living system]]. [@source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology]
:::

## Structure and relationships — the annual cycle

In winter the bare vine looks still, yet it lives on stored carbohydrates and continues to respire at a low rate. As soil and air warm, water transport is restored. Drops of xylem sap may then appear at fresh pruning wounds: grapevine **bleeding**. Buds subsequently swell, their scales separate and the first green leaf tips become visible at **budburst**. [@source.awri-bud-dormancy-budburst] [@source.sperry-spring-xylem-filling-1987]

Shoot growth now accelerates. Leaves unfold, tendrils seek support and compact inflorescences become visible. During **flowering**, the small green grape flowers shed their caps. After fertilisation, set fruitlets develop into hard green berries. Size and structure increase markedly during this first growth period.

At **véraison**, the transition to ripening begins: berries soften and coloured varieties begin to change colour; white varieties change visibly and physiologically too. Ripening then continues until grower and winemaker consider the fruit suitable for the intended wine. **Harvest** is consequently a human decision within a biological process, not an automatic finishing switch. [@source.awri-berry-ripening]

After picking, healthy leaves may continue photosynthesising. Shoots continue to mature into brown canes, reserves are renewed and leaves senesce before they fall. Once the vine is bare again, it does not start from nothing: buds, wood and reserves carry the previous season into the next budburst.
:::

:::detail{#twee-seizoenen parent="de-jaarcyclus" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-flowers source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology"}
### One crop begins across two growing seasons

While this year’s flowers and young berries are visible, structures for next year’s shoots and potential inflorescences are already forming in young buds. After winter, the individual flower parts develop further and the new inflorescence emerges on a new shoot. The reproductive journey from bud formation to harvested bunch therefore spans parts of two growing seasons. [@source.cornell-grapevine-flowers] [@source.nmsu-grapevine-phenology]

This two-season perspective explains why light, vine balance or damage in year one may later be reflected in bunch number and development in year two. Flower biology and yield formation deserve their own deeper explanation; here the important point is that the boundary between calendar years is not a hard biological divide.
:::

:::figure{#jaarlijkse-cyclus depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.grapevine-phenology.annual-cycle"}
**The annual grapevine cycle — twelve recognisable stages**

1. **Winter dormancy:** the vine is leafless; closed buds and permanent wood bridge the cold or dry resting season.
2. **Bleeding:** positive xylem pressure may make sap drip from a fresh pruning wound as the root zone becomes active again.
3. **Budburst:** bud scales open and the first green leaf tips appear.
4. **Shoot growth:** new shoots extend, leaves unfold and inflorescences become visible.
5. **Flowering:** small green flowers shed their caps; pollination and fertilisation can take place.
6. **Fruit set:** fertilised flowers develop into small green berries; flowers that did not set are shed.
7. **Green berry growth:** hard green berries increase in size while seeds and fruit tissues develop.
8. **Véraison:** berries soften and change appearance; the transition proceeds unevenly within a bunch.
9. **Ripening:** sugars, acids, water, aroma-related and phenolic compounds continue developing in changing proportions.
10. **Harvest:** picking time follows from fruit condition, intended wine, weather and practical trade-offs.
11. **Leaf senescence and fall:** chlorophyll breaks down, leaves change colour and the active canopy disappears.
12. **Cane maturation and reserve renewal:** shoots become woody and carbohydrates are stored in roots and permanent wood for maintenance and new growth.

The circle orders the principal stages. They overlap in a real vine: cane maturation and storage begin before harvest, while buds and berries across a vineyard do not all reach the same stage together.
:::

## How it works — timing and overlap

A phenological stage has an onset, a spread and an end. A bud on the sunny side of a vine may open sooner; within a bunch, one berry may be colouring while another remains green. At vineyard level, an observer may therefore record first flowering, half flowering or the point at which an agreed share of berries has reached véraison. The stage describes a population, not an imaginary uniform plant.

Processes also run alongside each other. Shoots continue growing as flowering approaches. New leaves gradually change from net users to net exporters of assimilates. Berries grow while buds for the following season are being formed. After harvest, the canopy may still contribute to reserves while leaves remain healthy and active. The orderly sequence is therefore a reading route through a system with overlap.
:::

:::detail{#van-rust-naar-groei parent="timing-en-overlap" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-bud-dormancy-budburst source.sperry-spring-xylem-filling-1987"}
### From bud dormancy to active growth

Bud dormancy is not a single on–off state. A newly formed bud is first restrained by the rest of the growing vine, then enters a deeper internal dormancy, and may later remain closed mainly because outside temperatures are unsuitable. In many temperate regions that last phase governs much of winter. Once sufficient warmth becomes available, the preformed shoot can unfold. [@source.awri-bud-dormancy-budburst]

Few mature leaves are available during the earliest growth. The vine uses stored material from roots, trunk and arms. Positive root pressure can meanwhile help restore water in xylem vessels; at open pruning wounds this becomes visible as bleeding. As the canopy develops, transpiration assumes the main role in water movement and the new leaves supply an increasing share of carbon. [@source.sperry-spring-xylem-filling-1987]
:::

:::detail{#warmte-en-modellen parent="timing-en-overlap" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.cameron-temperature-phenology-2022 source.parker-variety-phenology-2013"}
### Winter chill, spring warmth and model thresholds

Phenology models often summarise development as a rest or chill component followed by accumulated warmth — **forcing** — until a stage is reached. This is a useful approach, but the chosen starting date, temperature threshold and cultivar parameters all shape the result. A single formula is therefore not a universal biological clock. [@source.parker-variety-phenology-2013]

Intervals also respond differently. Research across several Australian vineyards found that the period from budburst to flowering changed more strongly with spring temperature than later intervals, with different responses among cultivars. A warmer season does not necessarily move every stage forward by exactly the same number of days. [@source.cameron-temperature-phenology-2022]
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cameron-temperature-phenology-2022 source.parker-variety-phenology-2013 source.oiv-climate-variability-2015"}
## Conditions and variation

Cultivars have recognisable relative profiles: some burst early, while others flower or ripen later. Yet “early” is always a comparison within an environment. Temperature, elevation, latitude, soil water, root-zone conditions, vine health and [[concept.vintage|the course of the season]] all move the actual dates. [@source.parker-variety-phenology-2013]

Weather affects more than speed. Frost shortly after budburst may damage young tissue. Cool, wet or windy weather around flowering may disrupt flowering and fruit set. Heat and water shortage later change shoot growth, leaf function and berry development. Later tickets give these effects their full mechanistic treatment; in phenological terms, they chiefly determine **when** a vulnerable stage coincides with a hazard.

Long records show that budburst, flowering, véraison and harvest have advanced in many regions during warmer decades. The consequences depend on the whole seasonal pattern: an earlier stage may encounter different temperatures, frost windows or heat exposure than before. Climate trends are therefore tracked across several stages, cultivars and years rather than by one early harvest date. [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015]
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs

Phenology turns a calendar action into a targeted decision. Pruning takes place during dormancy, but timing may influence budburst and thus exposure to spring frost. What the canopy needs changes through shoot growth and flowering. During berry growth and ripening, attention shifts towards water, exposure, health and the approaching harvest window.

The correct stage also matters for observation. A loose inflorescence demands a different disease or weather assessment from a closed, growing bunch. A colouring berry is not the same as a harvest-ready berry. By first naming what the plant is doing, a grower can better define the purpose of an intervention and the other processes it may affect at the same time.
:::

:::detail{#oogst-en-naoogst parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.hall-postharvest-phenology-2016 source.awri-berry-ripening"}
### Harvest and the period afterwards

Harvest ends on-vine development for picked fruit, but not the plant’s season at once. Desired grape composition, fruit condition, the weather outlook, labour and intended wine together determine picking time. The complete balance between technological, aromatic and phenolic ripeness belongs in the separate explanation of berry development and ripeness. [@source.awri-berry-ripening]

After picking, a healthy canopy may continue producing carbohydrates. Particularly where sufficient warmth and leaf duration remain, this post-harvest period is important for replenishing reserves in permanent wood and roots. Early leaf damage or rapid leaf fall shortens that window. [@source.hall-postharvest-phenology-2016]
:::

## Global context

In the Northern Hemisphere, budburst usually occurs in local spring and harvest later in the same calendar year; in the Southern Hemisphere, the same biological stages are shifted by roughly half a year. Month names are therefore poor universal explanations. The sequence of organs and processes travels better.

Nor does the classic picture of a long cold winter fit everywhere. Under warm subtropical conditions, bud dormancy may be incomplete or uneven, and achieving a uniform new cycle can require deliberate management. In very cool regions, winter survival and a short growing period instead set sharp boundaries. [@source.lavee-grapevine-bud-dormancy-1997]

The period between harvest and leaf fall varies too: in warmer regions a long active post-harvest phase may remain, while cold or early frost quickly closes the leaf window elsewhere. The same number in the cycle thus receives a local date and duration while retaining its biological meaning. [@source.hall-postharvest-phenology-2016]
:::

## Evidence and limits — measurement and comparison

Systems such as the modified Eichhorn–Lorenz scale assign codes to visible stages: from winter bud through budburst, flowering and fruit set to véraison, harvest maturity and leaf fall. This lets a researcher use the same definition across vineyards and years. The code is an observation convention; the plant does not know its number. [@source.awri-grapevine-growth-stages]

Comparisons consequently require the same definition and a clear scale. “Beginning of flowering” and “50% flowering” are different observations. A single vine, a representative row and a vineyard average are not interchangeable either. Date series become meaningful only when cultivar, place, stage definition and measurement method are known.
:::

:::detail{#model-is-geen-kalender parent="meten-en-vergelijken" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.parker-variety-phenology-2013 source.cameron-temperature-phenology-2022"}
### A model predicts within its calibration

A phenology model can predict a likely stage from temperature data and cultivar parameters. This supports planning and climate analysis, especially when tested against local observations. Uncertainty increases when a model is transferred to another variety, climate, training system or exceptional season. [@source.parker-variety-phenology-2013]

The most useful question is therefore not merely “which date does the model give?”, but also: which stage has been defined, which data calibrated the model, how wide is the spread and which processes — such as water status or crop load — are absent from the simplification?
:::

## Significance for wine

Phenology does not directly state how a wine will taste. It describes the conditions under which the raw material passed through successive developmental stages. Early budburst may alter the frost window; early véraison may move ripening into a warmer part of summer; a late harvest may offer further development while extending exposure to rain, heat or disease.

“An early year” therefore becomes meaningful only alongside the cultivar, place and stage being discussed. Combined with weather, vine balance and harvest objective, phenology helps explain why two [[concept.vintage|vintages]] required different decisions. It is the timeline on which later knowledge of flowering, ripeness, climate and vineyard management can be placed.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Phenology reads the season from the grapevine itself. Its stages follow a recognisable order, but their date, duration and overlap emerge anew from cultivar, place, weather, vine condition and human decisions.
:::
