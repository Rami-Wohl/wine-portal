:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-vintage-labelling-2023"}
A **vintage** is the year in which a wine's grapes were harvested. The year on a bottle is therefore neither the bottling year nor a quality score. It tells you where the grapes' story begins in time; to understand the wine you also need its origin, grape variety and the maker's choices. [@source.oiv-vintage-labelling-2023]
:::

:::section{#jaar-op-etiket depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-vintage-labelling-2023"}
## The year on the label

If a label says 2022, it refers to the 2022 harvest, even if the wine was bottled or sold years later. It is a simple clue with a great deal behind it: wine begins with grapes grown in a particular season. [@source.oiv-vintage-labelling-2023]

Do not read the date as a promise that literally every grape came from that one year. Labelling rules determine when a harvest year may appear. The international OIV standard permits a limited share of grapes from other years; the applicable rules of the country and origin designation still matter. [@source.oiv-vintage-labelling-2023]
:::

## A season leaves its mark

A vine does not receive the same spring, summer or weeks before harvest every year. Heat, sunshine, rain and drought affect grape growth and ripening. Frost or hail can also limit the crop. The same vineyard can therefore produce grapes with a different balance of ripeness and freshness in two years. [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015]

That makes vintages fascinating, but not simple. Rain on the wrong day need not be equally damaging everywhere, and a warm summer is not automatically a success. Place, grape and vineyard work all shape a season's outcome. Here vintage meets [[concept.terroir|terroir]]: one points to a particular harvest year, the other to the interplay of place and practice. [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015] [@source.oiv-terroir-definition-2010]
:::

:::detail{#groeiseizoen parent="seizoen-in-de-druif" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-climate-variability-2015"}
### More than the weather at harvest

A year's influence begins well before picking. Conditions around budbreak and flowering affect how much fruit develops; warmth and water during ripening influence grape composition. The choice of picking date matters too. A vintage is therefore not a snapshot of one September day, but the outcome of a whole growing season and the grower's responses to it. [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015]
:::

:::section{#lezen-zonder-ranglijst depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-climate-variability-2015 source.champagne-non-vintage-brut"}
## Not a ranking in itself

‘Good year’ sounds attractively simple, but which wine are we talking about? A wet spell can affect vineyards and grapes differently even within one region. A careful producer can make a lovely wine in a difficult year; favourable weather cannot replace good work. Nor can the date tell you how a bottle has been stored since. [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015]

Not every wine carries a vintage. Many non-vintage Champagnes bring together wines from different harvests to achieve a desired house style. That is no hidden flaw, but a deliberate form of [[concept.assemblage|blending]]. [@source.champagne-non-vintage-brut]
:::

:::detail{#schaal-van-oordeel parent="lezen-zonder-ranglijst" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.oiv-climate-variability-2015"}
### Narrower places, sharper questions

A vintage chart for a whole country or large region compresses many different experiences. The OIV, by contrast, points to climatic differences between region, vineyard and even the vine's immediate surroundings. It is more revealing to compare one wine or producer across several years, or different producers from a limited origin, than to treat every bottle with the same year alike. Vintage then becomes a way to investigate differences, not a final verdict. [@source.oiv-climate-variability-2015]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
A vintage places a harvest in time. The year shapes the grapes, but place and human choices also determine what eventually reaches the bottle.
:::
