After fruit set, a grape begins as a small, hard and acidic green berry. By harvest, that same berry may be soft, sweet and aromatic, with skin and seeds that have each followed their own development. **Berry development and ripeness** concerns those connected changes — and the human judgement of when the fruit suits a particular wine. Véraison is not a magical switch date, and ripeness is not a single number. [@source.awri-berry-ripening] [@source.coombe-mccarthy-berry-growth-2000]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-berry-ripening source.coombe-mccarthy-berry-growth-2000"}
## Overview

Within the [[concept.grapevine-phenology|grapevine's annual cycle]], this process forms the bridge between fruit set and harvest.

Berry development starts where [[concept.flowering-fruit-set-yield|flowering and fruit set]] end. The young green berry first grows while seeds, skin and pulp form. Visible expansion then slows during a **lag phase**. Around **véraison**, the transition to ripening begins: the berry softens, sugar import and storage increase sharply and coloured cultivars develop skin colour. White cultivars pass through the same transition without turning purple.

A whole bunch does not switch at once. Hard green, softening and already coloured berries may hang beside each other. Véraison is therefore a period across a population of berries. Harvest is not an automatic biological endpoint either: grower and winemaker choose when composition, flavour, health, weather and intended wine style align closely enough. [@source.awri-berry-ripening]
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-berry-ripening source.gouot-grape-secondary-metabolites-2021"}
## Structure and relationships — different tissues, different paths

A grape berry is more than juice. The **skin** protects the berry and contains, among other things, pigments, aroma and flavour compounds and many of the phenolics that red-wine maceration may extract. The **pulp** contains abundant water, glucose and fructose, organic acids and dissolved components. **Seeds** develop inside and contain oils and phenolics. Vascular bundles connect the berry through its pedicel to the [[concept.vine-as-living-system|rest of the vine]].

These components change at the same time, but not in the same way. Sugar may rise quickly while malic acid falls; the skin may gain colour while seeds continue to develop; aroma compounds or their odourless precursors follow cultivar-dependent paths. “The berry ripens” is therefore a useful summary of several processes, not a description of one substance directing everything else. [@source.gouot-grape-secondary-metabolites-2021]
:::

:::detail{#schil-vruchtvlees-en-zaden parent="opbouw-en-samenhang" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gouot-grape-secondary-metabolites-2021 source.fredes-carmenere-seed-maturity-2010"}
### Skin, pulp and seeds provide different information

Juice may be central to white-wine production, whereas skin contact in red, rosé and orange wines has much more influence on which compounds reach the must. That distinction does not arise only in the cellar: skin thickness and condition, pigment content, tannin types and aroma or flavour precursors are already properties of the harvested berry. [[concept.tannin|Tannin]] is not one uniform reserve either; skins and seeds contribute different fractions that become available differently during processing.

Seed colour and texture can provide additional clues, but they are not a universal ripeness meter. Research on Carménère found relationships between seed appearance and aspects of phenolic development without one visible seed class explaining all chemical variation. Tasting, crushing and measurement therefore complement each other. [@source.fredes-carmenere-seed-maturity-2010]
:::

:::section{#werking depth="foundation" source_refs="source.coombe-mccarthy-berry-growth-2000 source.awri-berry-ripening source.sweetman-malate-metabolism-2009"}
## How it works — from hard green berry to ripening fruit

Grape expansion is often described as a double-sigmoid curve. During the first growth period, cells divide and enlarge, seeds develop, and tartaric and malic acids accumulate. Berry growth slows during the intervening lag phase. A second growth period then begins alongside softening and the transition to ripening. The model describes a recognisable pattern; in real berries the boundaries are gradual and their timing and magnitude differ by cultivar and season. [@source.coombe-mccarthy-berry-growth-2000]

After ripening starts, the berry imports substantial carbon from active leaves through the phloem. Glucose and fructose accumulate especially in the pulp. Acidity generally declines at the same time. Malic acid, after accumulating earlier, is actively metabolised and consumed; tartaric acid is metabolically more stable, although its concentration can change when berry volume expands or water is lost. Total acidity, individual acids and pH therefore do not move as interchangeable versions of the same measurement. [@source.sweetman-malate-metabolism-2009]
:::

:::detail{#kleur-aroma-en-textuur parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gouot-grape-secondary-metabolites-2021 source.awri-berry-ripening"}
### Colour, aroma and texture follow their own pace

In coloured cultivars, anthocyanins begin accumulating in the skin around véraison. White cultivars do not produce these purple pigments in the same way, but also soften and become more translucent. Other phenolics may already be present before véraison and continue to change later. [[concept.tannin|Tannin]] development concerns more than quantity: extractability, tissue structure and the balance between skin and seed also matter for winemaking.

“Aromatic ripeness” is not a general molecular switch either. Cultivars build up, break down or transform different aroma-active compounds and odourless precursors. Methoxypyrazines, terpenes, thiol precursors and norisoprenoids each follow different patterns and respond differently to light, temperature, water and time. A central ripeness question for one cultivar may offer little guidance for another. [@source.gouot-grape-secondary-metabolites-2021]
:::

:::detail{#water-en-groei-zijn-geen-eenvoudige-pomp parent="werking" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.dai-grape-berry-water-carbon-fluxes-2010 source.coombe-mccarthy-berry-growth-2000"}
### Water and growth do not form a simple pump

Water and sugar transport, together with changing cell walls, help enable the second growth period. Yet the popular scheme of “xylem before véraison, phloem only afterwards” is too absolute. Relative flows through xylem and phloem, evaporation from the berry and possible backflow change during development; the xylem connection need not cease functioning structurally. [@source.dai-grape-berry-water-carbon-fluxes-2010]

A rising sugar concentration does not by itself prove how much sugar the berry has imported. Concentration may change through new sugar, volume expansion, water loss or a combination. Late in ripening, some berries may lose weight while their juice becomes more concentrated. That is a different route from undisturbed physiological ripening and becomes deliberately or inadvertently important in [[concept.late-harvest|late-harvest]] fruit.
:::

:::figure{#besontwikkeling-in-beeld depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.berry-development-ripeness.berry-development-sequence"}
**Berry development — two growth periods and a gradual transition**

1. **Early green growth:** small, hard berries begin rapid growth after fruit set; skin, pulp and seeds are established.
2. **First growth period:** berries expand substantially, remain green and acidic, and accumulate organic acids among other components.
3. **Lag phase:** visible volume growth slows while seed development and internal preparation continue.
4. **Véraison:** berries do not soften at once; in a coloured cultivar, green, pink and purple berries temporarily coexist.
5. **Ripening:** sugar storage accelerates, malic acid declines, and skin, seeds, aromas and phenolics continue along their own trajectories.
6. **Later ripening:** the berry may continue changing or lose water and weight; darker or more concentrated is not automatically more suitable for every wine.

The four bands below show, from top to bottom, simplified directions for berry size, sugar, acid and skin pigment in a coloured cultivar. They are not universal measurement curves: cultivar, individual berry and season shift each path.
:::

## Conditions and variation

Cultivar and clone set the playing field: early and late cultivars, white and coloured cultivars, thick and thin skins and different aroma and phenolic pathways do not respond identically. Vine condition, number and size of bunches, light around the berries, temperature, water availability and ripening weather then modify that starting point. Even within one block, shade, leaf area, soil water and crop load vary.

Heat can accelerate malic-acid loss, while very high temperatures may affect colour or aroma development differently from sugar accumulation. Light can stimulate some skin compounds, but strong exposure also raises berry temperature and sunburn risk. Water deficit can shift growth and composition; severe or poorly timed stress may instead constrain photosynthesis, health and ripening capacity. Every claim therefore needs timing, intensity, cultivar and location. [@source.sweetman-malate-metabolism-2009] [@source.gouot-grape-secondary-metabolites-2021]
:::

:::detail{#ongelijkheid-binnen-tros-en-perceel parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity"}
### Ripening also varies across space

A bunch contains berries on its outside and inside, top and bottom, in sun and shade, and with different starting sizes. Bunches then differ between shoots, vines, rows and parts of the block. An average can summarise those differences, but can also hide them.

That spread matters in practice. Uneven véraison may later produce a mixture of riper, less ripe, shrivelled or damaged berries. A sample containing only the most attractive outer berries then describes mainly the sampler's selection. Representative sampling is therefore part of understanding ripeness, not merely a laboratory formality. [@source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity]
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs — when is the grape suitable?

Near harvest, soluble solids are commonly measured, usually expressed as °Brix, alongside pH and titratable acidity. Together these values provide valuable information about the juice. They do not fully describe how the skin tastes, how readily colour and tannin are released, how seeds have developed, which aromas or precursors are present, or how much rot and damage occurs across the block. [@source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023]

There is consequently no universal harvest number. Fruit for a fresh sparkling-wine base requires a different balance from fruit for a structured red or a sweet [[concept.late-harvest|late-harvest wine]]. Waiting may advance sugar, flavour or skin development, but may also lose acid and prolong exposure to rain, heat, birds, rot or shrivelling. Cellar capacity, labour and the ability to pick blocks separately or in several passes are part of the real decision too.
:::

:::detail{#meerdere-rijpheidslenzen parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.melendez-grape-maturities-2013 source.fredes-carmenere-seed-maturity-2010"}
### Several ways to view ripeness

**Technological maturity** usually groups juice measurements such as sugar, titratable acidity and pH. **Phenolic maturity**, especially for coloured grapes, directs attention to skin colour, tannins, extractability and often seeds. **Aromatic or flavour maturity** covers development followed through smelling and tasting or targeted analysis. Berry mass, juice yield, skin texture and health matter alongside them.

These terms help prevent one Brix value from standing for the whole grape. They are not three independent biological clocks, however, and have no universally fixed endpoint. Which observations carry most weight depends on cultivar, method and intended wine. Research following juice chemistry, phenolics and sensory assessment side by side shows precisely that these dimensions may diverge. [@source.melendez-grape-maturities-2013]
:::

:::detail{#oogsten-is-een-besluit-onder-onzekerheid parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023"}
### Harvest is a decision under uncertainty

A grower never sees the future wine directly in the vineyard. Measurements and tasting describe today's fruit; weather forecasts, health and logistics determine what delay may cost or offer. Processing also changes the raw material: pressing, skin contact, extraction, acid adjustment and fermentation prevent a laboratory result from mapping one-to-one onto a wine value.

A sound harvest decision therefore uses a time series rather than a single snapshot. The direction and rate of change in Brix, pH, acidity, berry mass, flavour and health are often more informative than one “target number”. Uncertainty does not disappear, but becomes visible and discussable.
:::

:::figure{#rijpheid-als-meervoudige-afweging depth="intermediate" media_id="media.concept.berry-development-ripeness.ripeness-variables"}
**Assessing ripeness — seven observations, several possible harvest moments**

1. **Soluble solids:** a refractometer mainly tracks sugar concentration in juice; °Brix is useful but does not describe the whole berry.
2. **Acid chemistry:** pH and titratable acidity answer different questions and may be supplemented by individual acids such as malic acid.
3. **Skin and phenolics:** colour, tannins, skin texture and extractability are particularly relevant to wines made with skin contact.
4. **Seeds:** colour, hardness and taste provide context for seed-tissue development without independently fixing harvest date.
5. **Aroma and flavour:** tasting and targeted analysis follow cultivar-specific flavours, aroma-active compounds and precursors that do not track sugar synchronously.
6. **Water and berry mass:** berry mass and juice yield help distinguish growth, dilution, water loss and concentration.
7. **Health and risk:** rot, damage, shrivelling and the weather forecast determine how much developmental room truly remains.

The three baskets do not form a ranking. An earlier, middle or later moment may suit a different wine style or risk situation; the decision weighs the seven observations together with the block and its intended use.
:::

## Global context

The developmental model applies in both hemispheres, but not on the same calendar. In a cool season, acid may persist while sugar rises slowly; in warm conditions, sugar may climb quickly while malic acid falls faster. An aromatic white cultivar may be followed especially for acid, aroma and skin health, while colour, tannin and seeds may carry more weight for a coloured grape intended for long skin contact. These are emphases, not fixed rules for “white” and “red”.

Drought, rain and production goal also change the trade-off. In a dry region, water loss can raise concentration without proportionate further development. During a wet harvest period, sound fruit may matter more than waiting for additional compositional change. The same vineyard may become interesting at another moment for sparkling, still dry, sweet or fortified wine. Ripeness therefore always combines biology with intent.
:::

## Evidence and limits — measuring without erasing variation

A useful sample reflects the part of the block that will actually be harvested. Berries or bunches are therefore gathered across rows, vines, bunch positions, and sun and shade sides using a repeatable pattern. Samples require prompt, appropriate transport because damage, heat and time can change the result. Repeating the same route on successive dates makes the trend more comparable. [@source.awri-berry-sampling-maturity]

Analysis starts with a good question. Brix tracks soluble solids, chiefly sugar; pH follows hydrogen-ion activity and buffering; titratable acidity measures how much base is needed to neutralise acids. Berry mass helps identify concentration through water loss. For red grapes, skin contact during sample preparation can affect measured pH and colour. Methods must therefore remain consistent when results are compared. [@source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023]
:::

:::detail{#concentratie-is-geen-hoeveelheid parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.dai-grape-berry-water-carbon-fluxes-2010 source.sweetman-malate-metabolism-2009"}
### Concentration does not reveal its cause by itself

A concentration is an amount per volume or mass. It may rise because a substance is imported or formed, but also because the berry loses water. It may fall because a substance is broken down, but also because berry growth causes dilution. Following concentrations alone can confuse these routes.

Research and careful practice therefore combine concentration with measures such as berry mass, volume, weather, tissue observations and repeated samples. This distinction becomes especially important late in ripening, when physiological change, evaporation, damage and shrivelling may occur together. [@source.dai-grape-berry-water-carbon-fluxes-2010]
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gouot-grape-secondary-metabolites-2021 source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023"}
## Significance for wine

Harvest date determines the raw material entering the cellar: how much sugar can ferment, which [[concept.acidity|acid structure]] is present, how skins and seeds behave, what aroma potential is available and how much sorting or adjustment may be needed. This directs possible wine style and vinification, but does not fix the eventual wine. Yeast, extraction, oxygen, temperature, maturation and many other choices still follow.

Understanding berry development makes regional and cultivar descriptions more precise. “Long season”, “picked early”, “phenolically ripe” or “high natural acidity” then cease to be isolated quality words and become clues that demand context: which cultivar, season, measurement, goal and trade-off?
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
A grape does not ripen along one measuring stick. Sugar, acids, water, skin, seeds, phenolics, aroma and health change together but not in synchrony; harvesting means choosing which combination at that moment suits the wine one intends to make.
:::
