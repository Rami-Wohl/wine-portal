:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-carbonic-maceration source.awri-carbonic-maceration-2018"}
In carbonic maceration, part of winemaking begins inside intact grape berries in an oxygen-poor, carbon-dioxide-rich atmosphere. Grape enzymes change sugars, acids and aroma compounds, among other constituents. Yeast subsequently completes alcoholic fermentation of the released juice.
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-carbonic-maceration source.awri-carbonic-maceration-2018"}
## Overview — starting with an intact berry

A harvested, intact berry still contains living tissue. When oxygen is absent, its metabolism changes: **intracellular conversion** takes place, meaning within its cells. This start differs from [[concept.fermentation|yeast fermentation]] in free juice. Both processes can coexist in a real vat. [@source.awri-carbonic-maceration-2018]

The technique belongs among [[concept.winemaking-routes|production routes]] using intact fruit. It changes the beginning of winemaking; full conversion into wine also involves further fermentation and [[concept.pressing|pressing]]. [@source.awri-carbonic-maceration]
:::

:::section{#werking depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-carbonic-maceration-2018 source.ifv-carbonic-maceration"}
## How it works — changes inside and outside the berry

Under oxygen-poor conditions, grape enzymes produce a limited amount of alcohol and alter malic acid and aroma compounds, among other constituents. The intracellular phase converts only part of the sugar. Crushing and pressing release juice for further yeast fermentation. Where free juice is already present, that yeast activity can begin earlier. [@source.awri-carbonic-maceration-2018] [@source.ifv-carbonic-maceration]
:::

:::detail{#appelzuur-in-de-bes parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-carbonic-maceration-2018"}
### Acid change has a different cause here

Malic acid conversion inside the intact berry involves grape enzymes. This differs from bacterial [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|malolactic conversion]], which produces lactic acid. Both processes can play a role in making the same wine. A decrease in [[concept.acidity|acidity]] therefore does not identify the process without knowledge of how fermentation developed. [@source.awri-carbonic-maceration-2018; pp. 70–71]
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-carbonic-maceration source.awri-whole-bunch-fermentation source.beaujolais-vinification-2020"}
## Conditions and variation — carbonic and semi-carbonic

A strictly carbonic start creates a CO₂-rich atmosphere around fruit kept as intact as possible. In **semi-carbonic** processing, fermenting juice at the bottom supplies an important part of that gas. Bunch weight releases juice while berries higher up remain intact. Inter Beaujolais describes this combination as a regional practice. [@source.awri-carbonic-maceration] [@source.beaujolais-vinification-2020]

‘Whole-bunch fermentation’ describes the material, including stems. How much intracellular conversion occurs also depends on berry integrity and conditions in the vat. The proportion of intact fruit to free juice can change as the process develops. [@source.awri-whole-bunch-fermentation]
:::

:::figure{#twee-beginsituaties depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.carbonic-maceration.two-starts"}
**Where does the CO₂ atmosphere come from?**

1. **Carbonic start:** supplied gas supports the atmosphere around intact fruit, initially with as little free juice as possible.
2. **Semi-carbonic start:** fermenting juice below produces gas around intact berries above. Intracellular conversion and yeast fermentation coexist.

The pair compares two simplified starting situations. In a real vat, the distribution of intact fruit and juice changes during the process.
:::

:::section{#toepassing-en-keuzes depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ifv-carbonic-maceration source.awri-whole-bunch-fermentation"}
## Application and decisions — fruit, atmosphere and pressing

The winemaker monitors fruit condition, atmosphere and temperature. Damaged berries release more juice; insufficient oxygen exclusion can allow unwanted microorganisms to develop. Duration and pressing time are matched to the intended outcome. [@source.ifv-carbonic-maceration]

Juice sampled at the bottom does not necessarily describe the contents of still-intact berries. Pressing can release additional sugar. The liquid therefore needs continued fermentation monitoring and management afterwards. Free-run and pressed fractions can be assessed separately. [@source.awri-whole-bunch-fermentation] [@source.ifv-carbonic-maceration]
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-carbonic-maceration source.ifv-carbonic-maceration"}
## Evidence and limits — one vat contains different environments

The strict model helps explain the mechanism. Commercial vats almost inevitably contain some released juice, however, allowing yeast fermentation. A wine with carbonic characteristics can therefore result from several processes. For comparison, the proportion of intact fruit, temperature course and subsequent handling matter more than the term alone. [@source.awri-carbonic-maceration]
:::

:::detail{#proefopzet parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.ifv-carbonic-maceration"}
### Comparisons sometimes involve several interventions

In the VINAROMAS research described by IFV, the carbonic treatment also included crushed fruit at the bottom, and juice was fermented cooler after pressing than in the standard treatment. The eventual aroma differences therefore reflect an entire process. IFV itself identifies the different fermentation temperature as a possible contributing explanation. The experiment supports a comparison between those treatments; it does not isolate the intracellular phase alone. [@source.ifv-carbonic-maceration; VINAROMAS]
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-carbonic-maceration source.ifv-carbonic-maceration"}
## Significance for wine — a different aromatic start

Carbonic maceration is used to influence aroma profile and structure. Early pressing can limit subsequent contact and [[concept.extraction|extraction]]; longer or mixed approaches offer different possibilities. The technique appears in fruit-led young wines as well as other intended styles. Its place within [[concept.maceration|maceration]] explains the process choice, while grapes and subsequent handling help determine the eventual character. [@source.awri-carbonic-maceration] [@source.ifv-carbonic-maceration]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Carbonic maceration begins inside intact berries; yeast fermentation completes the wine. When a process is described as carbonic, also ask about free juice, atmosphere and pressing time.
:::
