A grapevine is not a bunch of grapes with some roots underneath, but a **perennial plant** in which old and new parts cooperate each season. Roots and permanent wood survive winter; buds, shoots, leaves, flowers and grapes form the annual growth. Water, dissolved minerals and organic compounds made by the leaves connect all those parts. Understanding that system makes viticultural choices easier to interpret without tying every intervention directly to one flavour or level of quality. [@source.cornell-grapevine-roots] [@source.cornell-grapevine-flowers]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-roots source.cornell-grapevine-flowers"}
## Overview

Above ground, a vineyard appears to start again each year: vines break bud, flower, bear grapes and lose their leaves. Beneath that rhythm lies continuity. The trunk, arms and woody roots form a permanent framework. New shoots emerge from it, while stored reserves help sustain early growth before a mature leaf canopy exists. [@source.cornell-grapevine-roots]

That makes a grapevine old and young at once. This year’s bunch grows on this year’s shoot, from a bud initiated during the previous growing season, on wood that may be much older. The plant therefore connects not only organs, but seasons. [@source.cornell-grapevine-flowers]
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-roots source.cornell-grapevine-flowers"}
## Structure and relationships

The finest young root portions absorb water and dissolved mineral nutrients. Thicker roots anchor the vine, transport materials and contain important storage tissue. Above ground, the trunk supports permanent wood; annual shoots grow from it with a sequence of **nodes**. At a node sit a leaf, often a tendril or inflorescence, and a bud from which later growth can emerge. [@source.cornell-grapevine-roots]

An inflorescence consists of small flowers. After successful pollination and fruit set, these develop into berries. A bunch is therefore the endpoint of a developmental sequence, but not a detached product: leaf, root, shoot and permanent wood continue to supply, consume and store material during its growth.

[[concept.ampelography|Ampelography]] uses characteristics of leaves, shoots and bunches, among others, to describe and distinguish grape varieties; this page focuses on what those organs do within the living plant.
:::

:::detail{#knop-verbindt-seizoenen parent="opbouw-en-samenhang" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-flowers"}
### The bud connects two crop years

A compound winter bud contains tiny primordia for future shoots and potentially inflorescences. An important part of next year’s crop potential is therefore established during the current growing season. Winter cold, spring frost and the vine’s later condition can still change much, but a crop in one year literally has a history in the year before. [@source.cornell-grapevine-flowers]
:::

:::figure{#anatomie-van-de-wijnstok depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.vine-as-living-system.vine-anatomy-cutaway"}
**Grapevine anatomy — permanent framework and annual growth**

1. **Fine roots** provide much of the active contact with soil water and dissolved nutrients.
2. **Woody roots** anchor the vine, connect fine roots to the trunk and store reserves.
3. **Trunk and permanent wood** support the training system and connect multiple growing seasons.
4. **Annual shoots** carry new leaves, tendrils and reproductive organs.
5. **Buds** contain the beginnings of later growth and can connect two seasons.
6. **Leaves** intercept light, exchange gases and produce organic compounds through photosynthesis.
7. **Inflorescences** consist of flowers from which berries may form after fruit set.
8. **Grape bunches** are growing sinks that receive water and carbon compounds.

:::

## How it works

Water enters the plant mainly through young root portions. In the **xylem**, it moves with dissolved minerals through root, trunk and shoot towards the leaves. Evaporation from the leaves — **transpiration** — helps pull the water column through the plant. When stomata close to limit water loss, carbon-dioxide uptake falls at the same time and photosynthesis is also restricted. [@source.cornell-grapevine-roots] [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020]

In green leaves, photosynthesis uses light energy to fix carbon dioxide into organic compounds. A mature leaf that is a net exporter is called a **source** in this model. Through the **phloem**, assimilates — especially sugars and other organic substances — move to places that use or store them on balance: **sinks**. These include a growing shoot tip, young roots, developing grapes and storage tissue. [@source.li-carbon-sink-strength-grapevine-2021]
:::

:::detail{#sources-sinks-en-reserves parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.li-carbon-sink-strength-grapevine-2021 source.martinez-luscher-source-sink-grapevine-2021"}
### Sources and sinks change roles

“Source” and “sink” are not permanent organ names, but roles at a given time. A young leaf uses more than it exports and is then a sink; a mature, well-lit leaf can become a source. A berry becomes a strong sink during ripening, while woody roots and trunk can store carbohydrates and release them again in spring. Distribution depends on sink size, activity, distance, developmental stage and the condition of the whole plant. [@source.li-carbon-sink-strength-grapevine-2021] [@source.martinez-luscher-source-sink-grapevine-2021]
:::

:::detail{#transportmodel parent="werking" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.dai-grape-berry-water-carbon-fluxes-2010 source.escalona-grapevine-carbon-balance-2012"}
### A network, not two simple pipes

Blue and orange arrows are useful for distinguishing functions, but reality is more dynamic. Xylem and phloem occupy branching vascular bundles, fluxes change through the day and season, and phloem flow runs from a current source to a current sink — not by default only downwards. Around véraison, the balance between water inflow, backflow and evaporation in berries changes. This does not mean that xylem universally stops functioning; research indicates that the connection can remain structurally intact. [@source.dai-grape-berry-water-carbon-fluxes-2010]

Nor is “the leaf makes sugar” the whole balance. Leaves, roots, fruit and woody parts consume carbon through respiration. Net available carbon is therefore what remains after maintenance and growth, not total gross photosynthesis. [@source.escalona-grapevine-carbon-balance-2012]
:::

:::figure{#transport-en-verdeling depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.vine-as-living-system.transport-source-sink"}
**Transport, allocation and reserves — three connected models**

1. **Uptake:** fine roots take up water and dissolved mineral substances from their immediate surroundings.
2. **Xylem:** blue arrows follow the simplified route from roots through trunk and shoot to leaves.
3. **Transpiration:** water vapour leaves the leaf; that evaporation is an important driver of water flow.
4. **Source and phloem:** a mature leaf exports assimilates through phloem to active destinations.
5. **Changing sinks:** shoot tips, grapes, roots and storage tissues do not always compete equally; their demand changes.
6. **Reserves:** roots and permanent wood store material, support early spring growth and are replenished later.

The arrows show principal directions at a selected moment. They are not a literal map of every vascular element and deliberately simplify a changing network.
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020 source.escalona-grapevine-carbon-balance-2012"}
## Conditions and variation

Light determines how much energy a leaf can intercept; temperature affects the rate of many reactions; water connects root uptake, stomata and cooling. A shaded, old or damaged part of the canopy therefore contributes differently from a healthy, well-lit leaf. At the same time, root activity, shoot growth and grape demand change through the season. [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020]

Drought is not one uniform condition either. Soil water, atmospheric evaporative demand, root distribution, cultivar, [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]] and acclimation together influence how quickly a vine responds. Moderate limitation may restrain vegetative growth; severe or prolonged limitation can damage photosynthesis, berry growth, reserves and survival. Context determines where useful restraint ends and damage begins. [@source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020]
:::

:::section{#keuzes-en-afwegingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.martinez-luscher-source-sink-grapevine-2021 source.pellegrino-vine-carbohydrate-status-2014"}
## Decisions and trade-offs

Pruning determines how many buds can grow. Shoot and canopy management change active leaf area and exposure. Crop thinning lowers current fruit demand; irrigation can alter vine water status. Plant material — including [[concept.clonal-selection|clone]] and rootstock — also affects how the vine responds to its environment. All these choices intervene in the same system, so one action may have several consequences.

A smaller crop, for example, does not automatically mean that the plant has ample carbon to spare. If much active leaf area is also missing, source capacity may be low. Conversely, a vine with a large canopy may make many assimilates, but also use much water and maintain vegetative growth. Sound interpretation considers demand, production, water and reserves together. [@source.pellegrino-vine-carbohydrate-status-2014]
:::

:::detail{#effect-over-seizoenen parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.martinez-luscher-source-sink-grapevine-2021"}
### A decision can carry over

When a vine retains healthy leaves after harvest, it can replenish reserves. Loss of active leaf area or heavy demand can restrict that storage. Because reserves support early growth and buds were initiated earlier, management in one season can affect budbreak, growth and fruitfulness in the next. The size of that effect remains dependent on timing, climate and the plant’s initial condition. [@source.martinez-luscher-source-sink-grapevine-2021]
:::

:::section{#wereldwijde-context depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020"}
## Global context

A grapevine in the Mosel, Mendoza, Marlborough or Stellenbosch has the same main organs and transport tissues. What differs is the calendar and which constraint dominates at a given time. In a cool region, heat or a short season may limit growth; in a hot, dry region, water supply and heat stress become more prominent; in a humid environment, vigorous shoot growth and disease pressure can help determine canopy management.

The hemisphere changes the months, not the biological order. This systems page is therefore not a regional recipe: it supplies the language with which later pages can explain local conditions and decisions precisely.
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.dai-grape-berry-water-carbon-fluxes-2010 source.escalona-grapevine-carbon-balance-2012"}
## Evidence and limits

Water potential, gas exchange, sap flows, carbohydrate reserves and organ growth can all be measured, but no single measurement captures the whole vine. Researchers therefore combine observations and models. An arrow diagram reveals the principal relationships; it does not predict exactly how much material travels to every berry in every hour.

That distinction helps test popular simplifications. Minerals absorbed by roots are essential to plant functions, but do not provide a direct flavour route from rock to glass. A source–sink model explains allocation, but does not make “more leaves” or “fewer grapes” better under every condition. The model is valuable precisely when its changing conditions remain attached. [@source.dai-grape-berry-water-carbon-fluxes-2010] [@source.escalona-grapevine-carbon-balance-2012]
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gambetta-grapevine-drought-physiology-2020 source.pellegrino-vine-carbohydrate-status-2014"}
## Significance for wine

A grape’s composition develops while the plant receives water, fixes carbon, grows, respires and allocates material. Viticulture does not control those processes with one switch, but changes conditions and proportions. A term such as “low yield”, “old vines”, “water stress” or “leaf removal” therefore becomes informative only when timing, intensity, climate and vine condition are also known.

The central insight is not merely that everything is connected, but **how**: roots and xylem connect the vine to water; functioning leaves supply assimilates; phloem connects current sources to current sinks; reserves connect seasons. That framework makes the next subjects — phenology, soils, water management, pruning, canopy and ripening — much easier to understand.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
A grapevine bears fruit through the work of the whole plant and with material from more than one season. Water flow, leaf function, growth, fruit and reserves must therefore be read as one changing system.
:::
