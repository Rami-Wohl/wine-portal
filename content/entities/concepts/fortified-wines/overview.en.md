:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-fortification source.oiv-liqueur-wine"}
## Overview

The technique of [[concept.fortification|fortification]] connects the family. Added alcohol joins alcohol produced through [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]]. Addition timing helps explain how much grape sugar is retained; product rules determine the appropriate raw material and alcohol source for a particular wine.

This page connects the technique with sweetness, ageing and different wine families. [[concept.sweet-wines|Sweet wine]] overlaps with it, while dry fortified wine also belongs.
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-vinification source.sherry-fortification source.sherry-ageing"}
## Structure and relationships

Read a fortified wine through three moments: **raw material and fermentation, alcohol addition, ageing and blending**. In Port, spirit is added during fermentation, retaining sugar. For dry Sherry, the base wine first ferments fully. [@source.ivdp-port-vinification] [@source.sherry-fortification]

Subsequent [[concept.elevage|maturation]] develops the wine further. In Jerez, this can involve a yeast layer, flor, or an oxidative environment. Sweetness and ageing choices therefore provide different information. Fortified wines can follow different ageing routes from one another.
:::

:::section{#werking depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-vinification source.sherry-fortification"}
## How it works

Alcohol addition changes the environment in which yeast works. During fermentation, the intervention can stop the process; after complete fermentation, it adds alcohol to already dry wine. The [[concept.fortification|fortification page]] explains possible timings and their effects.

| Process question | Example | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Was fermentation interrupted by fortification? | Port | Some grape sugar is retained |
| Was dry base wine fortified? | Dry Sherry | Added alcohol accompanies a dry starting wine |
| What ageing follows? | Flor or oxidative ageing in Jerez | The environment develops the wine further |

These examples clarify connections between steps. Implementation follows the conditions of the particular product. [@source.ivdp-port-vinification] [@source.sherry-fortification]
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.sherry-ageing source.wine-australia-rutherglen"}
## Conditions and variation

During biological ageing, flor lives at the wine surface. This yeast layer changes the wine and limits direct oxygen contact. Oxidative ageing takes place without that protective layer and gives oxygen a different role. Fino and Oloroso make the contrast visible within dry Sherry. [@source.sherry-ageing]

The wider family also includes sweet fortified Muscat and Muscadelle from Rutherglen. This Australian example shows fortified wine beyond Iberian practice. Grape variety, sweetness and ageing choices remain relevant for each wine. [@source.wine-australia-rutherglen]
:::

:::detail{#flor-en-solera parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sherry-ageing"}
### Flor describes the environment; solera organizes blending

In **solera**, a portion of wine is repeatedly withdrawn from one ageing group and replenished from another. Wines of different ages come together. The system can serve biological and oxidative ageing. Flor is the yeast layer; solera is the system of fractional withdrawal and replenishment. [@source.sherry-ageing]

A barrel-ageing duration and a solera description therefore provide different information about time. Assigning one harvest year does not fully describe a wine from such a blending system.
:::

:::section{#keuzes-en-afwegingen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.sherry-ageing source.sherry-fortification"}
## Decisions and trade-offs

The maker connects alcohol addition with the intended ageing environment. For Sherry, the chosen fortification supports ageing under flor or without that layer. Ageing conditions and blending then help maintain and develop the intended profile. [@source.sherry-fortification] [@source.sherry-ageing]

General [[concept.oxidation|oxidation]] and biological ageing each involve their own processes. Deliberately oxidative maturation receives a different judgement from unintended oxidation. Examine the intended route and wine condition, rather than using colour or alcohol percentage as the whole explanation.
:::

## Global context

- **Port, Portugal:** fortification during fermentation retains grape sugar. [@source.ivdp-port-vinification]
- **Sherry, Spain:** dry base wine and different ageing routes reveal breadth within one wine family. [@source.sherry-fortification]
- **Pantelleria, Italy:** the passito liquoroso variant combines dried grapes with fortification. [[concept.passito|Passito]] explains the wine term. [@source.masaf-pantelleria-specification-2024]
- **Rutherglen, Australia:** sweet fortified Muscat and Muscadelle add different varieties and a southern hemisphere context. [@source.wine-australia-rutherglen]

These examples explain different combinations. An individual wine then calls for its own origin, grapes and production conditions.
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-liqueur-wine source.oiv-fortification"}
## Evidence and limits

The OIV describes fortification and the liqueur wine category. Technical categories provide shared vocabulary; local legislation and product specifications provide binding conditions for a particular name. Permitted alcohol source, sweetness and ageing are therefore read for the specific product. [@source.oiv-liqueur-wine] [@source.oiv-fortification]

High alcohol can also come entirely from fermentation. 'Fortified' specifically describes an addition. That addition alone says little about subsequent flavour development: sugar, raw material and ageing also belong in the explanation.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ivdp-port-vinification source.sherry-ageing"}
## Significance for wine

Ask three questions: **when was alcohol added, how much sugar was retained, and how was the wine aged afterwards?** They place sweet Port and dry Fino within the same family and explain their differences.

For the particular wine, questions follow about grape variety, origin, age designation and blending. A producer's technical sheet and applicable product rules complete the general process picture. The family gives orientation; the individual wine supplies the specific expression.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Alcohol addition, sweetness and ageing are three connected choices. Their combination explains the variety of fortified wines.
:::
