**Appassimento** is the Italian term for partially drying grapes before fermentation. Water leaves the berry, producing a smaller volume of more concentrated juice. It most often takes place with harvested bunches in a drying room, although region, style and producer determine the precise method.

The word names a treatment of the grape. It neither guarantees a sweet wine nor serves as a synonym for Amarone.
:::

:::section{#italiaanse-procesnaam depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93 source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
## Overview — an Italian word for controlled drying

A bunch left for weeks or months after harvest changes visibly: plump berries become lighter and wrinkled. Sugars, acids, pigments and aromatic compounds occupy a smaller volume. At the same time, the grape continues for a while to respond to dehydration, oxygen and temperature. Appassimento is therefore more than a simple equation of “water out, flavour in”.

The technique occurs in several Italian wine traditions. Some produce sweet [[concept.passito|passito wine]], while others produce powerful dry wine. The name tells us how the raw material was treated, not how the bottle ultimately tastes.
:::

:::section{#selecteren-en-drogen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021 source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento"}
## Method — select first, then give air time

Appassimento begins with sound, ripe bunches. Split, bruised or mouldy berries can deteriorate quickly during drying and affect neighbouring fruit. Bunches are therefore harvested gently, sorted and placed in thin layers on racks or in perforated crates, or suspended.

In an Italian drying room, a *fruttaio*, moving air carries moisture away. Grapes need to breathe without heat, humidity or crowding accelerating harmful rot. Regular inspection and renewed sorting are as much a part of the method as waiting.
:::

:::detail{#lucht-en-droogtempo parent="selecteren-en-drogen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### Air and drying rate shape both style and health

Temperature, relative humidity, airflow, bunch density, berry skin and duration determine how quickly water disappears. A cool, slow course may preserve or develop different compounds from rapid drying in warmth. Bunches can also dry unevenly within one room.

Modern ventilation or dehumidification can regulate conditions, but cannot make grapes uniform. Measuring, moving and sorting remain necessary. The goal is a chosen degree of concentration in sound fruit, not the greatest possible weight loss.
:::

:::detail{#geen-recept-voor-heel-italie parent="selecteren-en-drogen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93 source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento"}
### Tradition and technique are not identical everywhere

Racks, crates, mats and hanging bunches can all belong to Italian drying practice. The roles of outdoor air, mechanical ventilation, temperature control and minimum drying time vary too. A local appellation may set exact limits that the general word *appassimento* does not.

One photogenic old drying loft should therefore never be read as a universal recipe. Understanding a particular wine requires its origin, production rules and producer choices as well as the process term.

:::section{#van-bes-naar-most depth="foundation" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
## Winemaking — little juice, substantial potential power

Dried grapes yield less must than fresh grapes. Their tough skins and sticky pulp make pressing or crushing harder, while the high sugar concentration places yeast under considerable stress. With dark grapes, skin contact also determines how much colour and [[concept.tannin|tannin]] enter the wine.

The winemaker then chooses whether fermentation converts nearly all the sugar or stops earlier. Complete fermentation can make a dry, high-alcohol wine; retained sugar can make a sweet one. Appassimento permits both routes.
:::

:::detail{#droog-of-zoet parent="van-bes-naar-most" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento source.valpolicella-consortium-recioto"}
### Fermentation decides what concentration becomes

Before fermentation, sugar is chiefly fuel. When yeast converts most of it, concentration appears mainly as alcohol, extract and structure. If fermentation is deliberately stopped or stalls under difficult conditions, more residual sugar remains.

Acid, tannin and alcohol then decide whether richness feels lively, firm or heavy. Drying can concentrate what was already present, but cannot repair fruit that lacked acidity, health or balance before drying.
:::

:::detail{#concentratie-heeft-meer-dan-een-oorzaak parent="van-bes-naar-most" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### A rising concentration can have more than one cause

A measured compound may rise per litre because juice volume contracts, because the berry genuinely forms new compounds under drying stress, or through both processes. Other compounds are broken down or oxidised. Researchers therefore compare not only concentrations per litre, but quantities per berry or unit of dry mass.

That distinction explains why two lots with the same weight loss do not automatically produce the same must or wine.
:::

:::section{#amarone-en-recioto depth="foundation" source_refs="source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento source.valpolicella-consortium-recioto"}
## Wine styles — Amarone and Recioto share dried grapes, not sweetness

Valpolicella uses varieties including [[grape.corvina|Corvina]], [[grape.corvinone|Corvinone]] and [[grape.rondinella|Rondinella]] for drying. For [[appellation.amarone-della-valpolicella|Amarone della Valpolicella]], bunches commonly rest for about one hundred to one hundred and twenty days in a *fruttaio* before the wine ferments dry. [@source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento]

[[appellation.recioto-della-valpolicella|Recioto della Valpolicella]] likewise begins with dried grapes, but fermentation is stopped to preserve its characteristic sweetness. [@source.valpolicella-consortium-recioto] The contrast makes the central rule clear: appassimento concentrates the grape; subsequent vinification helps determine the wine style.
:::

:::section{#woorden-op-hun-plaats depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93"}
## Terms — appassimento, passerillage and passito

[[concept.passerillage|Passerillage]] is the broader international process family for drying grapes on or off the vine. *Appassimento* is the Italian vocabulary within that family and in its best-known examples refers to postharvest drying. [[concept.passito|Passito]] generally names wine made from dried grapes.

The terms overlap but are not fully interchangeable. An Italian appellation may require *appassimento* without naming its wine *passito*, as Amarone demonstrates. Conversely, *passito* only reveals its grape, drying method and sweetness when read with its origin.
:::

:::section{#in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
## In the glass — richness without a fixed aroma list

Appassimento wines may show ripe or dried fruit, spice, cocoa, flowers, nuts or candied peel. They often feel fuller and may carry more alcohol, residual sugar or tannin than wine from comparable fresh grapes.

These are possibilities, not a diagnosis. Variety, origin, drying rate, mould, oxidation, fermentation, oak and bottle age all shape the result. A raisin note does not prove appassimento, and a carefully made appassimento wine need not taste of raisins.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Appassimento is not a flavour trick but a chain of choices between harvest and press. The grape loses water, the producer safeguards health and drying rate, and only fermentation turns that concentration into a dry or sweet wine.
:::
