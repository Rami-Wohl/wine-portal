:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-lees-contact-2020"}
**Bâtonnage** is the deliberate stirring of lees — sediment composed chiefly of yeast cells and fine particles — through wine. It temporarily intensifies contact between wine and lees. It is one possible action during [[concept.lees-ageing|lees ageing]], but not a synonym for it: wine can also remain undisturbed on its lees. [@source.awri-lees-contact-2020]
:::

:::section{#betekenis-en-werkwijze depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-lees-contact-2020"}
## Meaning and method — setting the sediment in motion again

After [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]], solids settle at the bottom of a vessel. During bâtonnage, the winemaker brings part of that sediment back into suspension. The wine turns cloudy before the particles gradually settle again.

The French term refers to the *bâton*, the stick traditionally used to stir wooden barrels. Modern cellars also employ other tools and tank systems. The defining action remains the same: intentionally distributing the lees through the wine.
:::

:::detail{#praktische-uitvoering parent="betekenis-en-werkwijze" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-lees-contact-2020"}
### Stirring is more than a frequency on a calendar

How often and vigorously the wine is stirred, how much lees is present and whether the vessel is opened all change the intervention. Temperature, vessel size and time since fermentation matter as well. “Weekly bâtonnage” therefore does not describe the complete cellar regime.

The winemaker assesses the wine between interventions and may reduce or stop stirring. Bâtonnage is not a process that must run automatically once begun.
:::

:::section{#effect-op-de-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-lees-contact-2020"}
## Effect on the wine — contact can shift texture and aroma

Stirring the sediment temporarily exposes more wine to the lees. Compounds from yeast cells and other particles may consequently be exchanged differently or more rapidly. Bâtonnage can contribute to a fuller mouthfeel, altered aromatic development and a different interaction with oxygen. [@source.awri-lees-contact-2020]

These are possibilities, not guaranteed tasting notes. Grape variety, lees composition, vessel, duration and the rest of the [[concept.elevage|ageing process]] together determine whether the difference is perceptible and desirable.
:::

:::detail{#geen-recept-voor-romigheid parent="effect-op-de-wijn" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-lees-contact-2020"}
### No recipe for creaminess

Bâtonnage is often mentioned alongside creaminess or added body. That shorthand obscures how [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|malolactic fermentation]], oak, alcohol, acidity and ageing time also shape mouthfeel. Quiet lees contact without stirring can likewise produce change.

The technique therefore says something about how a wine was handled, but cannot predict its taste on its own.
:::

:::section{#keuze-en-risico depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-lees-contact-2020"}
## Choice and risk — stirring requires continued tasting

A winemaker may use bâtonnage to direct lees contact, but can equally make a deliberate choice not to stir. Sound, suitable lees and close observation matter more than the technique's reputation. The question is always which problem the action solves or which stylistic aim it supports.
:::

:::detail{#zuurstof-en-microbiologie parent="keuze-en-risico" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-lees-contact-2020"}
### Between reduction, oxidation and microbial activity

Stirring can introduce oxygen, particularly when a barrel is opened, while returning settled material to chemical and microbial reactions. Insufficient appropriate oxygen can allow reductive odours to stand out; excessive exposure can encourage oxidation, consume protective sulphur dioxide or promote volatile acidity. [@source.awri-lees-contact-2020]

There is consequently no universally ideal frequency. The winemaker follows aroma, flavour, temperature, free sulphur dioxide and microbial condition, adapting the action to the particular wine.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Bâtonnage is a tool, not a mark of quality: it returns lees to the wine, after which material, timing, oxygen and observation determine what the intervention actually does.
:::
