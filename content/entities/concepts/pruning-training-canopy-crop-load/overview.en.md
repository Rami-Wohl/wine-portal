A grapevine does not naturally grow into the orderly rows that make a vineyard recognisable. The grower first builds a permanent form, chooses each winter which buds will carry the new season, then guides the shoots, leaves and bunches that emerge. **Training, pruning, canopy management and crop load** are therefore not separate tricks, but successive decisions about space, light, fruit and the capacity of [[concept.vine-as-living-system|the same living plant]]. [@source.reynolds-vanden-heuvel-training-systems-2009] [@source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.reynolds-vanden-heuvel-training-systems-2009 source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022"}
## Overview

**Training** gives the vine its multi-year architecture: where the trunk, head or cordons sit, how high the fruit hangs and whether shoots grow freely, upwards, downwards or across a roof. The **trellis system** is the physical support made of posts, wires or a pergola; it is not the same as the plant form, although the two are designed together.

**Pruning** removes wood and determines which buds and fruiting wood remain. In winter, this establishes the first distribution of potential shoots and bunches. **Canopy management** covers the green work that follows: removing excess shoots, positioning shoots, hedging, removing leaves around the fruit zone or deliberately preserving shade. **Crop load** finally describes the amount of fruit in relation to the vine’s capacity and functioning leaf area. Tonnes per hectare alone reveal only part of that relationship.

These four fields run on different clocks. A training system may last for decades; pruning is renewed annually; green work follows the [[concept.grapevine-phenology|annual cycle]], growth and weather within weeks or days. A sound choice therefore begins not with “which technique is best?” but with: how is this vine growing, what raw material is wanted at harvest, and which constraint is being managed here and now?
:::

## Structure and relationships

Beneath its bark, a vine keeps its history. Roots, trunk, head and any cordons form the **permanent wood**. The green shoots of one summer lignify in autumn to become **one-year-old wood**. Their nodes carry compound buds containing the next year’s compressed shoot; depending on variety, position and conditions, those shoots may carry one or more inflorescences.

With **spur pruning**, short pieces of one-year-old wood carrying a few buds remain on permanent cordons. With **cane pruning**, the grower selects one or more longer one-year-old canes each year, ties them down and generally retains renewal wood for the following year. The distinction is therefore about more than cutting short or long: it determines where fruitful buds originate, how much tying is required and how easily the plant can later be renewed. Basal bud fruitfulness, frost risk, vine spacing, labour and mechanisation all influence the choice. [@source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022]
:::

:::detail{#de-draad-is-niet-de-wijnstok parent="opbouw-en-samenhang" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.reynolds-vanden-heuvel-training-systems-2009"}
### Vine form and support solve different problems

A post-and-wire system can support shoots, direct their growth and leave room for machinery, but the wires do not decide how many buds or fruit the vine can carry. Conversely, the same basic vine form can be supported in different ways: upright shoots may be tucked between movable wires, trailing shoots may hang from a high cordon, and a divided canopy may spread growth over two planes.

Three-dimensional placement changes how much leaf area receives direct light, how many leaves shade one another and which microclimate surrounds the bunches. Trials therefore compare not only named systems, but also exposed leaf area, shoot density, fruit-zone arrangement, vigour and climate. [@source.reynolds-vanden-heuvel-training-systems-2009]

**From permanent form to a supported crop**

1. **Architecture:** trunk and cordons determine for many years where one-year-old wood and new growth can be placed.
2. **Dormant pruning:** the grower selects fruiting wood and buds. This sets an initial ceiling for shoot and bunch number, but frost, budbreak and fruit set can still change the outcome.
3. **Spring work:** unwanted or double shoots are selectively removed; the remaining shoots are spaced and supported rather than allowed to form one dense mass.
4. **Summer work:** shoot tips, laterals and leaves are managed selectively. Partial shade remains around the bunches here because airflow and protection both matter.
5. **Ripening assessment:** leaf function, shoot growth, bunch number, berry condition and time left in the season are read together. Any cluster thinning is a correction within that system, not a self-contained quality recipe.

## How it works — a chain that begins in winter

Retained buds break into shoots. Their number and distribution affect how quickly the canopy closes and how many potential bunches appear. [[concept.flowering-fruit-set-yield|Flowering and fruit set]] then determine how many flowers actually become berries. Growth, water, weather and damage may alter the planned load further. Dormant pruning is therefore an early setting, not an exact harvest forecast.

Leaves intercept light and supply assimilates; shoot tips, roots, reserves and fruit demand them to different degrees over time. A very dense wall may carry much total leaf area but also many shaded leaves that contribute little and create a more humid fruit zone. An excessively open wall may lack functioning leaf area or suddenly expose berries to intense sun and heat. The practical goal is a canopy with enough healthy, active leaves, room for shoots and a fruit-zone microclimate suited to the intended crop.
:::

:::detail{#compensatie-in-plaats-van-een-vast-budget parent="werking" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.reynolds-vanden-heuvel-training-systems-2009 source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024"}
### The plant compensates, but not without limits

A vine does not divide a fixed sugar budget simply by bunch number. After pruning or thinning, remaining shoots, leaves or berries may grow differently; greater light can alter leaf activity, and less fruit may prolong vegetative growth. The response depends on timing, water, nutrition, variety, rootstock and the season that remains. An intervention therefore changes both demand and future supply.

This is why cluster-thinning trials produce varied results. Removing bunches directly reduces yield, but effects on sugar, acids, phenolics or aroma are not equally large or in the same direction everywhere. A heavy load that demonstrably delays ripening presents a different starting point from an already balanced vine whose fruit is removed without diagnosis. [@source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024]
:::

## Conditions and variation

Suitable architecture begins with the plant’s growth habit. Upright shoots lend themselves to a vertical wall; more procumbent growth may hang more naturally from a high wire. Strong vigour can quickly create layers of leaves on a narrow plane, while a weak vine may never fill a large system. Bud fruitfulness partly determines whether short pruning retains enough fruitful positions.

The site then changes the assignment. In a cool or humid [[concept.climate-weather-site-microclimate|climate and microclimate]], more light and air around bunches may support drying and ripening. In a hot, sunny climate, deliberate shade can limit surface temperature and sunburn. [[concept.vine-water-relations|Vine water status]], [[concept.vineyard-soils|soil volume]], wind, row direction, slope and the sun’s path influence which side and which moment are vulnerable. The same leaf-removal treatment can therefore be useful, unnecessary or damaging.
:::

:::detail{#blootstelling-is-timing parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.vanderweide-leaf-removal-meta-analysis-2021 source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021"}
### Exposure is also about timing

Early, selective leaf removal around bloom can affect fruit set and cluster compactness and gives young berries time to acclimate gradually to more light. A meta-analysis of pre-bloom leaf removal found, on average, less bunch rot, lower yield and compactness, and higher sugar concentration, but variety, rootstock and treatment severity altered the response. The average is not a prescription for a particular block. [@source.vanderweide-leaf-removal-meta-analysis-2021]

Removing much foliage late and suddenly can expose berries that developed in shade. Sunburn arises through the interaction of radiation, berry temperature, developmental stage, water status and prior acclimation. Hedging can also expose fruit indirectly as shoots move or leaves dry. “Open” is therefore not an end in itself; the useful question is which dose of morning and afternoon light, airflow and shade this fruit needs at this stage. [@source.gambetta-grape-sunburn-review-2021]
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs — from winter cut to harvest correction

Each intervention has a direct task and shifts other work at the same time:

- **Dormant pruning** retains fruitful wood and a planned bud number. More buds may distribute growth over more shoots, but may also give a denser wall and more bunches; fewer buds may stimulate stronger individual shoots.
- **Shoot thinning** removes unwanted, double or poorly placed shoots. Early work is quick and leaves smaller wounds, but occurs before the season is fully visible.
- **Shoot positioning and tying** distribute shoots through space and keep alleys and fruit zones accessible. Repeated passes may be needed after wind or growth surges.
- **Hedging and lateral management** keep the wall within its space and can improve light distribution. Hedging too early or too severely can stimulate lateral growth or sacrifice functioning leaves.
- **Leaf removal** opens the fruit zone to light, air and treatments. It can also remove heat protection and, when done early, alter fruit set and yield.
- **Cluster thinning** lowers the fruit mass already present. It is most defensible when correcting a specific load, unevenness or damage; without diagnosis, lost yield is more certain than improved quality.

Sequence matters. A dense problem that could have been prevented through bud number and shoot spacing costs more labour to repair later with leaf removal. Conversely, extreme weather can overturn a sound winter plan. Management is therefore cyclical: observe, intervene, observe again and carry the lesson into the next pruning season.
:::

:::detail{#wonden-en-levensduur parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.rosace-pruning-wounds-2023 source.uga-dormant-pruning-2022"}
### Every form also becomes a history of wounds

Pruning shapes the vine, but every cut opens tissue. Fungi associated with trunk diseases can enter through fresh pruning wounds; susceptibility generally declines as wounds age, while pathogen, variety, pruning period and weather affect the rate. The literature analysis by Rosace and colleagues found substantial variation among studies and cautions against a single fixed safe interval. [@source.rosace-pruning-wounds-2023]

Over the long term, it also matters where conductive pathways are interrupted, how many large cuts accumulate and whether renewal wood remains available. A system efficient for the current year may be harder to rebuild after frost, breakage or diseased wood. Assessing architecture therefore means counting not only yield and labour, but also recovery routes and the expected life of trunks and arms.
:::

:::detail{#mechanisatie-verplaatst-de-beslissing parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.kurtural-fidelibus-mechanization-2021"}
### Mechanisation moves the precision

Machines can pre-prune, thin shoots, remove leaves, hedge and harvest. They increase speed and the chance of working at the intended time, and may make operations more consistent. Many machines are less selective than an experienced hand, however: their effect depends on row form, trellis, terrain, speed, settings and vine uniformity.

Mechanisation therefore does not remove the viticultural decision. Precision moves into vineyard design, calibration, trial strips, follow-up work and inspection for damage or missed zones. A labour saving can also create a different distribution of crop, foliage or fruit when the system was not designed for that machine from establishment. [@source.kurtural-fidelibus-mechanization-2021]
:::

## Global context — several valid architectures

Free-standing **goblet** or bush vines distribute shoots around a low head and can create their own shade without a continuous wire wall. A **vertical shoot positioned** canopy (VSP) directs shoots upwards and creates a narrow, accessible fruit zone, but may become dense or overly tall under strong growth. **High-wire** systems allow shoots to trail from a raised cordon and suit, among other situations, varieties with a more procumbent growth habit. **Pergola** or tendone forms spread foliage horizontally above the fruit zone and can organise vigorous growth and shade. Divided systems such as lyre or double curtain spread growth over two planes.

No name contains a quality level in itself. Low, old bush vines may yield little because of age, drought or spacing, but the form does not guarantee that result. A VSP row may be precise and airy or carry several leaf layers. A pergola may provide shelter and leaf area or retain excessive shade. The meaningful comparison concerns vigour, shoot direction, light, air, water, erosion and topography, worker safety, mechanisation and the intended product.
:::

## Evidence and limits — measuring which balance?

A grower can count buds, shoots and bunches; examine shoot length and internodes; estimate leaf layers and gaps; measure exposed leaf area; weigh yield; and weigh removed one-year-old wood after pruning. Repeated measurements on marked vines show whether the same plants are growing more strongly, more weakly or with a different distribution over time. Such observations make “a lot of canopy” or “low yield” more concrete.

Ratios such as yield to pruning weight try to summarise fruit and vegetative growth together. They are useful for comparisons within a block and system, but their range shifts with variety, climate, spacing, training, method and production goal. Yield per hectare also hides vine number and individual capacity; yield per vine hides row space and canopy form. No single number is the vine.
:::

:::detail{#gemiddelden-zijn-geen-recepten parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.vanderweide-leaf-removal-meta-analysis-2021 source.vanderweide-cluster-thinning-meta-analysis-2024"}
### An average effect hides the starting point

Meta-analyses can show that, across many trials, an intervention is associated on average with lower yield, less rot or altered sugar values. They do not automatically solve the decision in one particular block. Trials differ in variety, rootstock, climate, timing, severity, initial canopy density and year; publications also do not always report labour, economics or long-term reserves.

The best application therefore begins before the work: define the problem and preserve an untreated comparison or representative measurement row. Afterwards, assess not only harvest chemistry but also health, leaf function, uniformity, labour hours and the growth revealed at the next winter pruning.
:::

## Significance for wine

These decisions shape the environment in which grapes develop. They can shift fruit set, cluster compactness, berry size, light and temperature around fruit, disease pressure, photosynthesis and the pace of [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|ripening]]. Sugars, acids, phenolics and aroma precursors may change in turn. The direction still depends on starting condition, timing, variety, climate and winemaking; an open canopy or low yield is not a flavour description.

Read producer language with four questions. **What was the objective?** More airflow, delayed budbreak, more even ripening, lower load or efficient work are different goals. **When was the intervention made?** Removing a leaf at bloom is unlike doing so shortly before harvest. **What condition was the vine in?** Vigour, water status and existing load shape the response. **Which outcome was measured?** A photograph of tidy rows, “hand harvested” or a low yield figure alone proves neither a healthy vine nor better wine.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Training builds the space, pruning chooses the annual starting point, canopy work guides growth and crop load assesses what the vine must carry. Sound viticulture connects all four across seasons; it does not chase one ideal number of buds, leaves or bunches.
:::
