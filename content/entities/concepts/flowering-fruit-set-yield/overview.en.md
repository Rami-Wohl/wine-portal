A bunch does not begin at harvest, or even at flowering. Within the [[concept.grapevine-phenology|grapevine's annual cycle]], potential inflorescences are initiated inside buds during the previous growing season. Further filters follow: which buds burst, how many fruitful shoots appear, how many flowers open, how many young fruits remain, and how heavy the berries eventually become. **Flowering, fruit set and yield formation** describes that whole chain—and shows why yield is never determined at a single moment. [@source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009] [@source.cornell-bud-fruitfulness-yield-2012]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009 source.awri-yield-estimation-2022"}
## Overview

Unobtrusive green flowers appear on the vine in late spring or the early part of the growing season. Every flower is an opportunity to form a berry, but the plant never converts all those opportunities into fruit. Flowers may not develop fully, may fall during or after flowering, may set but grow unevenly, or may become normal berries. What is harvested at the end is the result of all those successive selections.

Yield is therefore more than the number of kilograms picked. It begins with productive vines, buds and shoots, continues through bunch and berry number, and ends with average berry mass. A change early in the chain may later be amplified or partly compensated. More berries per bunch, for example, may coincide with smaller berries; fewer bunches may leave more growth capacity for those that remain. [@source.awri-yield-estimation-2022]
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-flowers source.cornell-bud-fruitfulness-yield-2012 source.tello-bunch-compactness-review-2018"}
## Structure and relationships — from bud to bunch

The yield chain starts inside a compound bud. During the first growing season, the beginnings of a future shoot and potential inflorescences form within it. This reproductive track draws on water, reserves and active leaves within the [[concept.vine-as-living-system|vine as a living system]]. After winter dormancy, the bud bursts in the second season. Only a living bud that bursts can produce a shoot; only a fruitful shoot carries one or more inflorescences. Each inflorescence then develops tens to hundreds of small flowers. [@source.cornell-grapevine-flowers]

After flowering and fruit set, berries sit on a branched central framework, the rachis. Final bunch form depends on more than berry number. Rachis length and branching, pedicel length and berry size all determine how much space remains between berries. Bunch compactness is therefore a composite trait, not simply another term for high yield. [@source.tello-bunch-compactness-review-2018]
:::

:::detail{#twee-groeiseizoenen parent="opbouw-en-samenhang" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.cornell-grapevine-flowers source.cornell-bud-fruitfulness-yield-2012"}
### One crop is prepared across two seasons

While this year's visible bunches are growing, potential inflorescences for next year are already being initiated in young buds. Light around those buds, canopy condition, reserves and cultivar-specific fruitfulness can help determine how many inflorescences later appear. Winter cold, damage or uneven budburst may subsequently remove part of that potential.

The calendar-year boundary is therefore misleading. A small crop may result from limited bud fruitfulness in the previous season, bud or shoot loss, few flowers, poor fruit set, small berries, or a combination of these. Looking only at weather during flowering is rarely enough. [@source.cornell-bud-fruitfulness-yield-2012]
:::

:::section{#werking depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009 source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
## How it works — from flower to berry

In most cultivated *Vitis vinifera* cultivars, one flower contains both stamens and a pistil. The petals are joined at the top to form a green cap, the **calyptra**. At capfall it detaches, exposing the anthers and stigma. Pollen can germinate on the stigma; a pollen tube then grows through the style towards an ovule. Pollination is the arrival of pollen, fertilisation is the union of reproductive cells, and fruit set is the persistent growth of the ovary. They are three connected but distinct steps. [@source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009]

Grapevine flowers are largely self-pollinating. Wind and movement may distribute pollen, but a visit from bees is not required for normal set. Following successful fertilisation, seeds and fruit tissues develop and the ovary begins to swell. Even after ordinary flowering, a substantial share of flowers or very young fruitlets is shed; a bunch in which every flower became a berry would not be the normal benchmark. [@source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009]
:::

:::detail{#niet-iedere-bloem-wordt-een-bes parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010 source.muntean-millerandage-review-2025"}
### Three different bunch outcomes

With ordinary partial fruit set, some young berries remain while the rest abscise cleanly. With [[concept.coulure|coulure]], that loss is unusually severe and the bunch retains few berries. With [[concept.millerandage|millerandage]], different developmental outcomes remain side by side, such as normal berries, small often seed-poor berries and sometimes hard green ovaries that do not ripen normally. [@source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010] [@source.muntean-millerandage-review-2025]

The two terms do not describe the same condition. Coulure is fundamentally about loss; millerandage about uneven development that remains visible. Both may occur in one bunch or season, but they require different observation and explanation.
:::

:::detail{#bestuiving-is-niet-hetzelfde-als-zetting parent="werking" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009 source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
### Pollination does not prove fruit set

The presence of pollen does not show that a pollen tube grew successfully, an ovule was fertilised, and the young ovary subsequently received enough growth signals and assimilates. Disruption can occur at several points in the chain. A bunch may therefore lose flowers despite visible pollination, or retain small berries after incomplete seed development.

The reverse measurement problem also occurs: few berries per bunch do not automatically prove poor fruit set. A cultivar or inflorescence may have started with relatively few flowers. Correct diagnosis therefore requires both the initial flower number and the later berry categories to be examined. [@source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010]
:::

:::figure{#bloem-tot-vruchtzetting depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.flowering-fruit-set-yield.flower-to-set-outcomes"}
**From grapevine flower to fruit set — process and possible outcomes**

1. **Closed flower:** five fused petals form a green cap around the reproductive organs.
2. **Capfall and open flower:** the calyptra detaches; stamens bearing anthers surround the pistil with stigma and ovary.
3. **Pollination and fertilisation:** the cutaway shows pollen on the stigma and the route of a pollen tube towards the ovules inside the ovary.
4. **Young set berry:** the ovary swells as seed and fruit tissues begin to develop.
5. **Ordinary partial fruit set:** some flowers become evenly growing young berries while others abscise naturally.
6. **Coulure:** many flowers or very young fruitlets are shed, leaving a conspicuously sparse bunch.
7. **Millerandage:** normal and much smaller berries or arrested green ovaries remain together within the bunch.

The plate compresses time and scale. Its three final branches are recognition models; real vineyards show intermediate states, and coulure and millerandage may occur together.
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009 source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
## Conditions and variation

Cultivar and vine provide the starting point. Flower number, pollen performance, seed formation and the natural tendency towards compact or loose bunches differ genetically. Vine condition and conditions during flowering are superimposed on that background. Temperature affects the pace of capfall, pollen germination and growth; prolonged cloudy weather may constrain carbon supply; water or nutrient problems may alter vegetative and reproductive growth together.

None of these factors acts alone. A vigorous shoot is itself a strong user of assimilates and may compete with the inflorescence around flowering. An open, functioning canopy can instead intercept light and supply carbon. The outcome therefore depends on timing, severity, cultivar and the reserves and active leaf area already available to the vine. [@source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009]
:::

:::detail{#ras-en-planttoestand parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010 source.plantgrape-merlot"}
### The same weather, a different bunch

Cultivars do not respond equally to the same flowering period. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], for example, is known for susceptibility to coulure, whereas other cultivars under similar conditions may differ chiefly in berry size or bunch compactness. Even within one cultivar, rootstock, shoot vigour, leaf area, nutrient status and the balance between growth and fruit can shift the response. [@source.plantgrape-merlot]

“Cold during flowering” therefore supplies context but not a complete cause. A convincing explanation combines weather records with flowering progress, vine condition and what was actually observed in flowers, berries and seeds.
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs

Pruning determines how many buds a vine carries into the season; shoot selection determines how many burst shoots and inflorescences remain. Later, canopy management, water and nutrient strategy, bunch thinning and harvest selection may further alter the distribution between growth and fruit. Every intervention touches a different part of the yield chain and may affect not only the current season but also later bud fruitfulness or reserves.

The objective is therefore not simply “maximum set”. A very compact bunch may be more vulnerable to damage, uneven ripening and rot as berries touch and the interior remains less open. A very loose or sparse bunch may severely restrict yield. Desirable bunch architecture varies with cultivar, climate, disease pressure, production objective and harvest method. [@source.tello-bunch-compactness-review-2018]
:::

:::detail{#timing-verandert-meerdere-componenten parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.cornell-bud-fruitfulness-yield-2012 source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010"}
### Intervention begins by finding the weak link

Where few bunches are present, an intervention around flowering offers little help if the problem arose earlier through bud fruitfulness or winter loss. With many bunches but few berries, investigation instead turns to flower number, flowering, set and early fruit loss. Where berry number is normal but bunch mass is low, berry growth, water availability or harvest timing may matter more.

Timing changes the trade-off as well. Strong canopy work just before or during flowering may improve the microclimate but also alter leaf area and carbon balance. Bunch thinning later corrects bunch number, not the original fruit set. A sound decision therefore starts with the yield component that actually differs.
:::

:::section{#wereldwijde-context depth="foundation" source_refs="source.vasconcelos-flowering-review-2009 source.tello-bunch-compactness-review-2018"}
## Global context

The biological sequence is recognisable worldwide, but neither its calendar nor an ideal bunch form is universal. Flowering follows shoot development in both hemispheres; only the months change. In humid regions, an open bunch may favour drying and disease management. Under dry conditions, other constraints such as water and heat may matter more. A naturally compact cultivar consequently demands different attention from one with long branches and loose berries.

Production objective also matters. Table-grape growing may deliberately pursue large, even and visually attractive berries with methods that do not automatically suit wine grapes. For wine, the task is a workable balance between quantity, health, ripening progress and the intended harvest date. No single region supplies a universal standard.
:::

:::section{#meten-en-vergelijken depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010 source.awri-yield-estimation-2022"}
## Evidence and limits — measurement and comparison

Fruit set is commonly calculated as retained berries relative to the initial number of flowers. This requires a reliable count or estimate before flowering and a clear agreement about the later measurement date. Counting berries only after set confounds flower number with set success. Normal berries, small seed-poor berries and arrested green ovaries must also be recorded separately when millerandage is being investigated. [@source.dry-reproductive-performance-2010]

Yield estimates become more certain as the season advances. Early in the season, more components that can still change have to be assumed; closer to harvest, bunch number, berry number and berry mass can be sampled more directly. Spatial variation among vines and rows remains important, so a representative sample matters at least as much as the formula. [@source.awri-yield-estimation-2022]
:::

:::detail{#opbrengst-is-een-product parent="meten-en-vergelijken" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-yield-estimation-2022 source.cornell-bud-fruitfulness-yield-2012"}
### Yield is a product, not a single number

A useful decomposition is: productive vines × shoots per vine × bunches per shoot × berries per bunch × average berry mass. Missing vines, uneven shoot fruitfulness, selective removal and pre-harvest loss may also be included in practice. Every factor has its own measurement error and biological timing. [@source.awri-yield-estimation-2022]

This multiplication explains why small departures may accumulate—and why compensation occurs. Fewer berries may coincide with larger individual berries; extra shoots do not necessarily supply proportionally more bunches. Accurate crop forecasting therefore needs local historical records as well as current measurements, not merely a fixed average bunch mass.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.tello-bunch-compactness-review-2018 source.awri-yield-estimation-2022"}
## Significance for wine

Flowering and fruit set determine how much and what kind of fruit the vine must support for the remainder of the season. Bunch number, berry number and berry size influence the relationship between leaf area and fruit, the course of ripening and the time at which a site reaches its intended composition. Bunch architecture also affects berry contact, drying and the ease with which damage or rot can develop inside the cluster. [@source.tello-bunch-compactness-review-2018]

These relationships are not a simple quality ladder. Low yield may reflect deliberate selection, but also frost, poor set or damage. An open bunch may be practically useful, while unevenly developed berries can complicate sampling and harvest selection. The value of this knowledge lies in diagnosis: understanding **which step** shaped the final raw material.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
A crop is not made on one day. It forms as bud fruitfulness, flowering, fruit set, berry number and berry mass follow one another across two growing seasons—and each step limits the possibilities of the next.
:::
