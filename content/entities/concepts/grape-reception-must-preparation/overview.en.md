Once grapes reach the winery, the harvest becomes a series of decisions: what stays together, what is removed, and when is juice released? Reception and must preparation connect the condition of the fruit to the way it will ferment. A whole bunch, a detached berry and a tank of juice each need different handling.

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-grape-preparation source.oiv-pressing source.eurlex-wine-oenological-practices-20260818"}
## Overview — from harvested fruit to fermentation

Must is the juice obtained from fresh grapes. [@source.eurlex-wine-oenological-practices-20260818; Annex VII, Part II.10] Preparation then depends on whether juice is separated from skins or the grape mass continues together.

This stage runs from reception and sorting to preparation for [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]]. [[concept.pressing|Pressing]] can be an early separation within it, although it usually comes later in red fermentation on skins. [@source.oiv-pressing]
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-grape-preparation"}
## Structure and relationships — bunch, berry and juice

**Destemming** removes berries from the branching bunch stem. **Crushing** opens their skins and releases juice. These operations answer different questions: a destemmed berry can remain intact, whereas a whole bunch includes stems. **Pressing** then separates liquid from solids. A winemaker may omit or combine operations. [@source.oiv-grape-preparation; II.1.2–1.3]
:::

:::figure{#materiaalkeuzes depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.grape-reception-must-preparation.material-choices"}
**Three starting points in the winery**

1. **Whole bunch.** Berries and the branching bunch stem remain together.
2. **Detached intact berries.** The bunch stem has been removed; berry skins remain closed.
3. **Crushed fruit.** Opened skins, pulp, juice and intact seeds lie together.

The illustration compares material states. Its numbers do not prescribe a processing sequence.
:::

## How it works — assess, select and protect

Protection begins in transit: limit damage and delays, work cleanly and manage temperature. Once juice is released, oxygen contact and microbial activity also need attention. [@source.oiv-so2-reduction-practices-2020; B–C]

Reception checks cover lot identity, temperature, damage and disease. Sorting removes unwanted bunches, berries or other material. Its purpose depends on the intended wine and the [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|ripeness]] of the fruit: keeping sound material separate begins before a tank is filled.

Assessment includes a representative sample for sugar, pH and titratable acidity, with yeast-available nitrogen measured where needed. Visible fruit condition and measured composition inform the same decision. [@source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023; Sampling matters]
:::

:::detail{#transport-en-bescherming parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.oiv-so2-reduction-practices-2020 source.awri-sulfur-dioxide"}
### Preparation starts during transport

Limit crushing in transit, use clean containers and coordinate harvesting with winery capacity. Once damaged berries release juice, microorganisms gain access to the liquid. Shorter delays, temperature control and careful transfers help protect the lot. [@source.oiv-so2-reduction-practices-2020; B–C]

Inert gas can limit oxygen contact during juice handling. [[concept.sulfur-dioxide|Sulfur dioxide]] supports antioxidant and microbial protection; its effective fraction depends partly on pH and binding. The same addition therefore provides different protection in different lots. [@source.awri-sulfur-dioxide]

[[concept.oxidation|Oxidation]] calls for a deliberate choice: early oxygen uptake is usually limited, while controlled must oxidation followed by clarification is a specific alternative practice. [@source.oiv-so2-reduction-practices-2020; C]
:::

## Conditions and variation — what stays with the juice?

- **White:** many routes separate juice from skins early; whole-bunch pressing is one option. A winemaker can also choose controlled [[concept.maceration|skin contact]] first. This changes the opportunity for [[concept.extraction|extraction]]. In skin-fermented white wine, skins remain during fermentation. [@source.awri-white-prefermentation-skin-contact-2020]
- **Rosé:** direct pressing releases juice as black grapes are pressed; short maceration before separation is another route. With *saignée*, part of the juice is drawn from a vat of black grapes. Direct pressing also involves juice–skin contact, but does not require a separate maceration stage. [@source.ifv-rose-vinification-itineraires-11; p. 14]
- **Red:** skins remain in the fermenting mass. Free-run wine is separated from the solids, and pressing recovers another fraction. The chosen contact regime determines the timing. [@source.oiv-pressing]
:::

:::detail{#aangetast-fruit parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-managing-botrytis-fruit-2023"}
### Damaged fruit changes the options

[[concept.vineyard-hazards-diseases-pests-disorders|Vineyard disease]] can call for further testing and separate processing. With [[concept.botrytis|Botrytis]], one concern is **laccase**, a fungal enzyme that promotes oxidation in the presence of oxygen. Removing visibly poor fruit does not resolve that question; a targeted test may be needed. SO₂ does not inactivate laccase. For affected red fruit, the AWRI advises a rapid fermentation start and avoiding cold soaking. This responds specifically to disease, rather than prescribing how to treat a healthy harvest. [@source.awri-managing-botrytis-fruit-2023; p. 1–2]
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs — preparing the must

**Juice settling**, also called *débourbage*, allows suspended particles to settle before fermentation; the liquid is then separated from the deposit. Other separation techniques can serve the same preparatory purpose. This takes place at a different stage from [[concept.clarification-and-fining|wine clarification and stabilisation]]. [@source.oiv-must-settling; II.2.1.5]

The desired clarity involves a trade-off. Grape solids contribute lipids and nitrogen that can support yeast, while also affecting aroma and texture. Removing everything is therefore not necessarily the best starting point. [@source.awri-juice-solids-white-fermentation]

An adjustment starts with a specific need:

- **Sugar:** enrichment raises potential alcohol, for example through permitted sugar or must products, or concentration. Its purpose differs from making the finished wine taste sweet. Permitted methods are legally bounded. [@source.eurlex-wine-oenological-practices-20260818; Annex VIII, I.A–B]
- **Acid:** acidification or deacidification adjusts acid balance; assess both pH and titratable acidity. [@source.awri-acidity-ph]
- **Yeast nutrition:** measure available nitrogen and match any supplementation to the must and yeast. The broader feeding strategy belongs with [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]]. [@source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023; Feeding your yeast]
:::

:::detail{#zuur-en-ph parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-acidity-ph"}
### Acid quantity and pH tell different stories

Titratable acidity describes how much base is needed to neutralise acids to a specified analytical endpoint. pH describes the acidity of the solution and matters for microbial control, among other purposes. Two musts with the same titratable acidity can have different pH values. An acid addition therefore calls for checking both measurements and a trial on the actual lot; potassium and tartrate equilibria influence the outcome. See [[concept.acidity|acidity]] for its broader significance in wine. [@source.awri-acidity-ph; What is the relationship between pH and titratable acidity?]
:::

## Global context — the same question, different boundaries

Warm, damaged fruit calls for different transport and protective choices from cool, intact fruit. The meaningful comparison starts with that condition, rather than a fixed distinction between wine countries. [@source.oiv-so2-reduction-practices-2020; B–C]

Permission varies too. In the **EU**, Regulation 1308/2013 limits enrichment by factors including wine-growing zone and method; acidification and deacidification have their own conditions. Water addition is excluded except for a specific technical necessity. [@source.eurlex-wine-oenological-practices-20260818; version 18 August 2026, Annex VIII I.A–D and II.A.1]

For **Australian wine production**, Wine Australia's June 2026 guide describes a limited provision for diluting high-sugar must with water before fermentation to facilitate fermentation, with a lower limit of 13.5° Baumé. The guide also points to restrictions in import markets. Australian permission is therefore not a worldwide winery rule. [@source.wine-australia-compliance-guide-2026; p. 53, Standard 4.5.1]
:::

## Evidence and limits — observation and measurement work together

A sorting table assesses visible material; an analysis examines a selected sample. Together they provide a better starting point than appearance or one number alone. Record the lot, timing and treatment associated with each measurement. A lot that looks sound after sorting may still need targeted checks following earlier fungal damage. [@source.awri-managing-botrytis-fruit-2023]
:::

:::detail{#monster-en-troebelheid parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023 source.awri-juice-solids-white-fermentation"}
### Measurements change with preparation

Skin contact can change sugar, pH and nitrogen measurements as substances are released from the fruit. Compare samples prepared in comparable ways. [@source.osu-harvest-grape-chemistry-2023; Sampling matters]

**NTU** expresses turbidity through light scattering. It helps compare juices, but does not identify exactly which particles or nutrients are present. The AWRI describes no universal ideal turbidity for all white musts: fruit, juice extraction and intended style matter. [@source.awri-juice-solids-white-fermentation; Practical considerations]
:::

## Significance for wine — read the decision behind the technique

When reading a winery description, ask what material arrived, which parts stayed together, and why separation or adjustment happened at that point. “Whole bunches” means something different from “uncrushed berries”; “pressed early” describes a different contact pattern from “fermented on skins”. [[concept.pressing|Pressing]], [[concept.maceration|skin contact]] and [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]] then become understandable parts of a connected process.
:::
