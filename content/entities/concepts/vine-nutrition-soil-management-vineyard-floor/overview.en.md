A grapevine needs nitrogen, potassium, phosphorus, magnesium and other essential elements, but feeding a vineyard is not a matter of adding as much as possible. An element must be available in the root zone, accessible to active roots and delivered when and where the plant can use it. Meanwhile, vegetation or cultivation below and between rows affects water, erosion, trafficability, organic matter and competition. **Nutrition, soil management and the vineyard floor therefore form one decision system: understand and measure first, then intervene with purpose.** [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024] [@source.guerra-steenwerth-floor-management-review-2011]

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024 source.oliver-soil-quality-viticulture-2013"}
## Overview

**Plant nutrition** concerns chemical elements a plant requires for growth, tissue construction and metabolism. Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come largely from air and water; roots also acquire mineral nutrients. These include macronutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulphur, and micronutrients such as iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine and nickel. “Micro” describes the small quantity required, not a minor importance.

A **soil mineral** is not the same as a **mineral nutrient**. Quartz, limestone or feldspar names rock material or a solid compound; a root does not absorb a little stone. Elements first need to become available in a suitable chemical form in the soil solution. Root activity, water, oxygen, temperature and interactions with other ions then determine whether uptake actually occurs. [[concept.vineyard-soils|The soil profile]] provides the environment; this page follows what management and the plant do within it.

The **vineyard floor** is the managed space below vines and between rows. It may be bare, mown, vegetated, mulched or cultivated at intervals, and the under-vine strip may be treated differently from the alley. There is no single ideal appearance. The grower is balancing root growth and nutrition, erosion and compaction, machine access, and competition for water or nitrogen.
:::

## Structure and relationships

Nutrients occupy several pools. Some are held in rock particles and secondary minerals, others on clay or organic matter, within living organisms and residues, or dissolved in soil water. Only the last, small and fluctuating pool is immediately next to roots, but the other pools can replenish it or retain elements.

Bacteria, fungi and soil fauna decompose residues and transform organically bound elements. Roots and microorganisms alter local acidity and chemistry. Mycorrhizal fungi may extend the reach of fine absorbing organs, particularly for poorly mobile nutrients, but their presence does not automatically make soil healthy and their effects vary with soil, plant and management. Organic matter also contributes to aggregation, water relations, ion exchange and food for the soil web. [@source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020]

After uptake, [[concept.vine-as-living-system|the vine]] distributes elements among young shoots, leaves, roots, bunches and reserves in permanent wood. Some are later withdrawn from older leaves and reused. Fallen leaves, roots and chipped prunings may return to the soil; grapes and removed wood leave the site. Leaching, runoff, erosion and gaseous processes can open the cycle further. [@source.schreiner-nutrient-uptake-young-vines-2016]
:::

:::detail{#bodemleven-heeft-functies-geen-smaakrecept parent="opbouw-en-samenhang" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020 source.nrcs-soil-health-assessment"}
### Soil life has functions, not a simple flavour recipe

A biologically active soil can transform residues, support aggregation, maintain pores and retain or release nutrients. Yet which organisms occur and what they do change with moisture, temperature, roots, season, depth and analytical method. More microbial biomass or a longer species list is therefore not automatically equivalent to better uptake, healthier vines or better wine.

A strong claim needs a chain of evidence: an altered community or activity, a demonstrated soil process, a plant response and a relevant outcome. Jumping directly from a microbiome profile to an aroma or sense of place skips several uncertain links. [@source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020] [@source.nrcs-soil-health-assessment]
:::

:::figure{#voedingskringloop-in-beeld depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.vine-nutrition-soil-management-vineyard-floor.root-zone-nutrient-cycle"}
**From soil pools to vine and back**

1. **Pools:** elements occur in mineral particles, clay, organic matter, residues and any applied fertilisers or soil amendments.
2. **Transformation:** weathering and biological decomposition can release elements; organisms may also immobilise them temporarily.
3. **Soil solution and retention:** dissolved ions move with water or across short distances towards roots, while exchange, precipitation and binding alter availability.
4. **Uptake:** fine roots and root hairs acquire suitable ions. Mycorrhizal hyphae may extend explored volume, but they are one route within a larger root–soil system.
5. **Allocation:** the vine sends elements to growing organs and bunches and stores some in roots and permanent wood; demand and allocation change through the season.
6. **Return, removal and loss:** leaves, roots and residues may return; grapes remove elements; leaching, erosion, runoff and gaseous processes may cause losses.
:::

## How it works

A laboratory may measure a large amount of an element in soil while the vine acquires little. **Pool or stock** is the total or method-extractable quantity; **availability** is the fraction accessible and absorbable under current conditions; **plant status** is what actually entered tissue. The three are not synonyms.

pH affects particle charge, solubility and the chemical form of elements. In acidic conditions some metals dissolve more readily and may become toxic, while other nutrients become strongly retained. In calcareous soils, iron may be present yet functionally unavailable, with chlorosis as one possible result. There is no universal pH at which every element and every vine performs best; soil type, carbonate, rootstock and analytical method matter. [@source.tagliavini-rombola-iron-chlorosis-2001]

Water carries dissolved ions towards roots and sustains root activity, while prolonged saturation restricts oxygen and changes soil chemistry. Drought restricts transport and root growth, so a nutrition problem may appear even where the soil stock is not low. [[concept.vine-water-relations|Water status]] and nutrition are intertwined but distinct.

Elements serve different roles. Nitrogen is important to amino acids, proteins and leaf growth; phosphorus to energy transfer and membranes; potassium to osmotic regulation and enzymes; magnesium sits at the centre of chlorophyll; calcium contributes to cell walls and signalling; sulphur to particular amino acids. Micronutrients often act within or alongside enzymes. Both shortage and excess can cause problems, and adding one element may shift uptake or balance of another.
:::

:::detail{#vraag-opname-en-reserves parent="werking" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.schreiner-nutrient-uptake-young-vines-2016 source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024"}
### Demand, uptake and reserves do not move in lockstep

A perennial vine does not begin spring with empty stores. Roots, trunk and arms supply stored elements to early growth as uptake from soil develops. Later, leaves and other organs can redistribute elements. Peak soil uptake therefore need not coincide with the highest concentration in a leaf or the period of strongest berry growth.

Tissue concentration is also a ratio. Rapid growth can dilute an element through more biomass even while total uptake rises; restricted growth can raise a concentration without greater acquisition. Laboratory values must therefore be read beside shoot growth, yield, water status and developmental stage. [@source.schreiner-nutrient-uptake-young-vines-2016] [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024]
:::

## Conditions and variation

The same analysis can mean something different in two vineyards. A deep, active root system explores more soil than young or damaged roots. [[concept.rootstock|Rootstock]] and variety differ in vigour and acquisition. A heavy crop removes more of some elements; [[concept.pruning-training-canopy-crop-load|pruning, canopy and crop load]] change demand. [[concept.grapevine-phenology|Developmental stage]] determines which organ is growing and which tissue makes sense to sample.

The site also moves through time. Cold soil slows roots and transformation, drought slows transport, saturation limits oxygen, and intense rain can relocate soluble nitrogen or carry soil particles away. pH, carbonate, salinity, texture, compaction and depth govern how roots use the available volume. A yellow leaf may therefore fit a real shortage, an uptake constraint, root damage, water stress, disease or a combination. The symptom opens the diagnosis; it does not close it.
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs

Sound nutrition management begins with a question: what problem is visible, where does it occur and since when? A useful diagnosis combines:

- a map or field pattern of weak and healthy vines;
- growth, leaf colour, fruit set, yield and pruning mass across years;
- soil profile, pH, organic matter, roots, moisture, drainage and compacted layers;
- a representative soil test for pools and soil condition;
- leaf-blade or petiole analysis at a consistent developmental stage;
- the history of fertilisation, irrigation, floor management and extreme weather.

Only then comes the intervention. A soluble fertiliser can address a specific shortage, but dose, chemical form, placement and timing govern loss and availability. Foliar feeding may have a rapid or diagnostic role in some situations but cannot replace a functioning root zone. Compost, manure, mulch and other organic inputs supply more than nutrients: carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, maturity, salinity, volume and repeated use influence decomposition and soil condition. Lime, sulphur, gypsum and other amendments alter different properties; they are not interchangeable recipes.

Floor choice serves several aims at once. Vegetation may slow erosion, carry traffic, add organic inputs, influence infiltration and restrain vigorous vines through competition. In dry conditions it may also use water and nitrogen needed by vines. Mowing, rolling, grazing, mulching, shallow cultivation or seasonal termination shift the timing of those effects. Open ground reduces competition and may suit particular operations, but exposes soil more directly to raindrop impact, runoff, crusting and erosion. [@source.guerra-steenwerth-floor-management-review-2011] [@source.abad-cover-crops-viticulture-soil-2021]
:::

:::detail{#cover-crop-is-een-ontwerp parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.abad-cover-crops-viticulture-soil-2021 source.novara-vineyard-floor-water-conservation-2018"}
### A cover crop is a design, not an on–off switch

Resident vegetation, grasses, legumes and mixtures differ in roots, growth season, nitrogen fixation, height and competitive strength. Permanent or winter-grown; every row or alternate rows; alley only or under-vine as well: each design distributes protection and competition differently. Termination timing matters too. A winter cover mown or rolled before summer drought places a different demand on the water budget from a permanent, actively growing sward.

Benefits are similarly multiple: erosion control, trafficability, biodiversity or vigour regulation may matter more than added nitrogen. A successful cover is therefore judged against its stated objective and its side effects on water, nutrition, frost risk, labour, pest habitat and yield. [@source.abad-cover-crops-viticulture-soil-2021] [@source.novara-vineyard-floor-water-conservation-2018]
:::

:::detail{#verkeer-grondbewerking-en-amendments parent="keuzes-en-afwegingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.oliver-soil-quality-viticulture-2013 source.rodrigo-comino-vineyard-erosion-review-2018 source.nrcs-soil-health-assessment"}
### Traffic, tillage and amendments have a time dimension

Repeated machinery traffic, particularly on wet soil, can compress pores and increase resistance to roots. Loosening only the surface may temporarily improve aeration and infiltration while disrupting aggregates, speeding organic-matter loss or creating another compacted boundary. Deep ripping helps only when location, depth, moisture condition and cause are correct; a biological or drainage problem does not disappear because steel passed through the soil.

Amendments likewise do not act instantly or equally everywhere. Lime reacts with acidity and buffering capacity; gypsum supplies calcium without the same pH effect; compost changes organic inputs gradually; mineral fertilisers may be rapidly available but may also leach or accumulate salts. Trial strips, repeated profile observations and measurements over several seasons often teach more than a single whole-block treatment. [@source.oliver-soil-quality-viticulture-2013] [@source.nrcs-soil-health-assessment]

## Global context

[[concept.climate-weather-site-microclimate|Climate, weather and site]] change the priority. On a moist, vigorous site, permanent vegetation may help temper excess growth and carry machinery. In a dry location, winter cover may protect against erosion and then be terminated early to reduce summer competition. On steep or intensely rain-fed land, preventing soil-particle loss becomes a leading objective; on flat irrigated ground, salt movement, compaction and the traffic lane may matter more. [@source.rodrigo-comino-vineyard-erosion-review-2018]

Low natural fertility is not a universal quality ideal either. Excess available nitrogen may encourage strong growth and dense canopies, while too little may restrict leaf function, fruit set, reserves and yeast-available nitrogen in must. The useful range depends on variety, product, water, canopy and crop. Global viticulture therefore has no single best floor or fertiliser norm: the same practice can serve a different function on another seasonal calendar.

## Evidence and limits

A soil test describes a sample through a particular extraction method; a tissue test describes an organ at a particular time; a symptom records a plant response; yield and pruning mass record an outcome of the whole season. None proves the cause alone. Together, with a healthy comparison zone and a time series, they become much stronger. [@source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024]

Consistency matters. Compare the same tissue, developmental stage, comparable zones and laboratory method. Do not blend a weak corner and a vigorous centre into one average sample. Thresholds from one region, variety or rootstock are references rather than natural laws. Even a genuine shortage does not prove that more input is the best remedy; root health, water or pH may be the actual constraint.
:::

:::detail{#gezonde-bodem-als-functie parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.lazcano-healthy-vineyard-soils-2020 source.nrcs-soil-health-assessment"}
### “Healthy soil” should be translated into functions

Words such as living, regenerative or microbially rich become testable only when translated into specific aims: stable aggregates, adequate infiltration, root penetrability, erosion control, nutrient cycling or recovery after traffic. Changes in organic carbon often take years and depend on depth, bulk density and sampling. A short trial or a photograph of flowers does not establish lasting carbon storage.

Nor does functional soil make a direct flavour substance from slate, limestone or compost. Soil and management can influence grapes through water, nutrition, growth, crop and microclimate; fermentation and maturation then shape wine. That indirect route is important without being turned into literal soil flavour.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-monitoring-vineyard-nutrition-2024 source.guerra-steenwerth-floor-management-review-2011"}
## Significance for wine

Nutrition and floor management can shift shoot growth, leaf area, root activity, flowering, fruit set, yield, berry development and reserves. They consequently alter shade, disease pressure, the pace of [[concept.berry-development-ripeness|ripening]] and grape and must composition. Potassium, for example, can relate to berry and must pH; nitrogen status affects growth and nitrogen available to yeast during fermentation. Direction and magnitude depend on starting condition and never guarantee quality.

Read a producer claim through five questions. **What was the diagnosis?** **What objective did the intervention serve?** **What dose, placement and timing were used?** **What changed in soil and vine?** **Across how many seasons and against what comparison?** “No synthetic fertiliser”, “permanent cover” or “microbially rich soil” describes a practice or observation without yet explaining the wine.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Soil contains pools; roots encounter availability; the plant records the outcome. Sound soil management does not confuse the three, but connects observation, analysis, intervention and repeated review in one cycle.
:::
