:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-courtier"}
A **courtier** is an independent wine broker who brings a producer and merchant together. In Bordeaux, the courtier links the château and the [[concept.negociant|négociant]]: following available wines and the market, assisting negotiations over price and terms, and helping ensure that both parties understand the agreement. [@source.civb-courtier]

The courtier does not normally buy the wine on their own account and is therefore not a négociant. Their product is market knowledge, mediation and trust.
:::

:::section{#rol-in-de-handel depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-courtier source.fnscvsf-bordeaux-courtiers"}
## Trade — the third party at the table

A producer knows the vineyard and wine; a négociant knows customers and distribution markets. The courtier moves between those worlds. They visit estates, taste lots, understand available volumes and seek a buyer or seller suited to the transaction.

Independence is central: the courtier does not simply represent one stock of wine. They help information move between the parties, mediate the terms and follow execution. Within the [[concept.place-de-bordeaux|Place de Bordeaux]], this makes the courtier a specialised intermediary rather than another merchant in the chain. [@source.civb-courtier] [@source.fnscvsf-bordeaux-courtiers]
:::

:::detail{#van-monster-tot-bordereau parent="rol-in-de-handel" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-courtier"}
### From sample to agreement

The courtier may first locate wines and samples, taste them and advise producer and négociant about the market and quality. When the parties agree, a brokerage note or *bordereau* records the wine, volume, price, delivery and other terms. The courtier then remains involved in proper completion.

That mediation is especially visible during [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]]—selling while the young wine is still maturing at the château. The courtier helps connect offers and demand, but does not determine the price alone or guarantee how the wine will develop.
:::

:::section{#geschiedenis depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.fnscvsf-bordeaux-courtiers source.gcc1855-classification-history"}
## History — market knowledge before databases

Courtiers have existed in the French wine trade for centuries. They were particularly useful when distance, slow communication and variable lots made the market difficult to survey. By continually visiting estates and merchant houses, they built a living memory of quality, price and reliability. [@source.fnscvsf-bordeaux-courtiers]

That memory gained a famous public role in the [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 Bordeaux classification]]. The wine brokers' syndicate ordered the selected crus largely through established market prices and reputations. The courtiers did not “discover” quality in a single tasting; they formalised trading knowledge accumulated over years. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]
:::

:::detail{#onafhankelijk-maar-niet-neutraal parent="geschiedenis" depth="advanced"}
### Independent, but not outside the market

Independent means that the courtier stands between seller and buyer as a mediator. It does not mean that their judgement exists outside commercial interests, networks or historic power structures. Market knowledge can also reinforce established reputations and overlook less visible producers.

A courtier's advice should therefore be read as expert trading information, not as a universal judgement of quality. Producer, buyer, critic and drinker each asks a different question of the same wine.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
The courtier does not own the wine: they possess knowledge of wine, lots and markets. By independently connecting producer and négociant, the courtier makes transactions within the Place de Bordeaux possible and accountable.
:::
