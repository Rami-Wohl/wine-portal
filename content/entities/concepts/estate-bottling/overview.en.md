**Estate bottling** means that a wine was bottled at the named wine estate. On a French label, this most often appears as *mis en bouteille au château* or *mis en bouteille au domaine*. For PDO and PGI wine, such wording is regulated: the wine must come from the holding's grapes, be made there and not have left the holding before bottling. [@source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025]

The statement therefore says something meaningful about the chain of production, but it is not a quality rank. A modest wine may be estate-bottled; a great wine does not become great because of those few words.
:::

## Label — who put the wine into bottle?

Look on the front or back label for the small line naming the bottler. *Mis en bouteille au château* and *au domaine* indicate bottling within the producing estate. *Mis en bouteille à la propriété* is broader: under certain conditions, a cooperative cellar may count as an extension of the holding. *Mis en bouteille dans la région de production* says only that bottling took place in or immediately near the production area. [@source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025]

These phrases look similar, but they are not interchangeable. They help trace the route from vineyard to bottle: did the wine remain at the estate, go to a cooperative, or get bottled elsewhere within the region?
:::

### Not a seal of quality

Estate-bottled does not automatically mean that the wine comes from one vineyard, is organic, will age for decades or belongs to a [[classification.bordeaux-1855|classification]]. Nor does it say that the bottle was filled inside a literal castle: in wine, a [[concept.chateau|château]] is the name of an estate.

Its value lies in responsibility and traceability. The producing estate kept charge of the wine through bottling. Whether those decisions produced a good wine must still be judged from origin, producer, [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and ultimately the glass.
:::

## History — from merchants' barrels to château bottles

In [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]], even celebrated wines long left the estate in barrel. Merchants matured the wine and handled the bottling. That network enabled international trade, but the château name and the final bottle were not entirely in one pair of hands.

From 1924, Philippe de Rothschild had the entire crop of [[producer.chateau-mouton-rothschild|Château Mouton Rothschild]] bottled at the château. Other leading estates followed. The shift gave producers greater control over the final stage and made the bottle more clearly the recognisable finished product of one estate. [@source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe]
:::

:::detail{#de-precieze-woorden-tellen parent="waarom-het-belangrijk-werd" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025"}
### The exact words matter

The French terms operate within European and national labelling rules for wine with protected geographical origin. A similar English or foreign expression need not carry precisely the same legal conditions. Read the wording that is actually present before drawing a conclusion from it.

Even in France, *à la propriété* is not simply a more elegant version of *au château*: under certain conditions the former can include cooperative bottling, while the latter cannot. [@source.dgccrf-wine-labels-2025]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
*Mis en bouteille au château* says that an estate made the wine from its own grapes and bottled it there. It is evidence about origin and responsibility, not a promise of high quality.
:::
