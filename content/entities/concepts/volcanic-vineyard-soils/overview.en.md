‘Volcanic soil’ is a familiar but very broad family name. A vineyard may grow on basalt lava, ash, pumice, tuff or material weathered from them over thousands to millions of years. Age, climate and profile development can turn that origin into root environments that barely resemble one another. [@source.usgs-lithologic-classification] [@source.nrcs-andisols]
:::

:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.usgs-lithologic-classification source.maltman-vineyards-rocks-soils-2018"}
## Overview — one origin, many materials

**Basalt** is solidified lava; **volcanic ash** consists of fine eruptive material; **tuff** forms when volcanic fragments become cemented. Pumice, scoria and other materials add further variation. They may occur as loose deposits, broken rock or fully weathered parent material in a profile.

‘Volcanic’ therefore says something about origin, not automatically about texture, age, pH, fertility or water. Nor is **Andisol** a synonym for every volcanic soil: it is a soil classification based on specific andic properties. The general comparison framework lives on [[concept.vineyard-soils|vineyard soils]].
:::

## How it works — weathering makes the soil

Fresh hard basalt provides a different root environment from porous ash or strongly weathered material. Weathering forms new clay-like phases and oxides; organic matter and climate guide further profile development. Some soils with andic properties have low bulk density, stable aggregates and high capacity to retain water and nutrients. They may also bind phosphate strongly. [@source.nrcs-andisols]

This is not a rule for everything called volcanic. Young, coarse or glass-rich materials may store little water; shallow lava flows may limit rooting volume; humid volcanic landscapes may develop deep, strongly weathered profiles. Actual [[concept.vine-water-relations|water relations]] follow from pores, fine earth, depth, rainfall and roots.
:::

:::detail{#andisch-is-specifiek parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.nrcs-andisols"}
### Andic properties are specific, not decorative

Andic properties are established through mineralogical and laboratory criteria, not dark colour or the presence of a volcano on the horizon. Soils may have volcanic parent material without classifying as Andisols; some andic properties can even develop without much recognisable volcanic glass. Use the classification only when a reliable soil source supports it. [@source.nrcs-andisols]
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.nrcs-andisols source.maltman-vineyards-rocks-soils-2018"}
## Conditions and variation — from dry island to humid upland

In dry volcanic landscapes, wind, very low rainfall, young deposits and limited rooting volume may dominate vine performance. In wetter areas, the same broad geological origins can weather deeply into clay-rich profiles. Elsewhere, ash overlies older rock or lava, sediment and human-transported material are mixed.

A regional example must therefore describe its local profile. Santorini, the Canary Islands, Etna, Soave and the Azores do not share one universal ‘volcanic soil’; climate, topography, age, plant material and management differ fundamentally.
:::

:::section{#toepassing-en-keuzes depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.nrcs-andisols source.nrcs-available-water-capacity"}
## Application and decisions — name and measure the actual profile

Begin with the nature and age of parent material, then describe horizons, depth, coarse fragments and rooting limits. Next measure texture, bulk density, water and relevant chemistry. Only then can we tell whether a local ash layer stores water, a lava layer restricts roots or a strongly weathered profile binds nutrients differently. The volcanic family name does not determine the appropriate intervention.
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.maltman-vineyards-rocks-soils-2018"}
## Evidence and limits — smoke does not come from basalt

Volcanic rock contains minerals and elements, but roots selectively take up only dissolved, available forms. Plant metabolism, grape development and winemaking then intervene. A dark stone or ash layer therefore does not directly cause smoke, flint, salt or ‘minerality’ in wine. [@source.maltman-vineyards-rocks-soils-2018]

That does not make soil unimportant. It means the explanatory chain must be testable: which material, which profile, which physical or chemical property, which vine response and under what conditions? A tasting word alone does not complete that chain.
:::

:::detail{#marketing-en-classificatie parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.usgs-lithologic-classification source.nrcs-andisols"}
### Marketing name, rock name and soil classification

‘Volcanic wine’ can communicate landscape and shared identity. Scientifically, at least three levels remain separate: eruptive material, developed soil profile and formal soil classification. Confusing them assigns properties of one Andisol to every basalt vineyard or treats all ash as the same soil. Precise writing may use the public family name, then state which level was actually studied.
:::

:::section{#betekenis-voor-wijn depth="foundation" source_refs="source.maltman-vineyards-rocks-soils-2018 source.nrcs-andisols"}
## Significance for wine

Volcanic origin becomes relevant when it helps explain the current root environment: profile depth, porosity, water storage, pH, phosphate retention or a limiting lava layer, for example. Those properties interact with climate, variety and management within [[concept.terroir|terroir]]. The useful question is not ‘what does volcanic taste like?’ but ‘which soil developed here from which material, and what does it demonstrably do to the vine?’
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Volcanic is a geological family, not one soil type and not an aroma. The route to the vine runs through weathering, profile development, measurable properties and the local environment.
:::
