:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93 source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
**Passerillage** is the deliberate drying of ripe wine grapes before pressing. As water disappears, every litre of juice becomes scarcer and more concentrated. This can happen on the vine or after harvest, on racks, mats or in a ventilated room. [@source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93]

The word therefore describes a process, not one country, grape or wine style. The resulting wine may be sweet, but it can also ferment dry.
:::

:::section{#indrogen-met-een-doel depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93"}
## Overview — concentration through water loss

A ripe grape consists largely of water. When it remains hanging or is carefully laid out to dry after picking, the berry loses moisture and its skin wrinkles. Sugar, acids, pigments, aroma precursors and other compounds end up in less juice. The press therefore yields less must, but that must is richer.

This sounds simple, yet passerillage is not passive waiting. The producer chooses the harvest moment, sound starting fruit, drying place, airflow, duration and desired degree of dehydration. Every additional day can bring concentration, but also rot, oxidation or loss of freshness.
:::

## Methods — on the vine or after harvest

With **passerillage sur souche**, grapes remain on the vine. A dry autumn, sunshine and air movement can draw water from the berries. Meanwhile, the fruit remains exposed to rain, birds, insects and fungi.

With **passerillage hors souche**, sound bunches are picked first and then spread out or suspended. This may happen outdoors in sun and wind, or indoors on mats, in crates and on racks. The Italian term [[concept.appassimento|appassimento]] is often used for such drying. The OIV distinguishes natural drying from processes using regulated or forced air. [@source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93]
:::

:::detail{#lucht-temperatuur-en-tijd parent="aan-of-van-de-stok" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### Drying speed changes the result

Temperature, relative humidity, airflow, light, bunch compactness, skin and grape variety determine how quickly water is lost. Slow drying in cool, well-ventilated conditions differs from rapid dehydration in warmth. Even within one drying room, upper, lower and more tightly packed bunches may react unevenly.

Bunches are therefore inspected, moved or rejected. The goal is not the most raisined appearance possible, but a useful balance among concentration, soundness and the intended wine.
:::

:::detail{#geen-universele-techniek parent="aan-of-van-de-stok" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93 source.inao-cotes-du-jura-vin-de-paille source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento"}
### One family name, different rules

Passerillage is a broad technical family. An appellation may prescribe where, how long or at what temperature grapes dry; elsewhere, the final outcome is the main constraint. The OIV describes general practices, but that permission never replaces the rules of a particular origin.

Nor are the words fully interchangeable. *Appassimento* belongs to Italian traditions; *vin de paille* is a protected or regulated term in specific contexts. Process name, origin and local law must therefore be read together on a label.
:::

## The grape — more than concentrating sugar

Water loss increases sugar concentration and reduces the amount of juice. This gives yeast more potential alcohol or leaves room for residual sugar when fermentation stops. At the same time, acids, phenolics and aromatic compounds concentrate or change. The extent depends on grape, ripeness, drying rate and duration. [@source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021]

The berry remains biologically active during part of the drying period. Passerillage is therefore not the same as simply removing water from finished must.
:::

:::detail{#stress-en-stofwisseling parent="wat-er-in-de-druif-verandert" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### A harvested grape remains alive for a while

After picking, the berry receives no more water or sugar from the vine, but its cells still respond to dehydration, oxygen and temperature. Cell walls and membranes change; volatile compounds and phenolics may be formed, broken down or released more readily during crushing.

The eventual wine is consequently not merely a louder version of the same fresh grape. A long, cool process may preserve or develop a different composition from rapid warm drying, and grape varieties do not respond identically.
:::

:::detail{#concentratie-goed-rekenen parent="wat-er-in-de-druif-verandert" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### A higher number may have two causes

When a compound is measured per litre of juice, its value may rise because water disappears, because the berry genuinely produces more, or through both effects. Other compounds are consumed, oxidised or precipitated. Research therefore also compares amounts per berry or per unit of dry mass.

Without that basis, “increased during passerillage” is too crude. It does not reveal whether the grape changed chemically or merely retained a smaller volume of water.
:::

## Viticulture and winemaking — start sound and keep selecting

Drying magnifies more than desirable qualities. A damaged or mouldy bunch may deteriorate during weeks of storage and infect neighbouring fruit. Producers therefore harvest carefully, avoid crushed berries and monitor ventilation and humidity. Passerillage is not a way to turn poor fruit into good fruit.

After drying, berries are tough, sugar-rich and short of juice. Pressing takes time and yields little must. High sugar concentration stresses yeast, so [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]] may proceed slowly or stop before all sugar is converted.
:::

:::detail{#balans-en-stabiliteit parent="selectie-en-risico" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### Concentration also magnifies weaknesses

Acidity and phenolic structure can carry richness, but already-low acidity is not automatically refreshed through drying. Oxidation, unwanted microorganisms and volatile acidity likewise require attention. In sweet wine, the producer must prevent remaining yeast from restarting fermentation later.

The cellar can guide the result through pressing, temperature, fermentation and [[concept.elevage|maturation]], but it cannot repair every imbalance in the fruit.
:::

:::section{#verschil-met-botrytis depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93 source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
## Terminology — passerillage is not noble rot

In passerillage, water loss through exposure to dry air is the central action. In [[concept.botrytis|noble rot]], *Botrytis cinerea* makes the skin more permeable and the fungus actively changes the grape. Both can produce shrivelled, concentrated berries, but their biological route and aromatic effects are not identical.

Nor is [[concept.late-harvest|late harvest]] a synonym. Picking later may produce riper grapes without strong dehydration; it may enable passerillage, but may equally bring botrytis, rain or ordinary overripeness. “Late harvest” on a label does not automatically reveal how concentration arose.
:::

:::section{#wijntradities depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-du-jura-vin-de-paille source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento"}
## Wine styles — from vin de paille to Amarone

In [[region.jura|the Jura]], selected grapes for [[concept.vin-de-paille|vin de paille]] are dried after harvest and later made into sweet wine. The name recalls straw as a drying surface, although racks and suspended bunches are also used. [@source.inao-cotes-du-jura-vin-de-paille]

For [[appellation.amarone-della-valpolicella|Amarone della Valpolicella]], harvested dark grapes dry in a *fruttaio*, or drying room, before fermentation. The Valpolicella consortium describes a customary drying period of roughly one hundred to one hundred and twenty days. Amarone generally ferments dry: passerillage does not automatically mean dessert wine. [@source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento]
:::

:::detail{#geen-wereldwijd-recept parent="wijntradities" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-du-jura-vin-de-paille source.valpolicella-consortium-amarone-appassimento"}
### A family resemblance, not a global recipe

Straw wine, passito, appassimento and late-harvest wine dried on the vine share water loss, but not necessarily grapes, climate, drying place, duration, sweetness or maturation. Some styles pursue perfume and residual sugar; others colour, alcohol and structure.

The process family enables comparison. The origin entity remains necessary to understand which version is actually in the bottle.
:::

:::section{#in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
## In the glass — ripe fruit, but not always sweet

Wines from dried grapes may show raisin, dried plum or apricot, fig, candied peel, honey, spice or chocolate. They often feel fuller and richer in alcohol or sugar than wine from the same fruit without drying.

This is a range, not a tasting guarantee. Grape variety, drying rate, oxidation, fermentation, oak and bottle age together determine which aromas actually emerge. A raisin note alone does not prove passerillage.
:::

:::caveat{#beeld-is-geen-productiedossier depth="advanced" variant="uncertainty"}
A shrivelled bunch shows water loss, but not its full cause or destination. Drought, damage, noble rot and harmful rot may overlap in appearance. Without harvest context, inspection and production records, a photograph cannot prove passerillage or wine style.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Passerillage exchanges juice volume for concentration. Where and how the grape dries helps determine what is preserved, what changes and which risks increase; only grape, process, fermentation and origin together reveal the wine that follows.
:::
