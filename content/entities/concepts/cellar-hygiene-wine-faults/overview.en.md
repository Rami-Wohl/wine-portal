:::section{#overzicht depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-wine-faults source.uc-davis-winery-sanitation"}
## Overview

A **wine fault** is an unwanted deviation that impairs the intended wine. Judgement involves intensity and style: deliberate oxygen exposure during maturation calls for a different assessment from fruit loss in a young, fresh wine. A **taint** is a foreign aroma or flavour introduced through materials or the surrounding environment, for example.

Prevention starts at grape reception and continues through packaging. Investigation separates three questions: what can be perceived, which process or substance explains it, and what could still change? The same aroma descriptors can accompany different causes.
:::

:::section{#opbouw-en-samenhang depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-winery-cleaning source.uc-davis-winery-sanitation"}
## Structure and relationships — equipment and wine

**Cleaning** removes residues and deposits. **Sanitising** then reduces living microorganisms on the cleaned surface. The order matters: remaining material can prevent effective contact with the sanitiser. Sterilisation sets a more demanding objective and requires verification of the result. [@source.awri-winery-cleaning]

Contact points connect wine lots. A sampler, hose or coupling can transfer material from one wine to another. A cleaning plan therefore follows the entire contact chain, including the reuse and storage of equipment.
:::

:::detail{#moeilijke-contactpunten parent="opbouw-en-samenhang" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-winery-cleaning source.uc-davis-winery-sanitation"}
### What remains out of sight

Seals, valves and hard-to-reach areas deserve individual attention. A gleaming tank wall says little about residue behind a gasket. Cleaning, checking and protected storage complement one another; equipment contaminated again after cleaning can become a route of transfer once more.
:::

:::figure{#preventie-en-onderzoek depth="foundation" media_id="media.concept.cellar-hygiene-wine-faults.prevention-and-investigation"}
**Four connected activities**

1. **Clean:** remove residual material so that subsequent sanitation can achieve its purpose.
2. **Protect:** keep cleaned contact equipment clean until use and monitor the wine's condition.
3. **Compare:** record the observation and compare separate samples; appearance alone does not provide a diagnosis.
4. **Investigate:** choose suitable analysis and connect the result with the wine's history.
:::

## How it works — different routes to a fault

**Oxidation.** Reactions involving oxygen can change aroma and colour. Unwanted oxidation can diminish fruit. The meaning of that change depends on style and exposure; the chemistry is explained under [[concept.oxidation|oxidation]].

**Reductive aromas.** Volatile sulfur compounds can evoke rotten egg, onion or rubber. Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, can form during fermentation, for example, with yeast strain and nutrient conditions influencing its production. Here, 'reductive' describes an aroma character; finding the cause requires investigation of the compounds and process involved. [@source.awri-volatile-sulfur-removal]

**Volatile acidity, or VA.** This measurement chiefly concerns acetic acid. Small amounts also arise during normal fermentation; unwanted microbial activity can increase the concentration. Acetic acid bacteria require oxygen. Ethyl acetate is an ester with a solvent-like aroma that can intensify perception, but is distinct from the volatile acids being measured. [@source.awri-volatile-acidity-2018]

**Brettanomyces.** This yeast can produce volatile phenols with medicinal or barnyard-like aromas, among others. The presence of these compounds and the presence of living Brett cells are different analytical questions. Control addresses both spread and growth. [@source.awri-brett-control-2023]

**Cork taint and TCA.** TCA, or 2,4,6-trichloroanisole, is an important cause of musty cork taint. Contaminated oak can also be a source, so the word 'cork' does not establish which material introduced the compound. Investigation may identify other musty aroma compounds too. [@source.awri-oak-taints-2023]

**Refermentation and unwanted conversion.** Residual sugar can support yeast activity; remaining malic acid can allow bacterial conversion. Unwanted activity after packaging can alter the wine. See [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]], [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|MLF]] and the stability assessment at [[concept.bottling|bottling]]. Bubbles first call for investigation of their origin and the intended style.

**Mousiness.** This fault may become apparent only after tasting. The compounds responsible are difficult to smell at wine's low pH; conditions in the mouth change their perceptibility. Microbial formation is an important pathway and tasters differ in sensitivity. [@source.awri-mousy-off-flavours-2015]
:::

:::detail{#cellen-en-gevormde-stoffen parent="werking" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-brett-control-2023"}
### Stopping growth and removing existing compounds

An intervention that removes Brett cells does not automatically remove the volatile phenols they have already produced. Controlling the population and assessing the existing aroma problem therefore belong alongside each other. A treatment makes sense only when its intended objective is clear.
:::

:::section{#omstandigheden-en-variatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-microbiological-stability source.awri-sulfur-dioxide source.awri-volatile-acidity-2018"}
## Conditions and variation

Microbial growth depends on available nutrients, pH, temperature, time and protection. At higher pH, a smaller share of free [[concept.sulfur-dioxide|SO₂]] is present in the strongly antimicrobial molecular form. An earlier addition therefore tells you less than the wine's current condition.

Perception also depends on the wine and the taster. Ethyl acetate, for example, affects how readily the same acetic acid concentration is noticed. A detection threshold describes when something becomes perceptible; the point at which someone rejects the wine answers a different question.
:::

:::detail{#zwavel-is-een-stoffamilie parent="omstandigheden-en-variatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.wine-australia-sulfur-compounds-2022"}
### Sulfur compounds play different roles

SO₂, H₂S and aromatic [[concept.volatile-thiols|thiols]] are different compounds. Some volatile sulfur compounds contribute desirable varietal aromas; others cause a fault under particular conditions. A sulfurous smell is therefore not a direct measurement of added sulfite. [@source.wine-australia-sulfur-compounds-2022]
:::

## Decisions and trade-offs — from signal to action

A useful sequence for investigation is:

1. **Describe the signal.** Record aroma, taste, appearance and when the deviation becomes apparent.
2. **Establish its extent.** Compare samples from the affected lot and investigate shared materials or operations.
3. **Choose the analytical question.** Does it concern living microorganisms, a compound already formed or a source of contact contamination?
4. **Test the intervention.** Assess a targeted bench trial and check later whether the result lasts.

This summarises how prevention and investigation work together. Cleaning reduces routes of contamination; treating the wine serves a different purpose. For reductive aromas, the possibilities offered by aeration or copper treatment depend on the compounds and wine involved. Such intervention requires analysis and trials first; additional oxygen or copper can itself have unwanted effects. [@source.awri-volatile-sulfur-removal]
:::

## Global context — the same questions in different cellars

UC Davis highlights shared sampling equipment as a route of cross-contamination in both small and large wineries. Australia's AWRI discusses topping wine as a potential source of Brett contamination. Both examples show that risk can reside in supporting equipment or a small subsidiary lot.

In a 2015 publication, the AWRI linked reports of mousiness in white wine with changing practices, including higher pH and low SO₂. This is a reported practical observation, not an incidence figure for all white wines. The example shows why a stylistic choice also changes the monitoring required. [@source.awri-mousy-off-flavours-2015]
:::

:::section{#bewijs-en-grenzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-wine-faults source.awri-brett-control-2023"}
## Evidence and limits

Recognising an aroma helps formulate a hypothesis. Suitable chemical or microbiological analysis then investigates part of it. With Brett, targeted cell detection can reveal a risk before enough aroma compounds have formed to be smelled. Analysing those compounds instead addresses what is already present in the wine.

Results belong to a sample, method and moment. An observation carries more weight when comparisons and processing records support the same explanation. Writing down 'barnyard', 'musty' or 'rotten egg' alone does not identify the cause.
:::

:::detail{#latente-zwavelbronnen parent="bewijs-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.wine-australia-sulfur-compounds-2022"}
### Why temporary improvement calls for another check

Project AWRI 1701-3.4.2 identified polysulfides as latent sources of H₂S after bottling. The current aroma may therefore reveal only part of the potential for later change. The project also investigated how reaction products and conditions alter the effects of oxygen addition. Its findings support targeted choices and follow-up checks; they do not provide a universal aeration recipe for every sulfurous wine. [@source.wine-australia-sulfur-compounds-2022]
:::

## Significance for wine

When tasting, describe what happens before naming its cause. A fresh, clean tasting setup and, where available, a comparison sample help define an observation. Keep a stylistic preference, a sensory deviation and a confirmed cause distinct.

A technical sheet stating low sulfite or no filtration describes choices made. How well they work depends on the accompanying controls and the wine's behaviour. The transition to a stable package is explored further under [[concept.bottling|bottling]].
:::

Prevention follows the contact chain and the wine's condition. Diagnosis connects an observation with targeted evidence; only then can an intervention be matched to the right problem.
