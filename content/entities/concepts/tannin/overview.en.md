Tannin gives wine grip. You notice it as a drying mouthfeel, sometimes fine-grained and sometimes firm. It helps shape red wine, but more tannin does not mean more quality. What matters is how that grip fits the fruit, acidity and everything else.
:::

:::section{#mondgevoel depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-mouthfeel-2026 source.wset-tasting-guide-2018"}
## Recognising tannin

Tannins are a group of plant compounds belonging to the **phenolic** family. In wine they contribute to *astringency*: the rough, drying sensation on the gums and inside the mouth. This is a tactile perception, not simply a taste.

Bitterness is different, although both can occur together. [[concept.acidity|Acidity]], which makes the mouth water, and alcoholic warmth are different sensations too. A tannic wine may be completely dry or contain sweetness; *dry* in a wine description refers to sugar, not the amount of grip.

Describe both amount and texture: little or considerable grip, fine or coarse, supple or hard. “Abundant” and “hard” are not interchangeable. [@source.wset-tasting-guide-2018]
:::

:::detail{#speeksel-en-waarneming parent="mondgevoel" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.wset-mouthfeel-2026"}
### Why the mouth feels rougher

Tannins interact with proteins in saliva, changing lubrication in the mouth. The drying impression does not mean the wine actually removes all moisture. This helps explain why mouthfeel differs from an aroma you smell. Perceived texture arises where wine meets the mouth, not only inside the bottle. [@source.wset-mouthfeel-2026]
:::

:::section{#waar-tannine-vandaan-komt depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-wine-composition source.oiv-oak-maturation source.awri-skin-contact"}
## Where tannin comes from

Grapes supply tannins from skins and seeds; stems, if included, can contribute too. How much enters the wine depends partly on contact and handling in the cellar. Red wine is usually fermented on skins and consequently tends to have more noticeable tannin than whites made without prolonged skin contact.

White wine is not excluded. [[concept.maceration|Skin contact]] can give it distinct grip. Wood may also release tannins during [[concept.elevage|élevage]], the preparation of young wine for bottling. Grape and wood tannins therefore have different origins; a firm red need not have been aged in new oak. [@source.awri-skin-contact] [@source.oiv-oak-maturation]
:::

## From grape to wine

Tannin present in grapes and tannin in the finished wine are not the same thing. [[concept.extraction|Extraction]] transfers compounds from solids into liquid, but some can also bind to grape material or be removed during further treatment. Variety, maturity and cellar choices together determine what remains.

Contact time, temperature, developing alcohol and movement of the skins influence that transfer. Longer maceration is not a simple slider adding more of an identical tannin. The winemaker chooses not only *how much*, but which structure suits the batch. [@source.awri-extended-maceration-2019]

Choices remain after extraction. A winemaker may assess batches separately and blend them, or use a fining agent to remove some compounds contributing unwanted bitterness or astringency. Such treatment is not neutral: it may also affect colour and the rest of the mouthfeel. Small trials help establish whether the wine improves. “Remove all tannin” is not a meaningful general target. [@source.awri-fining-agents]
:::

:::detail{#kleur-en-tannine-uit-elkaar parent="druif-en-kelder" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-tannin-value-chain-2014"}
### Colour is not a tannin meter

Grape-derived red pigments, anthocyanins, are not the same compounds as tannins. During winemaking and ageing they can react together to form coloured compounds. That helps explain colour development, but does not make darkness a reliable measure of tannin amount or texture. Extraction, binding and subsequent reaction are different steps. [@source.awri-tannin-value-chain-2014]
:::

## Ageing and development

As a red wine develops, its grip may become more supple. This does not mean every hard young wine will age beautifully. Sufficient fruit and flavour must remain to support the structure; firm tannin alone is not a reason to put a bottle away for years.

Tannin belongs in assessing ageing potential, alongside acidity and flavour intensity. An immediately enjoyable red with little grip can fulfil its purpose perfectly. A wine with abundant fine grip may feel rich and harmonious. Assess balance, rather than scoring a single property. [@source.wset-tasting-guide-2018]
:::

:::detail{#zachter-is-niet-alleen-minder parent="ontwikkeling" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-tannin-value-chain-2014"}
### Softer does not only mean less

Research on old red wines shows that a softer mouthfeel cannot be explained solely by tannin precipitation. Older wines may still contain comparable amounts while composition and interactions with salivary proteins have changed. The simple sequence “larger chains, then precipitation, therefore softer” does not explain the entire development. [@source.awri-tannin-value-chain-2014]

A tannin measurement consequently does not predict an exact drinking window. Molecular changes, the rest of the wine and storage conditions must be considered together.
:::

:::section{#proeven-in-context depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wset-mouthfeel-2026"}
## Tasting and pairing in context

Taste grip alongside fruit, acidity and alcohol. Ask not only whether tannin is abundant, but whether the wine is cohesive and comfortable in the mouth. Words such as velvety, chalky or grainy describe a perception; they do not prove a particular soil or cellar technique.

Food changes the experience. A savoury, protein-rich dish may make the grip of a firm red feel less prominent. Serving temperature matters too: substantial chilling may make tannins seem harder. These are useful starting points for comparison, not a guarantee that every firm red suits every meat dish. [@source.wset-mouthfeel-2026]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Tannin means grip and texture. Its value lies in wine's balance and development, not in maximising roughness.
:::
