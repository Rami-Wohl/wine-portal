:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks"}
A **rootstock** is the lower, root-forming part of a grafted vine. The upper part — the **scion** — bears the grapes of the chosen variety. A vine can therefore produce [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] grapes while its roots come from different vine material. This combination became an important response to [[concept.phylloxera|phylloxera]]. [@source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks]
:::

:::section{#twee-delen-een-stok depth="foundation" source_refs="source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks"}
## Two parts, one vine

Picture a vine at eye level and follow its trunk down towards the ground. On a grafted vine, the **graft union** is where the upper part and rootstock have grown together. Above the union grows the variety whose grapes are harvested; below it lies the root system supplied by the rootstock. [@source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks]

This answers a common question: the wine-grape name usually refers to the fruit-bearing upper part, not the rootstock. Grafting does not turn Cabernet Sauvignon into a new grape variety.

## Why vines are grafted

European vines on their own roots often proved vulnerable to root-feeding phylloxera. Many rootstocks descend from North American vines that tolerate this pest better. Grafting European wine varieties onto them allowed growers to keep harvesting the desired grapes while reducing root vulnerability. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]

*Tolerate better* is the key phrase. A rootstock is not a general guarantee against every insect or growing problem.
:::

:::detail{#eigen-wortels parent="waarom-enten" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.osu-grape-phylloxera"}
### Not every vine is grafted

Own-rooted vines exist, for example where phylloxera is not a decisive local risk or where the plant material itself is less susceptible. “All vines in the world are grafted” would therefore be wrong. The choice depends on the actual risk and on the species or variety. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]
:::

:::section{#keuze-voor-plek depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks"}
## Choosing a rootstock for the site

Protection against phylloxera matters, but is not the only consideration. Soil, water availability, vigour, other possible soil pests and compatibility with the scion all play a part. A rootstock that performs well on one site need not be best on another. [@source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks]

Rootstock can thus influence growth and ripening indirectly. Yet a wine's flavour cannot simply be assigned to one rootstock: variety, climate, soil, growing and winemaking interact.
:::

:::detail{#grenzen-van-oplossing parent="keuze-voor-plek" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.osu-establishing-vineyard-rootstocks source.osu-grape-phylloxera"}
### No universal root recipe

Resistance to phylloxera varies among rootstocks and can play out differently against different insect populations. Nor is a given rootstock necessarily resistant to other root pests such as nematodes — microscopic worms. Above the graft union, a grafted vine can still be vulnerable to leaf diseases, frost or poor management. [@source.osu-grape-phylloxera]

Rootstock choice is therefore a trade-off, not a ranking from “best” to “worst”.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
The rootstock provides the roots; the scion provides the wine grapes. Together they can combine a desired variety with roots better suited to its conditions.
:::
