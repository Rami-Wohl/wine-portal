Once alcohol has formed, the wine still has more to say. Malolactic fermentation converts malic acid into lactic acid: acidity falls and character may shift. For many reds this is routine cellar work; for whites and sparkling base wines it is an important stylistic choice.
:::

:::section{#appelzuur-naar-melkzuur depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-microbiological-deacidification source.awri-mlf-success-2020"}
## From malic to lactic acid

Lactic acid bacteria convert the malic acid present in wine into lactic acid and carbon dioxide. This reduces [[concept.acidity|acidity]], generally making the wine feel less sharp. *Malolactic* refers to those two acids; cellar texts also use the abbreviation *MLF*.

This differs from [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]], in which yeast converts sugar into alcohol. Nor is it the [[concept.second-fermentation|second fermentation]] that produces bubbles in sparkling wine. Gas released by the conversion does not itself make a wine sparkling. “Malolactic fermentation” is the established name, but remembering that this is an acid conversion helps keep the distinction clear. [@source.awri-mlf-success-2020]
:::

:::section{#een-stijlkeuze depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-style-2020 source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020"}
## A choice for wine style

Malolactic fermentation is usual in most red wines. Besides changing acidity, it removes a substance that microorganisms could otherwise use later. That gain in stability is a reason to manage the process through to completion, not merely a way of making reds taste softer.

In white wine, the winemaker can choose between retaining more original acidity and developing a rounder profile. Sparkling base wines may also undergo MLF or be kept from it. Neither choice ranks above the other: both can belong in a convincing wine. MLF does not remove all acidity or necessarily make a wine heavy. [@source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020]
:::

:::detail{#volledig-gedeeltelijk-of-geblokkeerd parent="een-stijlkeuze" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020 source.awri-microbiological-stability"}
### Retaining acidity also requires protection

When malic acid is deliberately retained, unwanted conversion later must be prevented. Cooling can slow bacterial activity, but is not a lasting guarantee once conditions change. Appropriate microbial control, for example through sulfites and suitable filtration, belongs with that choice. The winemaker considers not only how fresh the wine tastes today, but whether that profile will remain in bottle. [@source.awri-microbiological-stability]
:::

:::section{#boter-is-geen-bewijs depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-style-2020"}
## Butter is not proof

The best-known aromatic association is butter. **Diacetyl**, a compound that may form through bacterial metabolism, plays a role. Not every wine that has undergone MLF smells buttery, however. Strain choice, timing and other wine components influence how much is formed and how noticeable it remains.

Oak aroma, [[concept.lees-ageing|lees ageing]]—time on settled yeast material—and malolactic fermentation are also different processes. A rounded white may have been shaped by several cellar choices. A buttery smell does not prove an oak barrel, while its absence does not rule out MLF. [@source.awri-mlf-style-2020]
:::

:::detail{#diacetyl-en-citroenzuur parent="boter-is-geen-bewijs" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-style-2020"}
### Aroma does not follow malic acid conversion alone

Diacetyl forms mainly through citric acid metabolism, not as an inevitable direct product of the malic-to-lactic step. Yeast and bacteria can also metabolise it further. “The malic acid is gone” therefore does not tell you how much butter aroma the wine must have. Acid development and aroma formation are connected, but are not identical gauges. [@source.awri-mlf-style-2020]
:::

:::section{#verloop-in-de-kelder depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-success-2020 source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020"}
## Progress and control in the cellar

The conversion may begin spontaneously or be started with a selected bacterial culture. A widely used species is *Oenococcus oeni*. The winemaker selects a culture suited to the wine; “spontaneous” and “selected” are not quality labels.

MLF need not wait until alcoholic fermentation has finished. In *co-inoculation*, bacteria are added during that fermentation; in a sequential approach they follow it. In either case, alcohol, pH, temperature, nutrients and sulfites act together. A suitable temperature alone does not ensure smooth progress. [@source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020]

Completion is checked by measuring the remaining malic acid. Chromatography can separate acids visibly; very low concentrations require more precise analysis. Waiting until bubbles disappear is not a reliable completion check. [@source.awri-mlf-success-2020]

## Stability and limitations

Completed MLF reduces the risk of remaining malic acid fuelling unwanted activity later. It does not sterilise the wine. Residual sugar, other nutrient sources, oxygen and unwanted microorganisms still require attention during [[concept.elevage|élevage]], the preparation of young wine for bottling.

There is also a tension within the process: malic acid disappears, but pH may rise. Higher pH makes protection against some microorganisms harder. After completion, the winemaker therefore reassesses protection and further treatment; “MLF finished” does not mean “ready for bottle without further care”. [@source.awri-microbiological-stability]
:::

:::detail{#bacterien-zijn-geen-uniforme-groep parent="stabiliteit-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-success-2020"}
### Desired conversion and spoilage are different

Lactic acid bacteria are not a uniform group with one beneficial effect. A culture that converts malic acid efficiently must also suit the batch's composition and its other microorganisms. During difficult progress, unwanted bacterial activity can damage the wine. Controlled MLF is therefore distinguished from the broad assumption that all bacterial activity in wine is either good or bad. [@source.awri-mlf-success-2020]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Malolactic fermentation changes acidity and may influence wine character. It is not a second alcoholic fermentation, a synonym for oak or a guarantee of butter aroma.
:::
