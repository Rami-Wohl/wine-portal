:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-style-2020"}
After alcoholic fermentation, a second microbial process can change the wine's character. Malolactic fermentation converts malic acid into lactic acid: acidity falls and the mouthfeel may become rounder. For many reds this is routine cellar work; for whites and sparkling base wines it is an important stylistic choice.
:::

:::section{#appelzuur-naar-melkzuur depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-microbiological-deacidification source.awri-mlf-success-2020"}
## From malic to lactic acid

Lactic acid bacteria convert the malic acid present in wine into lactic acid and carbon dioxide. This reduces [[concept.acidity|acidity]], generally making the wine feel less sharp. *Malolactic* refers to those two acids; cellar texts also use the abbreviation *MLF*.

In [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]], yeast converts sugar into alcohol; in malolactic fermentation, bacteria act on an acid. The [[concept.second-fermentation|second fermentation]] of sparkling wine has another task again: it creates the bubbles. The established name sounds related, but thinking of MLF as an acid conversion keeps the processes clear. [@source.awri-mlf-success-2020]
:::

:::section{#een-stijlkeuze depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-style-2020 source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020"}
## A choice for wine style

Malolactic fermentation is usual in most red wines. Besides changing acidity, it removes a substance that microorganisms could otherwise use later. Winemakers therefore manage the process both for stability and for a softer acid profile.

In white wine, the winemaker can choose between retaining more original acidity and developing a rounder profile. Sparkling base wines may also undergo MLF or be kept from it. Both choices can belong in a convincing wine. MLF reduces part of the acidity; the finished wine can still remain fresh and light on its feet. [@source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020]
:::

:::detail{#volledig-gedeeltelijk-of-geblokkeerd parent="een-stijlkeuze" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020 source.awri-microbiological-stability"}
### Retaining acidity also requires protection

When malic acid is deliberately retained, unwanted conversion later must be prevented. Cooling can slow bacterial activity while conditions remain suitable. Appropriate microbial control, for example through sulfites and suitable filtration, belongs with that choice. The winemaker considers the young wine's freshness and the likelihood that this profile will remain in bottle. [@source.awri-microbiological-stability]
:::

:::section{#boter-is-geen-bewijs depth="foundation" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-style-2020"}
## Butter aroma and diacetyl

The best-known aromatic association is butter. **Diacetyl**, a compound that may form through bacterial metabolism, plays a role. Strain choice, timing and other wine components influence how much is formed and how noticeable it remains, leaving many wines that underwent MLF with little obvious butter aroma.

Oak aroma, [[concept.lees-ageing|lees ageing]]—time on settled yeast material—and malolactic fermentation are separate processes that may jointly shape a rounded white. Butter aroma can fit MLF, but reveals little by itself about barrel use or the wider cellar approach. [@source.awri-mlf-style-2020]
:::

:::detail{#diacetyl-en-citroenzuur parent="boter-is-geen-bewijs" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-style-2020"}
### The route to diacetyl

Diacetyl forms mainly through citric acid metabolism, so it is not an inevitable direct product of the malic-to-lactic step. Yeast and bacteria can also metabolise it further. Acid development and aroma formation are connected, but follow different routes. [@source.awri-mlf-style-2020]
:::

:::section{#verloop-in-de-kelder depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-success-2020 source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020"}
## Progress and control in the cellar

The conversion may begin spontaneously or be started with a selected bacterial culture. A widely used species is *Oenococcus oeni*. The winemaker selects the approach and any culture to suit the wine and the desired degree of control.

MLF need not wait until alcoholic fermentation has finished. In *co-inoculation*, bacteria are added during that fermentation; in a sequential approach they follow it. In either case, alcohol, pH, temperature, nutrients and sulfites act together. A suitable temperature alone does not ensure smooth progress. [@source.awri-mlf-white-sparkling-2020]

Completion is checked by measuring the remaining malic acid. Chromatography can separate acids visibly; very low concentrations require more precise analysis. Bubbles alone provide too little certainty. [@source.awri-mlf-success-2020]

## Stability and limitations

Completed MLF reduces the risk of remaining malic acid fuelling unwanted activity later. Residual sugar, other nutrient sources, oxygen and unwanted microorganisms still require attention during [[concept.elevage|élevage]], the preparation of young wine for bottling.

There is also a tension within the process: malic acid disappears, but pH may rise. Higher pH makes protection against some microorganisms harder. After completion, the winemaker therefore reassesses protection and further treatment through to bottling. [@source.awri-microbiological-stability]
:::

:::detail{#bacterien-zijn-geen-uniforme-groep parent="stabiliteit-en-grenzen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.awri-mlf-success-2020"}
### Desired conversion versus spoilage

Lactic acid bacteria differ in their behaviour and suitability. A culture that converts malic acid efficiently must also suit the batch's composition and its other microorganisms. During difficult progress, unwanted bacterial activity can damage the wine. Controlled MLF therefore depends on the desired bacterium, suitable conditions and timely follow-up. [@source.awri-mlf-success-2020]
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Malolactic fermentation is a bacterial acid conversion that changes mouthfeel, stability and sometimes aroma. Its effect depends on the wine, bacterial culture, timing and subsequent cellar choices.
:::
