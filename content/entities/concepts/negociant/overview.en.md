:::summary{#orientatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade source.bordeaux-negoce-profession"}
A **négociant** is a wine merchant who bridges the distance between producer and market. In [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]], a négociant may distribute a château's wine to importers and other buyers, but may also purchase, select or blend wine and sell it under a house brand. The role is therefore not mere delivery: capital, stock, market knowledge and a sales network all meet in this profession. [@source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade]
:::

:::section{#rol-en-werkwijzen depth="foundation" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade source.bordeaux-negoce-profession"}
## Role and business models

One occupational name covers different activities. One négociant markets bottled estate wine under the château's name. Another buys grapes, must or wine, makes a selection or [[concept.assemblage|blend]], possibly handles [[concept.elevage|maturation]] and bottling, and sells the result under a merchant brand. Many houses combine elements of both models. [@source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade]

The distinction matters on the bottle. With château wine, the estate remains the producer behind the wine and the négociant is primarily its commercial route to buyers. With a merchant brand, the trading house takes much more responsibility for selection, style, presentation and continuity.
:::

:::detail{#wat-de-handelaar-overneemt parent="rol-en-werkwijzen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.civb-bordeaux-wine-trade source.bordeaux-negoce-profession"}
### What the merchant takes on

Wine may tie up capital for months or years before an end customer pays. A négociant can finance stock, store lots, organise quality and administrative controls, coordinate labels and packaging, and spread sales across different countries. Transport, currencies, credit and varying market rules also require attention.

These functions explain the role's persistence: even a famous château does not automatically possess a fine-grained network of importers, restaurants and shops around the world. The négociant aggregates demand and logistics, accepting commercial risk in the process.
:::

:::section{#plaats-in-de-keten depth="foundation"}
## Position in the chain

Within the [[concept.place-de-bordeaux|Place de Bordeaux]], the négociant stands between producer and onward distribution. A [[concept.courtier|courtier]] may broker the transaction between château and négociant, but normally does not buy the wine. The négociant does buy or receive an allocation, then sells to professional customers.

This route is not a law of nature. Some producers sell directly, some wines travel outside the network and one merchant house may perform several functions. The terms describe roles, not a compulsory row of counters through which every bottle must pass.
:::

## History

Bordeaux grew as a wine city through the connection between a large hinterland, navigable rivers and a port open to foreign markets. Wine commerce there can be traced to the Middle Ages; over the centuries, merchants gathered lots, credit and knowledge to connect fragmented production with distant buyers. [@source.boutoulle-bordelais-wine-trade-2000] [@source.msha-bordeaux-wine-history]

From the quays and warehouses of the Chartrons, this commercial world acquired a recognisable urban face. The exact work changed with [[concept.estate-bottling|estate bottling]], modern logistics and international distribution, but its essence remained: wine does not find its form only in the cellar; it must also be financed, traded and brought to the right market.

:::section{#macht-en-transparantie depth="advanced"}
## Market position and transparency

A large distribution network can give a négociant considerable information and bargaining power. Producers nevertheless decide what to offer and to whom; courtiers contribute their own market knowledge; buyers decide which price and stock to accept. The market is therefore interdependent rather than controlled by one party.

Nor is the full commercial route always visible to the drinker. Producer and bottling statements on the label help establish who made or bottled the wine. A distributor's name alone does not reveal how the wine was produced, while a château's name does not reveal how many hands the bottle passed through afterwards.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
The négociant makes wine tradable at scale. Sometimes the merchant distributes a château's wine and identity; sometimes it builds its own product from purchased wine. That distinction must be read afresh on every bottle.
:::
