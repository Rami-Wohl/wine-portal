**Vin de paille**, literally straw wine, is made from ripe grapes dried after harvest before pressing. Water loss makes juice scarce and sugar-rich; the finished wine is generally sweet and often sold in small bottles.

The name operates on two scales. “Straw wine” is an international wine family, while *vin de paille* is a protected traditional term for a limited set of French appellations. Actual straw is no longer the drying surface everywhere.
:::

:::section{#naam-en-familie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.eurlex-vin-de-paille-2023 source.wosa-wine-styles"}
## Overview — an old name, more than one tradition

Straw once provided a dry, airy surface on which bunches could shrivel after harvest. The name remained when racks, mats, crates and hanging bunches took over the same task. [[concept.passerillage|Drying away from the vine]] is essential, not the material beneath the grape.

Comparable wines appear as straw wine, Strohwein, slámové víno, *liastos* and [[concept.passito|passito]]. They share a process family, but not necessarily varieties, minimum drying time, sweetness, [[concept.elevage|maturation]] or legal protection.
:::

:::section{#drogen-buiten-de-stok depth="foundation" source_refs="source.oiv-passerillage-oeno-2-93 source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
## Method — harvest continues for weeks without the vine

Only sound, ripe bunches are suitable for keeping. After picking they lie in thin layers or hang with room for air. As berries wrinkle, their weight falls and the volume of pressable juice contracts. Damaged or mouldy fruit is removed along the way.

The producer monitors humidity, temperature, airflow and drying rate. Outdoor drying requires dry weather and protection from animals or unexpected rain; indoor drying requires ventilation and inspection. Neither is automatically safe.
:::

:::detail{#volume-inruilen-voor-concentratie parent="drogen-buiten-de-stok" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### Yield literally evaporates

Water loss means the same vineyard produces far less must. Remaining berries are tough and sticky, making pressing slow and juice recovery low. That combination of selection, storage, loss and difficult processing helps explain why straw wine usually appears in small quantities.

Concentration does not concern sugar alone. Acids, phenolics and aromatic compounds can concentrate or change under drying stress. Variety, ripeness and drying rate determine how much freshness and structure remain opposite the richness.
:::

:::detail{#drogen-zonder-uniformiteit parent="drogen-buiten-de-stok" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### A stack of bunches never dries evenly by itself

Berries within one bunch and bunches in different positions do not dry at equal rates. Air reaches edges and upper layers differently from crowded fruit. An average weight loss can therefore conceal sound, insufficiently dried and problematic berries at once.

Spreading, turning, moving and sorting again are substantive process steps. A prescribed minimum duration likewise does not mean every lot is ready at exactly that moment.
:::

## Origin and law — France restricts the term

European protection links the French traditional term *vin de paille* to [[appellation.arbois|Arbois]], [[appellation.cotes-du-jura|Côtes du Jura]], [[appellation.l-etoile|L'Etoile]], [[appellation.hermitage|Hermitage]] and [[appellation.correze|Corrèze]]. [@source.eurlex-vin-de-paille-2023] An arbitrary French wine from dried grapes cannot therefore carry the words automatically.

The style is especially visible in [[region.jura|the Jura]]. INAO describes Côtes du Jura vin de paille as a sweet wine from previously dried grapes, often with candied-fruit notes. [@source.inao-cotes-du-jura-vin-de-paille] Exact varieties, yields, sugar levels and maturation remain matters for each product specification.
:::

:::detail{#zes-weken-en-langer-rijpen parent="franse-bescherming" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-du-jura-vin-de-paille source.eurlex-vin-de-paille-2023"}
### A traditional term carries technical conditions

In the classic protected French context, selected grapes are dried away from the vine for at least six weeks: on straw or racks, or suspended. The relevant specifications also constrain varieties, sugar, yield and maturation; in the Jura context that includes lengthy maturation with a period in wood.

These are legal conditions for named PDO wines, not a global recipe for every straw wine. Current rules always take precedence over a historic bottle, older summary or translation.
:::

:::section{#druiven-kleur-en-stijl depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-du-jura-vin-de-paille source.eurlex-vin-de-paille-2023"}
## Grapes and colour — straw wine need not be white

Côtes du Jura permits [[grape.chardonnay|Chardonnay]], [[grape.savagnin|Savagnin]], [[grape.poulsard|Poulsard]] and [[grape.trousseau|Trousseau]] for vin de paille. The raw material can therefore be white or dark-skinned. Other protected origins use their own varieties and proportions.

The wine may be gold, amber, copper-toned or red-tinged. Colour follows grape, skin contact, oxidation and maturation; the word “straw” does not predict it.
:::

## Winemaking — sweetness from a difficult must

The concentrated grapes are pressed gently. Their must contains so much sugar that yeast begins under severe osmotic pressure and is later inhibited by alcohol as well. Fermentation therefore often proceeds slowly and stops with both alcohol and substantial residual sugar.

Maturation gives the powerful young wine time to integrate. Wood and oxygen can deepen nutty, spicy or dried-fruit characters, although choices vary by origin. Sweetness alone does not make a balanced wine: sufficient acidity and sound, clean raw material remain essential.
:::

:::detail{#stabiliteit-na-de-vergisting parent="persen-vergisten-en-rijpen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.sanmartin-postharvest-water-loss-2021"}
### Residual sugar requires control after fermentation

When fermentation stops, living yeast cells and abundant sugar may remain. The winemaker must clarify and stabilise the wine microbiologically so fermentation does not restart unexpectedly in cask or bottle.

Volatile acidity and oxidation also demand attention. Concentration amplifies not only desirable aromas, but may make a fault more emphatic too.
:::

:::section{#buiten-frankrijk depth="foundation" source_refs="source.wosa-wine-styles"}
## Global context — family resemblance without a shared law book

On Samos, Muscat grapes dry in the sun for the Greek *liastos* tradition. South Africa has its own category for naturally dried grape wine or straw wine, with its own requirements for grapes and residual sugar. [@source.wosa-wine-styles] Italy has many passito traditions, including [[concept.appassimento|appassimento]] in drying rooms.

These examples are related through water loss before pressing. They are not variants of one French appellation and are not legally interchangeable.
:::

:::section{#in-het-glas depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-cotes-du-jura-vin-de-paille source.wosa-wine-styles"}
## In the glass — small pours, broad character

Vin de paille and other straw wines may show candied citrus, apricot, fig, plum, honey, nuts, spice or caramel. Texture is often rich and sweet, while acidity can keep the finish lively. Red versions may add red and dark fruit or [[concept.tannin|tannin]].

Serve a small amount and first consider the relationship between sweetness, acid, alcohol and concentration. The aroma list is a range: dried-fruit scent proves neither a drying method nor a protected term.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Vin de paille is made by letting harvested grapes lose water before pressing. That simple principle produces little, highly concentrated must, but origin rules, varieties, drying method, fermentation and maturation determine which straw wine ultimately reaches the glass.
:::
