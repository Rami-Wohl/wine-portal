Château Mouton Rothschild stands on a low gravel rise in [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]]. Here *Mouton* probably refers not to a sheep but to an old form of *motte* or *mothon*: a mound in the landscape. The name suits an estate defined by a plateau where water drains readily and [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] takes the leading role. [@source.chateau-mouton-terroir]

Mouton was placed in the second tier in 1855 and promoted in 1973 to *premier cru*, the highest rank in the [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]]. Its [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]]—the château's principal wine—is often recognised by ripe black fruit, cassis, spice, polished oak and a generous yet long-lived structure. That exuberant profile is a signpost: [[concept.vintage|vintage]], blend, age and storage continue to shape every bottle. [@source.decanter-mouton-profile] [@source.decanter-mouton-vertical]

## History and ownership — an estate that fought for its place

The name Mouton predates the Rothschilds. During the eighteenth century, the property belonged, like Lafite and Latour, to Nicolas-Alexandre de Ségur; under the de Brane family it later became known as Brane-Mouton. Baron Nathaniel de Rothschild bought the estate in 1853 and gave it the name Château Mouton Rothschild. [@source.chateau-mouton-history]

The decisive figure was his great-grandson Philippe de Rothschild. He took charge in 1922 at the age of twenty and turned a seldom-visited family property into an estate with a distinct voice. Estate bottling, new cellars, artists' labels and ultimately the 1973 promotion followed under his direction. After his death in 1988, his daughter Philippine continued the work. Since her death in 2014, her three children Camille Sereys de Rothschild, Philippe Sereys de Rothschild and Julien de Beaumarchais de Rothschild have been co-owners. [@source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe] [@source.chateau-mouton-current-ownership]

Mouton is therefore both an old family holding and the result of deliberate change. The family name supplied resources and continuity; the present reputation was made as successive generations reshaped the wine, buildings and public identity.
:::

:::detail{#van-tweede-naar-eerste parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe source.decanter-mouton-profile"}
### From second to first—one word that took twenty years

Mouton stood at the head of the second growths in 1855. The list reflected contemporary trade reputation and prices, not a modern blind retasting. The family considered the placement too low, but the classification proved almost immovable. Baron Philippe campaigned for roughly twenty years to have it revised. In June 1973, a ministerial decree elevated Mouton to first-growth status. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history] [@source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe]

The promotion is exceptional because it was not an annual quality award. It changed Mouton's historic rank within one particular classification; it did not make the estate automatically superior to every second growth in every vintage. It did, however, crown a long period of investment, tighter selection and increasing market recognition, and made Mouton the youngest of today's five first growths.
:::

:::detail{#baron-philippe-fles-en-kelder parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe source.decanter-mouton-profile"}
### Taking the bottle back from the trade

In 1924, Philippe de Rothschild decided to bottle the entire crop at the château. Until then, many leading [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux wines]] left their estates in barrel; merchants handled storage and bottling. Through [[concept.estate-bottling|estate bottling]], the owner also assumed responsibility for the final stage before sale. Other major properties joined the movement. [@source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe] [@source.decanter-mouton-profile]

The decision demanded space. The 100-metre Grand Chai, designed by Charles Siclis, followed in 1926, allowing barrels to rest on a single level. A commercial choice was thus built literally into the estate: anyone wishing to deliver a bottle under their own name also needed to organise maturation, storage and presentation under their own roof.
:::

:::detail{#kunst-op-het-etiket parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-mouton-label-art source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe"}
### Every vintage receives a face

Poster artist Jean Carlu designed a striking label for the 1924 vintage. It initially remained a one-off experiment. In 1945, marking Philippe's return after the war and the Allied victory, Philippe Jullian placed a large V on the label. From that year onward, Mouton commissioned an original work from a contemporary artist for every new vintage. [@source.chateau-mouton-label-art]

Picasso, Miró, Chagall, Dalí, Warhol, Bacon and many others consequently appeared not on a museum poster but on a wine bottle. The art does not alter the liquid, but it changes how a vintage is remembered. Label and wine become a record of their time: one image for one harvest, while the Mouton name remains recognisable.
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — the quiet logic of the plateau

Most of the vineyard lies on the *Plateau de Mouton*, a gently rising mound that reaches 27 metres above sea level according to the estate. Deep gravel lies beneath a thin, poor topsoil, with clay and limestone below. [@source.chateau-mouton-terroir] The elevation is modest as mountains go, but meaningful in the [[region.medoc|flat Médoc]]: slope and porous gravel help excess rainwater drain away.

Cabernet Sauvignon is well suited to these warm, freely draining soils and forms the backbone of the red vineyard. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] offers a different ripening pattern and texture; [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] provide further options in the cellar. The final [[concept.assemblage|blend]]—bringing separate lots together—never follows the planting proportions automatically.
:::

:::detail{#plateau-in-cijfers parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-mouton-terroir source.chateau-mouton-winemaking source.decanter-mouton-profile"}
### Ninety hectares, not one even surface

Mouton currently reports about 90 hectares of vineyard. For the red plantings, the estate gives 81% Cabernet Sauvignon, 15% Merlot, 3% Cabernet Franc and 1% Petit Verdot, at 10,000 vines per hectare and an average vine age of around 44 years. [@source.chateau-mouton-winemaking] Those averages describe the property as a whole, not one parcel or one bottle.

The vineyard extends across several small gravel rises. Elevation, slope, stone size, clay, rooting and vine age vary locally. One early-ripening lot may therefore sit beside another that is harvested later, even though both carry the same estate name. [[concept.terroir|Terroir]] becomes practical here only when the team follows and vinifies the parcels separately.
:::

:::detail{#druiven-en-jaar parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-mouton-winemaking source.decanter-mouton-vertical"}
### Plantings are tools, not a recipe

The fixed planting percentages are sometimes read as though they were Mouton's formula. Yet the Grand Vin may contain considerably more Cabernet Sauvignon than the vineyard average, while another year may leave more room for Merlot. Ripeness, health and the taste of each lot outweigh a preset figure. [@source.chateau-mouton-winemaking]

A Decanter tasting of seven vintages from 1993–1999 illustrated this distinction between property and bottle. The 1995 season favoured earlier-ripening Merlot, while 1996 favoured later-ripening Cabernet; a recognisable Mouton family resemblance nevertheless remained in both. [@source.decanter-mouton-vertical] A grape ratio therefore helps explain what the cellar could choose, not what a taster must find.
:::

## Grand Vin — Pauillac with theatrical force

Château Mouton Rothschild is the Grand Vin: the principal wine for which the estate selects its most convincing parcels, older vines and vat lots. Cabernet Sauvignon almost always leads the blend. Compared with Lafite's more reserved reputation, Mouton is often described as more exuberant: cassis and dark fruit, exotic spices, cedar-like oak and a broad frame of ripe [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. [@source.decanter-mouton-profile] [@source.decanter-mouton-vertical]

“Theatrical” does not mean sweet or merely loud. Successful bottles combine concentration with freshness, length and enough tension to develop slowly. Young Mouton may smell generous yet taste closed; an older wine may weave the fruit into tobacco, earth and softer spice. The style is a family resemblance, not a prescribed tasting note.
:::

:::detail{#stijl-door-de-jaren parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-mouton-vertical"}
### The artist remains; the year changes

A vertical tasting of 1990s Mouton found recognisable cassis, exotic spice and polished oak, but also striking freshness and poise rather than sheer heft. The rainy years, the Cabernet vintage 1996 and the more Merlot-friendly 1995 each took a different shape. [@source.decanter-mouton-vertical]

This makes the sequence of artists' labels a beautiful but misleading metaphor if stretched too far. Every label is intentionally different; the wine does not change to a design brief. Season, harvest date, selection, barrel maturation and bottle age shift its expression. Storage history joins that list for mature wine: a famous vintage without a sound cellar remains vulnerable.
:::

:::detail{#strengste-selectie parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-mouton-wines source.chateau-mouton-winemaking"}
### Not every good lot becomes Grand Vin

Mouton says that no more than half of total production is selected as Grand Vin. The producer identifies outstanding parcels, older vines and the strongest vat lots as the basis. [@source.chateau-mouton-wines] That share is neither an annual quota nor proof that the other half was made with less care. It chiefly shows how narrow the selected top of the pyramid can be.

Individual parcels and varieties remain identifiable for as long as possible during vinification. The final blend emerges only through repeated tasting. A lot may therefore be sound and full of character yet fit Le Petit Mouton better. Selection is not simply about “best” and “inferior” wine; it also asks which combination carries the intended profile most clearly.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-mouton-wines source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe source.inao-pauillac-specification-2026"}
## Other wines — a second red and an island of white

Le Petit Mouton de Mouton Rothschild is the estate's [[concept.second-wine|second wine]]. Its first vintage, 1993, appeared as *Le Second Vin de Mouton Rothschild*; it has carried its present name since 1994, taken from Philippine's residence on the property. The wine comes particularly from younger vines that are harvested and vinified separately, supplemented by lots that are not chosen for the Grand Vin after tasting. [@source.chateau-mouton-wines]

Aile d’Argent is Mouton's dry white wine. It is therefore not white Pauillac: the protected Pauillac appellation applies only to still red wine. [@source.inao-pauillac-specification-2026] The white belongs to the same estate but follows a different designation and grape logic. Mouton Cadet must also be read separately: it is a brand of the family company, not Château Mouton Rothschild's second or third wine. [@source.chateau-mouton-baron-philippe]
:::

:::detail{#petit-mouton-en-aile-dargent parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-mouton-wines"}
### Two side paths with purposes of their own

Petit Mouton is harvested, vinified and matured with the same baseline care as the Grand Vin, but receives a different selection. Younger vines and the final tasting choice may produce a wine that opens sooner, although “second” guarantees no fixed flavour, price or drinking date. Its label draws on Jean Carlu's 1924 design for Mouton. [@source.chateau-mouton-wines]

According to current producer information, Aile d’Argent comes from seven hectares of sandy gravel. The plantings comprise [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]] and [[grape.sauvignon-gris|Sauvignon Gris]] together, [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]] and a small proportion of [[grape.muscadelle|Muscadelle]]; about half of the wine matures in new oak. [@source.chateau-mouton-wines] This white project broadens the estate, but says nothing about what is permitted under the red Pauillac rules.
:::

## Working methods and release — keeping parcels apart in order to connect them

Grapes are picked by hand, carried in small open crates, destemmed and sorted again. Gravity then takes them into the vats. [@source.chateau-mouton-winemaking] The purpose is less spectacular than the building may appear: move intact fruit gently and retain the ability to follow small, different lots separately.

After fermentation and selection, the red wines mature in barrel before bottling and distribution through the Bordeaux trade. Mouton participates in [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]], in which young wine is sold while it is still maturing at the estate. The name on the barrel is therefore already famous, but the final bottle still has time, blending and maturation ahead of it.
:::

:::detail{#hout-staal-en-tijd parent="werkwijze-en-uitgifte" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-mouton-winemaking"}
### Sixty-four vats and roughly twenty months

The present vat room contains 64 fermentation vats of different sizes: 44 in oak and 20 in stainless steel. Their capacities correspond to the parcels, so the team need not combine unlike grapes too early. [@source.chateau-mouton-winemaking] Vat material is one choice among many; temperature, [[concept.extraction|extraction]], duration and fruit quality remain at least as important.

Mouton describes about twenty months of maturation in new oak barrels for its red wines. During this [[concept.elevage|élevage]], barrels are topped up, racked and fined with egg white so that suspended particles settle. [@source.chateau-mouton-winemaking] This is producer-reported practice, not a Pauillac recipe. New oak and long maturation can affect texture, oxygen exchange and aroma, but only in proportion to wine and vintage do they become Mouton rather than a sum of techniques.
:::
