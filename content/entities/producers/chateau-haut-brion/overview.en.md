Château Haut-Brion stands in Pessac on the urban edge of Bordeaux. It is the only red first growth in the [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 Classification]] outside the Médoc and a red [[classification.graves|Classified Growth of Graves]]. That double position is not a double quality medal for every estate wine: each system has its own scope. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview] [@source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths]

The Dillon family has owned Haut-Brion since 1935. Its red [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]] usually blends [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|cabernet sauvignon]], [[grape.merlot|merlot]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|cabernet franc]]. Across vintages it is associated with fine-grained texture, savoury and smoky development and great length. These are recurring tasting impressions, not flavours automatically delivered by gravel. [@source.haut-brion-history] [@source.decanter-haut-brion-vertical]

:::section{#geschiedenis-en-eigendom depth="foundation" source_refs="source.haut-brion-history"}
## History and ownership — a cru with an early recognisable name

Documents from 1521 and 1526 connect the names Aubrion and Haulbrion with wine from this place. Jean de Pontac acquired the seigneurial rights in 1533 and began building the château in 1549. Under his descendants, Haut-Brion became a wine sold in London under a recognisable origin name when much Bordeaux was identified only by owner or broad region. [@source.haut-brion-history]

After several owners, American banker Clarence Dillon bought the estate in 1935. His family continued a long programme of modernisation; Prince Robert of Luxembourg represents the fourth generation. The Delmas family has meanwhile provided technical direction across generations. Ownership and daily winemaking form two distinct lines of continuity. [@source.haut-brion-history]
:::

:::detail{#londen-en-de-naam-haut-brion parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.haut-brion-history"}
### London made the place name internationally recognisable

Seventeenth-century English records identify Haut-Brion as a named wine. The Pontac family linked production, trade and hospitality, allowing customers to ask for a specific cru rather than claret alone. This gives Haut-Brion an important place in the history of estate identity.

It does not prove that one producer invented modern fine wine. Vinification, keeping and commerce changed across Bordeaux. Haut-Brion does provide unusually early, concrete documentation of an individual name.
:::

:::detail{#innovatie-zonder-rechte-lijn parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.haut-brion-history"}
### Innovation consisted of repeated adaptations

Haut-Brion bottled systematically at the estate from 1923 and later became an early adopter of stainless-steel fermentation vats under Jean-Bernard Delmas. These steps improved control and certainty of origin, but did not mark a simple passage from primitive to perfect.

Modern equipment still demands decisions about picking, extraction, press wine and blending. Historical importance lies in the combination of an early named cru, long documentation and repeatedly renewed craft.
:::

:::section{#wijngaard-en-terroir depth="foundation" source_refs="source.haut-brion-terroir"}
## Vineyard and terroir — a gravel terrace between city and streams

Haut-Brion lies on a terrace of small quartz-rich gravel between two modest watercourses. Sand, clay and gravel vary below. Drainage and soil depth affect available water and vigour; they do not prescribe aromas. [[producer.chateau-la-mission-haut-brion|Château La Mission Haut-Brion]] faces the estate and shares the broader terrace, but has its own vineyard and wine identity. [@source.haut-brion-terroir]

The city is not decorative context. Homes, roads and buildings surround a historic vineyard that escaped further urban expansion. Its microclimate and water relations must be understood locally, not as a miniature rural Graves landscape.
:::

:::detail{#druivenrollen-rood-en-wit parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.haut-brion-terroir source.haut-brion-wines"}
### Red and white require different grapes and selections

Cabernet sauvignon, merlot and cabernet franc each offer different possibilities for red wine structure, ripeness and aroma. Exact proportions change yearly. Château Haut-Brion Blanc uses [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|sauvignon blanc]] and [[grape.semillon|sémillon]]: sauvignon can add tension and aromatic clarity, sémillon volume and waxy development.

This white Grand Vin is not a white version of first-growth status. The 1855 classification concerns red Haut-Brion; the Graves classification also recognises the estate for red.
:::

:::detail{#stedelijk-terroir-en-behoud parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.haut-brion-history source.haut-brion-terroir"}
### Preservation is part of the modern terroir story

An enclosed urban vineyard cannot expand indefinitely or move parcels easily. Urban pressure makes continuity of existing land and protection from fragmentation especially important.

This does not mean city conditions automatically create ripeness or quality. It does show that terroir is maintained through land use, ownership and protection as well as soil and climate.
:::

:::section{#grand-vin depth="foundation" source_refs="source.haut-brion-wines source.decanter-haut-brion-vertical"}
## Grand Vin — refinement with a savoury signature

Château Haut-Brion Rouge is the red Grand Vin. Vertical tastings show power expressed through more than massive tannin: fine structure, dark fruit, tobacco, earth and smoky or warm-brick associations can appear together. Savoury notes often become clearer with bottle age. [@source.decanter-haut-brion-vertical]

No aroma is compulsory. “Smoky” is a recurring sensory association, not literal smoke or the direct taste of gravel. Vintage, blend, maturation and bottle condition shape the actual glass.
:::

:::detail{#merlot-en-textuur parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-haut-brion-vertical"}
### Merlot softens the profile without making it simple

Haut-Brion generally uses more merlot than several Médoc first growths. This can support a rounder middle and less pointed cassis character, while cabernet sauvignon and cabernet franc contribute length and aromatic tension.

The blend remains vintage-specific. A grape's share is only one factor beside ripeness, extraction, press wine and barrel selection.
:::

:::detail{#oude-fles-en-particulier-karakter parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.haut-brion-history source.decanter-haut-brion-vertical"}
### “Particular” is historically documented, not analytically fixed

Samuel Pepys recorded in 1663 a wine called Ho Bryan with a taste unlike any he had met. The observation matters as evidence of recognition and reputation, but supplies no modern chemical or sensory profile.

Old bottles demand equal caution. A historic name does not guarantee condition; provenance, fill, cork and storage must be assessed before stylistic comparison becomes meaningful.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.haut-brion-wines source.mission-haut-brion-wines"}
## Other wines — red, white and shared selections

Le Clarence de Haut-Brion is the [[concept.second-wine|second red wine]]; before the 2007 vintage it was Bahans Haut-Brion. It comes from the same red-vineyard context but carries another lot selection and is generally approachable earlier. [@source.haut-brion-wines]

Château Haut-Brion Blanc is a rare dry white Grand Vin from sauvignon blanc and sémillon. La Clarté de Haut-Brion is the shared second white of Haut-Brion and La Mission Haut-Brion. Common ownership and cellar organisation connect these wines, while their labels continue to mark distinct origins and selections. [@source.mission-haut-brion-wines]
:::

:::detail{#classificatie-volgt-kleur-en-wijn parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths source.haut-brion-wines"}
### The most famous rank does not automatically belong to the white bottle

“Château Haut-Brion” on a white bottle means the wine belongs to the estate, not that it is a white 1855 first growth. Nor should the red Graves classification be transferred to the white Grand Vin.

Always read colour, cuvée, appellation and classification statement together. Ownership is an organisational fact; classification has a defined wine and historical scope.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-opvoeding depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.haut-brion-wines source.decanter-haut-brion-vertical"}
## Winemaking and maturation — control without uniformity

Red grapes are selected and fermented separately by parcel and ripeness. [[concept.extraction|Extraction]] and use of press wine are assessed lot by lot before the [[concept.assemblage|blend]] is assembled. Barrel [[concept.elevage|maturation]] integrates structure and aroma before final fining and bottling.

Technique serves difference rather than standardisation. Every vintage calls for another balance of grapes and barrels. The Grand Vin is sold [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]]; an early barrel tasting can show direction but not development after bottling or decades of storage.
:::
