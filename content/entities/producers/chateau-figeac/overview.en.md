**Château Figeac** lies on the western side of [[appellation.saint-emilion-grand-cru|Saint-Émilion Grand Cru]], where three gravel rises emerge from the gentler landscape. The Manoncourt family has owned the estate since 1892, and the [[classification.saint-emilion|2022 classification]] made it one of two Premiers Grands Crus Classés holding distinction A.

The [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]] is simply called Château Figeac. Its vineyard makes it immediately recognizable within [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]]: [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] together account for seventy percent of the plantings, alongside [[grape.merlot|Merlot]]. Its style is consequently sought more in fragrance, freshness, fine structure and slowly unfolding complexity than in ripeness or volume alone.

## History and ownership — a great estate became a family inheritance

Figeac was once far larger than the château of today. In the eighteenth century, the historic estate covered about two hundred hectares according to research cited by Decanter. Financial difficulties led to sales and subdivision from 1832 onwards; several names around Saint-Émilion still echo that former expanse. [@source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile]

The line that shaped modern Figeac began in 1892, when Henriette de Chèvremont and André Villepigue bought the estate. Their descendant Thierry Manoncourt took charge in 1947 and remained connected to Figeac for more than sixty harvests. The château stayed with the family after his death in 2010. Marie-France Manoncourt, Hortense Idoine Manoncourt and Blandine de Brier Manoncourt each hold a governance or representative role today; Frédéric Faye has been managing director since 2013. [@source.chateau-figeac-builders]
:::

:::detail{#thierry-manoncourt parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-figeac-builders source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
### Thierry Manoncourt made continuity an active choice

Manoncourt was an agricultural engineer who initially came to Figeac to help for a short time. He stayed. Under his direction, the unusual varietal balance took on its enduring form, a second wine appeared as early as 1945, and stainless-steel vats were used alongside wood in the 1970s — an innovative step on the Right Bank at the time. [@source.chateau-figeac-builders]

That story matters more than a list of firsts: the present identity was not inherited unchanged, but built through successive decisions. The family preserves that line, while the team has sharpened parcel selection, cellar logistics and precision since 2013.
:::

:::detail{#het-oude-figeac parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
### Old Figeac lives on in its neighbours

Parts of the historic holding became independent through nineteenth-century sales. A parcel around the place known as *Au Cheval-Blanc* became part of what would later be [[producer.chateau-cheval-blanc|Château Cheval Blanc]]. The connection between the names is therefore historical and geographical, not organizational: Cheval Blanc and present-day Figeac are separate producers with their own vineyards, wines and classification histories.

The former two hundred hectares must not be read as the estate's current size either. They explain why “Figeac” appears in several château names around the western edge of Saint-Émilion; they confer neither Figeac's identity nor its rank on those businesses.
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — three islands of gravel

The estate covers 54 contiguous hectares, of which about 41 hectares are currently planted to vines. Three rises of gravel containing quartz and flint form its core, with clay deeper below. Between vineyard, château, park, water and woodland, almost a quarter of the property remains unplanted. [@source.chateau-figeac-place]

Gravel drains water readily and warms quickly; clay can retain water at depth. This helps explain why Cabernet can play such a prominent role here, but [[concept.terroir|terroir]] is not an aroma machine. Elevation, root depth, water availability, microclimate, yield and harvest timing all interact before a grape reaches the cellar.
:::

:::detail{#drie-druiven parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-figeac-place source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
### Cabernet is no supporting player here

The present vineyard is planted to 35% Cabernet Sauvignon, 35% Cabernet Franc and 30% Merlot. [@source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile] That is unusual in an appellation where Merlot dominates many estates. Cabernet Sauvignon can bring firmness, blackcurrant depth and a cool line; Cabernet Franc fragrance and tension; Merlot roundness and mid-palate.

Those words describe functions, not fixed flavour compartments. The final [[concept.assemblage|blend]] follows the quality of sites and grapes in a particular [[concept.vintage|vintage]]. Figeac therefore need never reproduce its vineyard percentages exactly in the bottle.
:::

:::detail{#oude-stokken-en-selectie parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
### Genetic memory beside replanting

After the severe frost of 1956, roughly half of the vineyard had to be replanted. Between 2008 and 2015, Figeac also established a small heritage plot using [[concept.massal-selection|massal selections]] from its own old vines. [@source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile]

Such a plot preserves genetic diversity within the estate, but age does not automatically confer quality. Health, yield, site and vintage remain decisive. Its value lies chiefly in choice: future planting material need not come from a single uniform clone.
:::

## Grand Vin — Cabernet speaks on the Right Bank

Château Figeac is the Grand Vin and the estate's clearest answer to its own vineyard. The high proportion of Cabernet gives it a recognizable direction within Saint-Émilion: blackcurrant, floral or herbal lift, cedar and graphite are recurring reference points, carried by freshness and fine but serious [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. Tobacco, earth and dried aromas may emerge with bottle age.

This is a bounded style portrait. Warm and cool years shift ripeness, proportions and texture; young wine can be closed or stern, while an old bottle depends entirely on storage. “Figeac style” is therefore not an aroma formula, but a recurring balance between Cabernet structure, Merlot breadth and the pace of the vintage. [@source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile]
:::

:::detail{#precisie-zonder-stijlbreuk parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
### Greater precision, not a new identity

Decanter describes the wines since the 2013 reorganization as more intense and polished without a major change to their fundamental style. Clarity of fruit, texture and the picking time of the Cabernets received particular attention. [@source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile]

That distinction avoids an easy story in which a new cellar automatically makes a new wine. Building, selection and technique can give a team more control; identity still depends on grapes, parcels and decisions in each individual year.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-figeac-wine source.chateau-figeac-builders source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
## Other wines — Petit-Figeac is a selection, not another address

Petit-Figeac is the estate's [[concept.second-wine|second wine]]. Figeac has produced a second selection since the 1945 harvest; it was called *La Grange Neuve de Figeac* through 2011 and has carried its present name since 2012. The grapes come solely from the estate, and the choice of which lots become Grand Vin or Petit-Figeac is made during blending. [@source.chateau-figeac-wine]

The producer describes Petit-Figeac as lighter, earlier to approach and less full-bodied than the Grand Vin. That does not make it a miniature with exactly the same proportions or evolution. Second wine here means a different selection and maturation within the same harvest, not automatically wine from young vines or one fixed parcel.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-opvoeding depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-figeac-builders source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
## Winemaking and maturation — more vats preserve more differences

Figeac harvests by hand and keeps lots separate for as long as possible. The new, partly underground winery entered use for the 2021 harvest. More and smaller vats allow parcels and picking dates to be followed with greater precision before the blend is decided. Gravity reduces the pumping needed to move grapes and young wine. [@source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile]

The point is not technological display, but room to decide. Cabernet Franc and Cabernet Sauvignon may ripen at a different moment from Merlot; separate fermentation allows the team to determine later which lot contributes to which wine and in what proportion.
:::

:::detail{#keldercontouren parent="wijnmaken-en-opvoeding" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
### Oak is a powerful frame

Under recent estate practice, the Grand Vin ferments partly in wooden vats and then undergoes a long [[concept.elevage|maturation]] in new oak, generally for up to about eighteen months. [@source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile] New oak can affect aroma, tannin and oxygen exchange; it is neither independent proof of quality nor the sole source of cedar-like notes.

These technical outlines are snapshots, not appellation rules. Barrel size, [[concept.extraction|extraction]] and duration may change by lot or future vintage. Anyone studying a particular bottle should therefore connect its method to the year concerned.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-reputatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.chateau-figeac-builders source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
## Classification and reputation — the A arrived in 2022

Figeac was listed as a Premier Grand Cru Classé from the first classification in 1955. In the 2022 edition, the château was promoted to Premier Grand Cru Classé with distinction A, alongside [[producer.chateau-pavie|Château Pavie]]. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022]

The promotion changed its formal position; it did not retroactively change the wine in older bottles. The 1995 label shown on this page correctly states only *Premier Grand Cru Classé*. Rank always belongs to the edition in force at the time.
:::

:::detail{#wat-de-a-wel-en-niet-zegt parent="classificatie-en-reputatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
### Recognition after assessment, not an eternal verdict

For the 2022 classification, quality and consistency across multiple years were assessed alongside reputation, the estate and its management. Figeac's new rank recognizes the outcome of that process and applies within this revisable edition.

It does not guarantee that every bottle stands above every other Saint-Émilion, nor that the next classification will be identical. Rank is useful context; producer, year, development and direct observation remain necessary to understand the wine.
:::

:::section{#etiket-serveren-en-bewaren depth="foundation" source_refs="source.chateau-figeac-wine source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
## Label, serving and storage — read the year first

A current bottle carries Château Figeac, Saint-Émilion Grand Cru and the applicable classification wording. Older bottles may state the rank differently without the label being wrong. Always check [[concept.vintage|vintage]], fill level, capsule, cork and bottle provenance first.

Young Figeac may benefit from air and a generous glass; old wine instead calls for gentle pouring and little dogma. Sediment is normal, but lengthy decanting can lose fragile aromas. Cool, stable storage matters more than any universal number of years to wait.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Figeac shows that “Right Bank” does not mean “as much Merlot as possible”. Three gravel rises, two Cabernets and careful selection give the château a grammar of its own within Saint-Émilion.
:::

:::caveat{#geen-vast-recept depth="advanced" variant="simplification" source_refs="source.chateau-figeac-place source.decanter-chateau-figeac-profile"}
Gravel plus Cabernet does not automatically produce the style of Figeac. Recognition emerges from site, plant material, ripeness, blend, cellar choices and bottle development together.
:::
