Château Lafite Rothschild stands at the quiet northern edge of [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]], where slight gravel rises give way to woods and marshes. Its name is thought to derive from the Gascon *La Hite*, a small hillock—a modest word for an estate that became one of the most famous names in [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate]

Lafite is a *premier cru*, the highest tier in the red-wine [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]], and has belonged to the Rothschild family since 1868. Its [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]]—the château's principal wine—is generally Cabernet-led and associated more with finesse, fragrance and slowly unfolding complexity than immediate display. Blackcurrant, cedar and floral notes are useful signposts, not a fixed recipe for every vintage or bottle. [@source.decanter-bordeaux-first-growths]

## History and ownership — an old name, a constructed reputation

The name Lafite appears in a document from 1234, according to the estate. That date marks an early reference, not the beginning of the vineyard as it exists today. Independent histories place deliberate vineyard development mainly in the late seventeenth century under Jacques de Ségur. His grandson Nicolas-Alexandre de Ségur further advanced the wine's quality and reputation during the eighteenth century. [@source.chateau-lafite-history] [@source.decanter-lafite-profile]

Lafite became a first growth in 1855. Thirteen years later, on 8 August 1868, Baron James de Rothschild bought the property. It has remained within this branch of the family and forms the historic heart of Domaines Barons de Rothschild (Lafite). Saskia de Rothschild has led the family company since 2018, succeeding her father Baron Éric. [@source.chateau-lafite-history] [@source.decanter-bordeaux-first-growths]

That long family line does not make Lafite timeless or unchanging. Its reputation began before 1868 and has been guarded afresh ever since: in the vineyard, in the choice of which lots become Grand Vin and in the confidence not to make a world-famous name speak more loudly than the wine itself.
:::

:::detail{#handel-en-hof parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-lafite-profile"}
### Trade and court made Lafite recognisable early

By the eighteenth century, Lafite was already sought outside France as a named origin. British prime minister Robert Walpole arranged regular shipments, while at Versailles the wine was served by Madame de Pompadour and later Madame du Barry. [@source.decanter-lafite-profile]

These were more than colourful names in a guest book. Bordeaux wine was generally sold through merchants as a cask product; an estate that buyers began requesting by name could build a reputation and price history of its own. Lafite's 1855 position did not appear from nowhere: the classification formalised a market standing created over generations.
:::

:::detail{#breuken-en-overdracht parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-lafite-profile source.chateau-lafite-hard-graft source.decanter-bordeaux-first-growths"}
### Revolution, phylloxera and generational change

Ownership before the Rothschild era was anything but calm. After debts forced the Ségur family to relinquish Lafite, it passed to Nicolas-Pierre de Pichard. He was executed during the French Revolution; the property was auctioned publicly and subsequently passed through Dutch and English hands before Baron James acquired it in 1868. [@source.decanter-lafite-profile]

A crisis that money alone could not solve followed soon after. [[concept.phylloxera|Phylloxera]] was identified at Lafite in 1876. The estate reports that production fell from 246 barrels in 1875 to 83 in 1876 and remained below two hundred for more than a decade. Lafite long resisted grafting onto resistant American [[concept.rootstock|rootstocks]], fearing a loss of quality, but eventually adopted the solution. [@source.chateau-lafite-hard-graft]

The 2018 handover from Baron Éric to Saskia de Rothschild was less dramatic but remains consequential: it connects six generations of family ownership to an agenda in which soil life, genetic diversity and climate adaptation have become more explicit. [@source.decanter-bordeaux-first-growths]
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — gravel between woods and marshes

Lafite does not occupy one monumental hill, but gently undulating rises at the northern end of Pauillac. The estate describes fine, deep gravel mixed with windblown sand above a well-drained Tertiary limestone subsoil. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate] Gravel lets rainwater move down and warms readily; sand, limestone and local variation alter how water and roots behave from parcel to parcel.

The vineyard also sits within a landscape of woods and marshes. This makes the romantic château view functional: high and dry enough for vines, yet never detached from the water around it. [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] provides the backbone, with [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] offering complementary possibilities. The exact [[concept.assemblage|blend]] changes by wine and [[concept.vintage|vintage]].
:::

:::detail{#drie-delen-een-domein parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-lafite-estate source.inao-pauillac-specification-2026"}
### Three parts form one vineyard

The estate currently reports 112 hectares of vineyard divided into three zones: the slopes around the château, the Carruades plateau to its west and a historic 4.5-hectare parcel in the neighbouring commune of [[appellation.saint-estephe|Saint-Estèphe]]. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate]

That last parcel is not a geographical mistake. The official Pauillac specification includes several historically designated parcels beyond the commune boundary, including land in Saint-Estèphe. Only precisely delimited and approved parcels qualify for this exception; a vineyard just across the border does not automatically become Pauillac. [@source.inao-pauillac-specification-2026]

The three zones also explain why Lafite's [[concept.terroir|terroir]] cannot mean one soil mixture or one tasting aroma. Their common name becomes meaningful only through parcel knowledge, separate vinification and selection in the cellar.
:::

:::detail{#oude-stokken-en-toekomst parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-lafite-estate"}
### Preserving old vines, seeking new answers

The estate reports that a few ungrafted vines are about 140 years old and survived phylloxera. Such plants are rare witnesses, not a blueprint for the whole vineyard. Most modern European vines instead grow on resistant rootstocks, and age alone does not guarantee a superior lot. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate]

For the future, Lafite runs the PHARE project: a trial and conservation parcel for [[concept.massal-selection|massal selection]]—propagating material from several strong vines rather than one uniform clone—and varieties that may cope better with heat and drought. The producer also describes a transition to organic farming and biodiversity corridors beside 126 hectares of Natura 2000 marshland. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate] These are current management choices, not a new definition of Lafite's flavour; their value will emerge across many growing seasons.
:::

## Grand Vin — intensity without raising its voice

Château Lafite Rothschild is the Grand Vin: the principal wine into which the estate gathers its strictest selection. Cabernet Sauvignon normally leads the blend. Yet within Pauillac, Lafite is not chiefly celebrated for the darkest colour or most immediate power. Its classical profile rests on precision, fragrance, length and a structure that can initially appear almost reserved. [@source.decanter-bordeaux-first-growths]

Blackcurrant and cedar belong to the Cabernet family; with development, violet, truffle-like and other earthy notes are also regularly found. A Decanter vertical, for example, found floral suppleness in 2011 and cedar, truffle and violets in 2001. [@source.decanter-lafite-vertical] These examples teach how the wine can speak, not what every bottle must smell like. Vintage, age, storage and bottle variation remain audible.
:::

:::detail{#huisstijl-en-jaargang parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-lafite-vertical source.decanter-bordeaux-first-growths"}
### Sometimes Lafite speaks; sometimes the year does

Across eight vintages from 1988 to 2012, Decanter observed that the estate signature sometimes prevailed and the growing year did at other times. [@source.decanter-lafite-vertical] This is precisely why a style description should offer direction without writing the wine shut. Finesse may appear slender and floral in a cooler year, fuller and darker in a riper one, while both remain credible Lafite.

The finest vintages can develop for decades, but reputation does not make an old bottle invulnerable. Temperature, cork, bottle size and documented storage history all help decide what remains after twenty, fifty or more years. The practical question is therefore not only “how old is this Lafite?” but also “how has this particular bottle lived?”
:::

:::detail{#selectie-gisting-en-hout parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-lafite-estate"}
### The technical contours of the Grand Vin

Lafite uses only vines older than ten years for the Grand Vin. Every barrel is tasted separately and only selected lots enter the final blend. Under the estate's current description, alcoholic [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]] lasts about fifteen days at 26–27°C; the assembled wine then ages for fifteen months in new oak barrels. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate]

That barrel period is [[concept.elevage|élevage]]: continued development between fermentation and bottling. New oak can add structure, spice and cedar-like notes, but it does not explain the wine by itself. Grape quality, extraction, acidity, [[concept.tannin|tannin]], oxygen and time work together; the same cellar formula can find a very different balance in two vintages.
:::

## Other wines — Carruades has a point of departure of its own

Carruades de Lafite is the estate's [[concept.second-wine|second wine]]. Its name comes from the Carruades plateau, a group of parcels adjoining the Lafite ridge that was acquired at the end of the nineteenth century. A separate wine was initially made there; later, strong lots could also contribute to the Grand Vin and the name Carruades came to identify the second blend. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate]

Carruades generally contains relatively more Merlot than the Grand Vin, beside Cabernet Sauvignon and sometimes Cabernet Franc. That can make the young wine rounder and more direct, but “second” does not mean a simple diluted version. Specific parcels and a selection of its own give Carruades a recurring accent, while the exact proportions are decided anew each year. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate]

Other Bordeaux estates owned by the family, such as [[producer.chateau-duhart-milon|Château Duhart-Milon]], are not third wines of Lafite. They have their own vineyards, classifications, selections and producer entities.
:::

:::detail{#carruades-in-de-kelder parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-lafite-estate"}
### Carruades in the cellar

Alcoholic fermentation for Carruades takes place in wooden, concrete and stainless-steel vats. Light pumping over supports the [[concept.extraction|extraction]] of colour and tannin; total [[concept.maceration|maceration]] lasts about twenty days, according to the estate. After [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|malolactic fermentation]]—which converts sharper malic acid into softer lactic acid—the wine spends approximately sixteen months in French oak barrels from the group's own Pauillac cooperage. [@source.chateau-lafite-estate]

These figures are a current producer description, not a legal recipe. Vat choice, pumping over and ageing time are adjusted by lot and vintage. The useful distinction from the Grand Vin therefore lies not in one temperature or barrel, but in origin, tasting selection and the final balance of the blend.
:::

:::section{#werkwijze-en-uitgifte depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-lafite-blending-notes source.chateau-lafite-primeurs source.decanter-bordeaux-first-growths"}
## Working methods and release — choosing before the market speaks

After harvest, parcels and vats initially remain legible as separate lots. In winter, the team tastes them blind, compares possible combinations and separates clear Grand Vin candidates from Carruades lots and from the uncertain middle group that returns to the table. [[concept.assemblage|Blending]] is not a correction after the fact here, but the moment when the château decides which voice each wine will carry. [@source.chateau-lafite-blending-notes]

Lafite still sells its young vintage through Bordeaux's [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]] system: merchants and professional buyers taste in spring while the wine is still ageing in barrel, with delivery following later. [@source.chateau-lafite-primeurs] Sales pass through [[concept.negociant|négociants]] on the [[concept.place-de-bordeaux|Place de Bordeaux]]. Production and international distribution therefore remain separate, even when the final buyer connects the name directly with the château.
:::

:::detail{#proeven-als-gedeelde-taal parent="werkwijze-en-uitgifte" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-lafite-blending-notes"}
### Tasting as a shared language

For the 2024 blend, the producer described a simple sorting system: secure Grand Vin candidates, clear second-wine lots and a middle group for further debate. Colour, balance and mouthfeel matter more than ticking off isolated aromas. Technical director Éric Kohler frames the question not as whether a lot smells of strawberry or liquorice, but whether it feels like Lafite. [@source.chateau-lafite-blending-notes]

This makes selection both disciplined and human. A favoured parcel has no automatic right to the Grand Vin; one convincing lot may alter the proportions of the entire wine. At a château with such a powerful reputation, that distinction is essential: the historic name is already fixed, but the contents of the bottle are not.
:::
