**Château Cheval Blanc** stands at the far western edge of [[appellation.saint-emilion-grand-cru|Saint-Émilion Grand Cru]], almost against [[appellation.pomerol|Pomerol]]. The estate was assembled from land belonging to [[producer.chateau-figeac|Château Figeac]] in the nineteenth century. Bernard Arnault and Albert Frère bought it in 1998; joint ownership now rests with LVMH and the Frère family.

The [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]] is Château Cheval Blanc. [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] plays an exceptionally large role beside [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], a combination capable of uniting fragrance, freshness and fine yet enduring structure. Cheval Blanc was a Premier Grand Cru Classé holding distinction A from 1955 through the 2012 edition, but did not enter the [[classification.saint-emilion|2022 classification]]. That historic rank matters, but it is not current.
:::

## History and ownership — a young château on old vineyard land

Vines had grown around the place called *Au Cheval-Blanc* for centuries, but the modern estate only began to take shape in 1832. Jean-Jacques Ducasse bought its core, and purchases of Figeac land over the next twenty years formed almost the same outline the property retains today. The wine was sold under the Cheval Blanc name from 1852. [@source.chateau-cheval-blanc-history]

The estate acquired its defining shape through Ducasse's daughter Henriette and her husband Jean Laussac-Fourcaud. Their descendants kept Cheval Blanc together for more than a century. Bernard Arnault and Albert Frère bought the château in 1998. LVMH acquired Arnault's half in 2009; joint ownership today rests with LVMH and the Frère family. [@source.lvmh-cheval-blanc-acquisition-2009] [@source.comite-colbert-chateau-cheval-blanc]
:::

:::detail{#de-negentiende-eeuwse-sprong parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-history source.decanter-chateau-cheval-blanc-profile"}
### Drainage, Cabernet Franc and a name of its own

Jean Laussac-Fourcaud had sections of wet ground drained and chose roughly equal proportions of Merlot and Cabernet Franc during the 1860s — unusual in Saint-Émilion. Planting was completed in 1871. Meanwhile the wine won medals at international exhibitions in London, Paris and Antwerp; two of them still appear on the label. [@source.chateau-cheval-blanc-history]

That combination turned a detached parcel into an independent identity. Family line, boundaries and varietal choice remained remarkably stable, but “tradition” did not mean standing still: drainage, replanting and brand-building were themselves radical changes.
:::

:::detail{#continuiteit-en-macht parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-history source.comite-colbert-chateau-cheval-blanc"}
### Continuity changed form

The 1998 sale ended the long Fourcaud-Laussac era, not the vineyard's coherence. The new owners retained the existing team and later invested in a new winery. Historic land therefore remained intact while the institutional setting shifted from family château to joint ownership involving a global luxury group.

That distinction matters when reputation is interpreted. A famous name can be an agricultural business, cultural heritage and scarce commodity at once. Ownership and investment power help explain present possibilities, but do not prove that every vintage or bottle is exceptional.
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — Saint-Émilion at the edge of Pomerol

Cheval Blanc does not occupy the celebrated limestone plateau around the village of Saint-Émilion. It lies on flatter Quaternary river deposits at the appellation's western edge. Gravel, clay and sand form an intricate pattern across the 42-hectare estate; the official vineyard account currently divides it into 56 plots. [@source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vineyard]

According to that current account, plantings are 55% Cabernet Franc, 40% Merlot and 5% [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]]. Cabernet Franc's unusually large share is not a decorative fact: it is one key to the fragrance, freshness and structure that distinguish Cheval Blanc within [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]].
:::

:::detail{#bodem-en-druif parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vineyard"}
### Combinations, not one soil

Gravel drains readily and warms comparatively quickly; clay retains more water and responds more slowly. Sand adds another water and temperature dynamic. Because these materials interlace, the same variety can ripen differently in two plots — and Cabernet Franc and Merlot can each play more than one role.

The château consequently follows plot, variety, planting year and [[concept.rootstock|rootstock]] separately. The [[concept.assemblage|blend]] does not repeat vineyard percentages: it selects from what a particular [[concept.vintage|vintage]] has produced across all those combinations.
:::

:::detail{#grenzen-van-terroirtaal parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vineyard source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vinification"}
### A mosaic [[concept.terroir|terroir]] is not yet a flavour recipe

The producer associates softer tannin with clay and greater aromatic elegance with gravel. That is a useful working model, not a universal law. Root depth, age, water supply, yield, disease, harvest timing and cellar decisions all run through the influence of soil.

“Gravel plus Cabernet Franc” is therefore an inadequate explanation. The mosaic chiefly provides room to decide: plots do not all respond to drought or rain at once, so selection and blending can preserve differences and bring them together.
:::

## Grand Vin — power without heaviness as an ideal

Château Cheval Blanc is the estate's principal wine. Cabernet Franc can contribute floral, herbal and graphite-like tension; Merlot often brings breadth and a velvet texture. In successful years, Cheval Blanc is therefore associated not only with concentration, but with fragrance, length, freshness and silky [[concept.tannin|tannin]].

That profile is a direction, not a tasting note guaranteed for every bottle. Varietal proportions change by year, warm and cool seasons shift emphasis, and bottle age may move primary fruit towards cedar, tobacco, earth and dried flowers. Storage also determines whether an old bottle appears refined or tired.
:::

:::detail{#selectie-maakt-de-wijn parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vinification"}
### Selection makes the Grand Vin

After separate [[concept.fermentation|fermentation]], lots are tasted for months before the blend is fixed. The producer says that roughly fifteen to forty-five plots may contribute to a Grand Vin in a given year; a selected plot enters as a whole. [@source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vinification]

The wine is therefore not simply “everything from the best soil”. It results from an editorial act in wine form: lots are selected to express place and year together. A smaller share of Cabernet Franc in one bottle does not mean the identity has suddenly vanished.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-petit-cheval source.chateau-cheval-blanc-petit-cheval-blanc source.chateau-cheval-blanc-history"}
## Other wines — two Petits, two different stories

Le Petit Cheval has been the red [[concept.second-wine|second wine]] since 1988. No fixed plot is assigned to it: lots from across the vineyard may be selected for Grand Vin, Petit Cheval or bulk wine. Petit Cheval generally opens earlier and emphasizes suppleness, but its blend may differ markedly from the Grand Vin. [@source.chateau-cheval-blanc-petit-cheval]

Petit Cheval blanc is different. After Château La Tour du Pin was acquired in 2006, suitable sections were planted or identified with [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]] and [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]]. The first commercial vintage was 2014. It is a separate dry white wine from [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] and adjoining land, not a white version of the red second selection. [@source.chateau-cheval-blanc-petit-cheval-blanc]
:::

:::detail{#namen-kunnen-misleiden parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-petit-cheval source.chateau-cheval-blanc-petit-cheval-blanc"}
### “Petit” does not by itself reveal origin

For red Petit Cheval, the name describes a selection tier within the red Cheval Blanc vineyard. For Petit Cheval blanc, it connects a later white project to the house. The shared brand name thus conceals a difference in land, grapes and production history.

Château Quinault L'Enclos and Cheval des Andes are also managed by the wider organization, but they are independent properties or collaborations. They are not part of Château Cheval Blanc's harvest and are therefore not treated as subsidiary selections from this vineyard.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-opvoeding depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-winery source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vinification"}
## Winemaking and maturation — each lot first finds its own voice

Architect Christian de Portzamparc's white, undulating winery opened in 2011. Concrete vats in different sizes allow every plot to ferment separately. After destemming, grapes move by gravity into the appropriate vat; tasting then determines the course of [[concept.maceration|maceration]] and [[concept.extraction|extraction]]. [@source.chateau-cheval-blanc-winery]

[[concept.malolactic-fermentation|Malolactic fermentation]] takes place in vat. Blending follows, then generally sixteen to eighteen months of [[concept.elevage|maturation]] in barrel. These outlines describe the château's current practice, not a rule of Saint-Émilion Grand Cru.
:::

:::detail{#precisie-en-haar-grens parent="wijnmaken-en-opvoeding" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-cheval-blanc-winery source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vinification"}
### Precision and its limit

The winery translates the plot structure into 67 vats of fourteen sizes. Daily tasting, temperature control and separate lots provide the team with abundant information and many decision points. Press wine is held apart when the château judges it insufficiently pure aromatically. [@source.chateau-cheval-blanc-vinification]

Precision is a means here: it prevents small differences from disappearing too early. Yet one vat per plot is not proof of superior wine. Quality still depends on the fruit and on human judgements about ripeness, extraction, selection, oak and time.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-reputatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021 source.chateau-cheval-blanc-history"}
## Classification and reputation — historically A, currently unclassified

From the first [[classification.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion classification]] in 1955, Cheval Blanc belonged to the Premiers Grands Crus Classés holding distinction A. That position was confirmed in successive editions, most recently in 2012. In 2021, however, the château decided not to submit an application for the 2022 edition because it considered the assessment criteria misaligned with its priorities. [@source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021]

Cheval Blanc is consequently a Saint-Émilion Grand Cru today but not a currently classified producer. Its historic rank and enduring reputation have not vanished; they simply must not be presented as a current formal title.
:::

:::detail{#rang-deelname-en-reputatie parent="classificatie-en-reputatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021"}
### Absence is not demotion here

A revisable classification assesses only candidates that participate. Cheval Blanc was therefore not lowered in rank in 2022 and did not lose a tasting contest; it stood outside the procedure. That differs fundamentally from a château that entered and received a different rank.

The market and wine world may continue to value an absent producer very highly, but reputation cannot replace legal or institutional status. Both time layers belong side by side.
:::

:::detail{#de-keuze-als-institutioneel-signaal parent="classificatie-en-reputatie" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021 source.chateau-cheval-blanc-history"}
### An icon can afford to refuse participation

Withdrawal also reveals power. A lesser-known producer may need classification for visibility and trust; Cheval Blanc already possessed global distribution and a reputation extending beyond any one edition. It could therefore reject the criteria publicly without disappearing from cultural memory.

That does not make the decision automatically right or wrong. It does show that classifications are at once quality framework, collective institution and economic instrument — and that their authority partly depends on who is willing to participate.
:::

## Label, serving and storage — a bottle carries its time layer

The two medallions on the label recall nineteenth-century exhibition successes. Older bottles may also correctly state *Premier Grand Cru Classé A*; wine released under the present situation should not simply inherit that historic title. Always read name, [[concept.vintage|vintage]], appellation and rank together.

Young Cheval Blanc may benefit from air and a generous glass. With an old bottle, provenance, fill level and cool stable storage matter more than a universal decanting time. Sediment is normal; gentle pouring protects the fragile aromas that make long maturation interesting.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Cheval Blanc is not a limestone Saint-Émilion in another guise. It is a border wine: born from Figeac land, rooted in clay and gravel beside Pomerol, and built around Cabernet Franc and Merlot.
:::

:::caveat{#historisch-is-niet-actueel depth="advanced" variant="simplification" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.decanter-cheval-blanc-ausone-withdrawal-2021"}
Do not call Cheval Blanc “Premier Grand Cru Classé A” without a time qualifier. The title describes its historic position through the 2012 edition; the château did not participate in the 2022 classification.
:::
