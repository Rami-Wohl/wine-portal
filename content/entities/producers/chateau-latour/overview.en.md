Château Latour stands where the gravel rises of [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]] slope towards the broad Gironde. The round stone tower on the label is instantly recognisable, yet the estate's essence lies in the vineyard around it: the Enclos, a walled core from which the Grand Vin is selected.

Latour is a *premier cru*, the highest tier in the red-wine [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 Bordeaux classification]]. Its documented history begins with a fortified tower in 1331; since 1993 the estate has been linked to François Pinault through the investment company Artémis. [@source.chateau-latour-history] The Grand Vin—the château's principal wine—is associated with Cabernet-led power, dark cassis-like fruit, fresh tension and a tannic structure that may demand time. This is a historically formed style profile, not a promise for every vintage or bottle. [@source.decanter-latour-tasting]
:::

## History and ownership — from fortress to wine estate

The name Latour reaches back to a military tower, not to the round building now seen in photographs and on labels. In 1331 Gaucelme de Castillon received permission to build a fortification in the parish of Saint-Maubert. A tower on the site appears again in Froissart's chronicles in 1378, guarding the Gironde during the Hundred Years' War. [@source.chateau-latour-history]

Centuries later the defensive site had become a wine estate. Through marriage and inheritance Latour passed to the Ségur family, who brought together several great holdings in the [[region.medoc|Médoc]] in the early eighteenth century. By 1855 its reputation was secure enough to place Latour in the highest tier of the new commercial ranking. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]

After several twentieth-century ownership phases, François Pinault acquired the estate through Artémis in June 1993. [@source.chateau-latour-history] That present ownership context matters, but does not explain Latour by itself: the name rests on a long relationship between place, selection, capital and generations of work.
:::

:::detail{#handel-maakte-reputatie parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-history source.decanter-chateau-latour-profile"}
### Trade turned a place into a name

In the early eighteenth century, demand grew in England and other northern European markets for individually recognisable Médoc wines. Latour was no longer traded merely as a barrel of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]], but as an origin with its own name and price. The estate's historical accounts show how sharply its price premium over ordinary Bordeaux increased within a few decades. [@source.chateau-latour-history]

Commercial success funded maintenance, replanting and closer selection, while the relationship also ran the other way: a more stable estate identity made a recognisable market name possible. The 1855 classification did not freeze a sudden jury decision; it formalised an order that brokers and buyers already expressed through reputation and trading prices. [@source.decanter-chateau-latour-profile]
:::

:::detail{#eigendom-en-continuiteit parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-history"}
### Ownership changed; the name remained tied to the place

Descendants of the Ségur family remained connected to Latour until 1962. Shares then passed first to the British Pearson group and subsequently to Allied Lyons, before Artémis took control in 1993. [@source.chateau-latour-history] The sequence shows why “family estate” may be too simple for historical châteaux: continuity sometimes resides more strongly in vineyards, cellar records and the estate name than in one uninterrupted bloodline.

After 1993 the cellars and buildings were renewed in several phases. The estate records work from 1999 to 2003 and another construction phase following its 2012 decision to hold wine longer in bottle. [@source.chateau-latour-history] Modern infrastructure does not stand opposite tradition here; it helps determine how an old philosophy of selection and release can continue in practice.
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — the Enclos beside the estuary

Latour's [[concept.terroir|terroir]] is concentrated around the *Enclos*: the walled vineyard around the château. Its low gravel rise stands above the Gironde and is bounded by streams on two sides. Gravel readily drains excess water and warms relatively quickly, while the estuary moderates the immediate surroundings. Together they create favourable conditions for late-ripening [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]].

This does not turn the Enclos into a flavour machine. Soil, relief and water determine possibilities; vine age, season, picking date and selection decide what reaches the bottle. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] complement Cabernet. The Grand Vin comes exclusively from the Enclos, but not every lot from that core automatically makes the principal wine. [@source.chateau-latour-grand-vin]
:::

:::detail{#wijngaard-in-cijfers parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-land"}
### A large holding with a compact core

Latour currently reports 96.5 hectares of vineyard, 47 of them within the Enclos. Its highest gravel rise reaches roughly sixteen metres above the Gironde, according to the estate. The reported planting across the property is approximately 76% Cabernet Sauvignon, 22% Merlot and 2% Petit Verdot. [@source.chateau-latour-land]

These figures describe the estate's toolkit, not the contents of one bottle. The final [[concept.assemblage|blend]]—combining separate lots—changes by wine and [[concept.vintage|vintage]]. Parcels outside the Enclos also lie in other parts of Pauillac; “Latour” is therefore not one homogeneous block of soil.
:::

:::detail{#enclos-als-selectie parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-land source.chateau-latour-grand-vin source.chateau-latour-les-forts"}
### The Enclos is a source area, not automatic promotion

All grapes for the Grand Vin come from old vines in the Enclos. The reverse does not follow: lots from this core may fall outside the Grand Vin after tasting and enter Les Forts de Latour. Several parcels at the edge of the Enclos also supply Les Forts as a matter of course. [@source.chateau-latour-les-forts]

This selection gives the estate name several layers. Appellation and classification say where Latour belongs and which historical rank it holds; internal selection decides which wine a barrel ultimately becomes. Without that distinction, Grand Vin, second wine and estate Pauillac can look misleadingly like three quality stickers on the same mixture.
:::

## Grand Vin — power held in tension

Grand Vin de Château Latour is the principal wine and the clearest expression of the Enclos selection. Cabernet Sauvignon almost always leads the blend. When young, the wine may seem closed and stern: dark berries, cedar-like aromas, a cool line of freshness and ample [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. With time, earth, tobacco and more savoury notes can emerge. [@source.decanter-latour-tasting]

The heart of that character is not sheer mass. Successful bottles combine concentration with length and freshness; tannin provides direction rather than only hardness. Vintage, harvest ripeness, selection and bottle storage may nevertheless shift the profile substantially. “Powerful and long-lived” is therefore a useful expectation, never a substitute for the actual wine.
:::

:::detail{#oude-stokken-en-assemblage parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-grand-vin source.chateau-latour-winemaking"}
### Old vines, strict selection and new oak

The estate describes the Grand Vin vines as averaging around sixty years of age. Cabernet Sauvignon generally forms more than 90% of the blend; the exact proportion emerges only after the separate lots have been tasted. [@source.chateau-latour-grand-vin]

The Grand Vin matures entirely in new French oak barrels. This is [[concept.elevage|élevage]]: the period of development between fermentation and bottling, during which wood, oxygen and time help shape texture and aroma. New oak can emphasise cedar, spice and structure, but the aim is not to impose the same oak aroma on every vintage. [@source.chateau-latour-winemaking]
:::

:::detail{#stijl-met-grenzen parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-grand-vin source.decanter-latour-tasting"}
### A reputation across many years, not a fixed tasting note

A wide-ranging Decanter tasting across many vintages shows a recognisable family of cassis, cedar, tension and firm build, but equally clearly reveals differences between years and bottle conditions. [@source.decanter-latour-tasting] An older wine may be refined and still vivid, or marked by storage and bottle variation.

Latour itself says that the Grand Vin often needs at least a decade before it begins to show fully and can develop far longer in strong vintages. [@source.chateau-latour-grand-vin] This is the producer's assessment, not a universal drinking window. Bottle provenance, storage, format and personal taste remain decisive.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-les-forts source.chateau-latour-pauillac"}
## Other wines — Les Forts and Le Pauillac

Les Forts de Latour is the estate's [[concept.second-wine|second wine]], first labelled under that name in 1966. It comes from parcels outside and at the edge of the Enclos, supplemented by lots from the core that were not selected for the Grand Vin. [@source.chateau-latour-les-forts] Les Forts is therefore not a diluted version of one fixed recipe, but a selection in its own right whose sources can shift from year to year.

Le Pauillac de Château Latour forms a third wine. Made annually since 1989, it comes chiefly from younger vines and lots not selected for Les Forts; the estate initially aimed it mainly at restaurants. [@source.chateau-latour-pauillac] All three carry Pauillac, but their place in the estate's selection, blend and maturation differs.
:::

:::detail{#drie-wijnen-drie-verhoudingen parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-grand-vin source.chateau-latour-les-forts source.chateau-latour-pauillac"}
### Three wines, three emphases

In the estate's current descriptions, Les Forts often contains about 25–30% Merlot and matures in 50–60% new barrels. Le Pauillac generally sits around 45% Merlot and 20% new oak, whereas the Grand Vin may contain more than 90% Cabernet Sauvignon and receives entirely new oak. [@source.chateau-latour-grand-vin] [@source.chateau-latour-les-forts] [@source.chateau-latour-pauillac]

These are indicative producer figures, not legal recipes. They help explain why the younger wines often become approachable sooner, but cannot rank every actual bottle automatically. A strong vintage of Les Forts may be more compelling than a poorly stored Grand Vin; name and selection never replace condition.
:::

:::section{#werkwijze-en-uitgifte depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-winemaking source.decanter-chateau-latour-profile"}
## Working methods and release — selection continues after harvest

After fermentation and initial development, the individual lots are tasted repeatedly. According to Latour, the formal blending process begins around mid-January: the Grand Vin is determined first, followed by Les Forts and finally Le Pauillac. [@source.chateau-latour-winemaking] This is a cascade of positive choices, not simply one tank from which the best is skimmed off in stages.

Since 2012 Latour has no longer offered new vintages routinely [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]]—the Bordeaux practice of selling wine while it is still maturing in barrel for later delivery. The château holds the bottles itself and releases parcels later through merchants. [@source.decanter-chateau-latour-profile] This shifts part of the early maturation and storage risk from the early buyer back to the estate.
:::

:::detail{#kelder-tijd-en-authenticiteit parent="werkwijze-en-uitgifte" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-latour-winemaking"}
### When the château releases the bottle

The estate currently gives broad pre-release holding periods of four to six years for Le Pauillac, six to eight for Les Forts and eight to ten for the Grand Vin. The actual release date is decided by wine and vintage. [@source.chateau-latour-winemaking]

Latour has also used an individual bottle-authentication system since 2007. [@source.chateau-latour-winemaking] That suits a wine often traded again years later: a bottle's life story becomes almost as relevant as its reputation at harvest. The practical lesson for the drinker remains simple: check the exact wine, vintage, provenance and condition before allowing the historical name to do all the thinking.
:::
