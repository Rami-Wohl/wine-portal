**Château Pavie** lies on the south-facing Côte Pavie, immediately southeast of the village of [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]]. Its 42 hectares of vines form one contiguous sweep across limestone plateau, steep clay-limestone slope and the lighter foot of the hill. The Perse family has owned the estate since 1998; Angélique and Henrique Da Costa continue its work following Gérard Perse's death in 2025.

The [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]], Château Pavie, is known for concentration, ripe dark fruit, firm [[concept.tannin|tannin]] and considerable ageing potential. Power is part of its identity, but does not take the same form every year: since the emphatic wines made around the turn of the century, the style has gradually become more precise, fresher and approachable earlier. Pavie has held distinction A, the highest tier of the [[classification.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion classification]], since 2012; the 2022 edition confirmed that position.

## History and ownership — from separate estates to one Pavie

The hillside carried the name *Pavie* before the present château existed; local tradition relates it to the red-fleshed peaches once grown here. The creation of the wine estate is better documented. From 1873, merchant Ferdinand Bouffard assembled several small properties into almost fifty hectares, although their wines were still sold separately. After 1919, Albert Porte sold Pavie-Pigasse — later Pavie-Decesse — and united the remaining parts into the Château Pavie recognizable on labels. [@source.chateau-pavie-history]

The Porte family was followed by Alexandre Valette in 1943; his grandson Jean-Paul Valette ran the estate from 1957. Gérard and Chantal Perse bought Pavie in 1998. Gérard Perse died in July 2025. Their daughter Angélique and her husband Henrique Da Costa, both active in the family business since 2005, now carry the château forward. [@source.lefigaro-chateau-pavie-succession-2026]
:::

:::detail{#de-perse-omslag parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-pavie-history source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### 1998 was more than a change of owner

At the time of purchase, parts of the vineyard were tired and the winery was outdated. Perse replaced missing vines, reduced yields and almost immediately built a new vat room with wooden vats and temperature control. A second major winery renovation was completed in 2013. [@source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025]

Both the means and the ambition changed. The powerful wine style, and the fierce reactions it provoked, became part of Pavie's modern reputation. Promotion to distinction A in 2012 therefore followed a long history of quality, but also fourteen years of highly visible reinvestment.
:::

:::detail{#een-groeiend-domein parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-pavie-domain source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### Old names disappeared into a larger whole

The present 42 hectares are not precisely the same group of parcels as in 1998. La Clusière and part of Pavie-Decesse were incorporated into Pavie in 2002; the remaining vineyards of Pavie-Decesse and Bellevue-Mondotte followed in 2022. [@source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025]

Such incorporation does not erase the history of those names, but it changes which wine their grapes may enter. An old bottle of Pavie-Decesse is a distinct historic product; grapes from the same former holding may become part of Château Pavie's blend in a later [[concept.vintage|vintage]].
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — an amphitheatre above the Dordogne

The estate's 42 hectares of vines form a contiguous, south-facing sweep above the Dordogne valley. Limestone plateau lies at the top, a clay-limestone slope descends below it, and the soils become sandier with patches of gravel at the foot. Altitude, gradient and exposure change over short distances; Pavie is therefore one estate, but not a homogeneous piece of ground. [@source.chateau-pavie-domain]

The southern slope receives abundant sunlight, while limestone and clay hold and drain water differently from the lighter soils below. This combination helps make a ripe, powerful style possible without making freshness irrelevant. It explains possibilities, not the automatic taste of every bottle.
:::

:::detail{#drie-druiven-drie-posities parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-pavie-domain source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### The plantings follow the relief

The present vineyard is planted to 50% [[grape.merlot|Merlot]], 30% [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] and 20% [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]]. Much of the Merlot occupies the cooler clay-limestone soils of the plateau, Cabernet Franc the slope with Fronsadais molasse, and Cabernet Sauvignon the warmer, lighter foot of the hill. [@source.chateau-pavie-domain]

These percentages describe the vineyard, not the recipe for every bottle. Ripeness and selection vary by parcel and year, so the final [[concept.assemblage|blend]] can differ markedly from the planted proportions.
:::

:::detail{#terroir-zonder-smaakautomaat parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-pavie-domain source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### Limestone is not a flavouring

Hard limestone, clay, molasse, sand and gravel influence drainage, water availability, rooting and warming, among other things. They do not enter the grape as literal “minerality”. Plant material, vine age and health, canopy, yield, picking date and the course of the weather also shape what reaches the cellar.

The same slope can consequently produce both powerful and taut wine. [[concept.terroir|Terroir]] here means the set of conditions and human responses to them, not one soil name that predicts the entire style.
:::

## Grand Vin — power with a long time horizon

Château Pavie is the Grand Vin. Its recognizable register includes ripe black fruit, plum, spice and sometimes floral or stony impressions, with considerable colour, volume and tannin. Tobacco, leather, earth and dried fruit may emerge in bottle. The finest years are built for long development; when young, the wine can be compact and emphatically structured.

This profile is not a tasting note for every vintage. Cooler years may be tauter and more fragrant, warmer ones broader and riper, while storage determines what remains in an old bottle. Pavie's core is not maximum weight, but the tension between a sunlit slope, great concentration and enough freshness to carry that power.
:::

:::detail{#stijl-en-debat parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### The controversy belongs to the wine's history

The 2000, 2003 and 2005 wines became symbols of a modern, deeply coloured and very full-bodied Saint-Émilion style. Some critics admired their intensity and longevity; others found extraction, ripeness and oak too dominant. This difference of opinion is not a footnote: it shows that prestige and technical ambition do not create a universal standard of taste. [@source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025]

Practice has since shifted. Shorter [[concept.maceration|macerations]], cooler fermentation, slightly earlier picking and less new oak have brought more freshness and finer texture according to the recent independent account. Pavie has not become light; its power is arranged differently.
:::

:::detail{#ontwikkeling-op-fles parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### Ageing softens, but does not rewrite everything

Tannin can feel softer after years in bottle, while primary fruit gives way to earthy, spicy and dried notes. A concentrated young wine may therefore become more harmonious. Age does not guarantee that every stylistic feature disappears, however, or that every taster experiences the same balance.

Provenance and storage are crucial with older Pavie. Fill level, temperature history and the condition of cork and capsule often say more about a particular bottle than a general statement that a vintage “can last fifty years”.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025 source.chateau-pavie-domain"}
## Other wines — Arômes has its own selection

Arômes de Pavie is the château's [[concept.second-wine|second wine]]. Since 2017, according to a recent producer profile, it has been drawn largely from dedicated parcels of old vines. This gives Arômes a distinct origin within the property; it is not merely the leftover vat after the Grand Vin has been selected. [@source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025]

Esprit de Pavie is different. It may use fruit from broader [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] holdings and declassified lots, and is therefore not a second wine from the same 42 hectares. A shared family name does not make the origins interchangeable.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-opvoeding depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-pavie-expertise source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
## Winemaking and maturation — intensity without automatically forcing it

Grapes are hand-picked, sorted and handled by parcel in relatively small wooden vats. Present practice uses moderate temperatures and gentle pump-overs to draw out colour, aroma and [[concept.tannin|tannin]] without imposing the same extraction level on every lot. Lots remain separate until tasting guides the blend. [@source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025]

This makes “traditional” versus “modern” too crude a contrast. Wooden vats and barrel maturation are familiar Bordeaux tools; temperature control, finer parcel selection and adjusted [[concept.extraction|extraction]] determine how they are used in a particular harvest.
:::

:::detail{#technische-contouren parent="wijnmaken-en-opvoeding" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-pavie-expertise source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### The current method is a snapshot

The producer describes about a week of cold pre-fermentation maceration, eight to ten days of [[concept.fermentation|alcoholic fermentation]] and then three weeks or more of skin contact. [[concept.malolactic-fermentation|Malolactic conversion]] takes place in barrel. The current estate page gives about eighteen months for [[concept.elevage|maturation]] with periodic racking; a recent independent account gives eighteen to twenty-four months and 60–70% new oak. [@source.chateau-pavie-expertise] [@source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025]

That range is more informative than false precision. Duration, barrel choice and the share of new oak can vary by wine, lot and harvest. These are reported current practices, not appellation rules or an eternal recipe.
:::

## Classification and reputation — from B to A

The first official Saint-Émilion classification in 1955 placed Pavie among the Premiers Grands Crus Classés with distinction B. The 2012 edition promoted the château to distinction A; Pavie retained that highest formal position in 2022, then alongside [[producer.chateau-figeac|Château Figeac]]. [@source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022]

This timeline prevents two misunderstandings. Pavie had already ranked highly for decades before 2012, and a 1990 bottle correctly does not carry the later A. The classification assesses a producer within a revisable edition; it does not rank every individual bottle.
:::

:::detail{#rang-en-reputatie parent="classificatie-en-reputatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
### A high rank does not end the conversation

Distinction A explains Pavie's institutional position, global visibility and market status. It does not decide whether a taster prefers its powerful style to a lighter Saint-Émilion, nor whether every vintage is equally successful.

Pavie makes the difference between classification and criticism particularly visible: the same wine could receive exceptional international praise and sharp rejection. Quality rank, stylistic preference and bottle performance are related, but not identical.
:::

:::section{#etiket-serveren-en-bewaren depth="foundation" source_refs="source.chateau-pavie-history source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
## Label, serving and storage — a bottle tells you when it was made

The 1990 bottle shown here names the Valette family and carries the classification wording Premier Grand Cru Classé. Later labels may show the Perse family, distinction A or a special commemorative design. Read such differences as time layers, not immediately as evidence of a counterfeit.

Young Pavie often benefits from air, a generous glass and food that can meet its structure. With an old bottle, standing it upright, pouring carefully and decanting cautiously are wiser than applying a fixed aeration time. Do not serve it too warm: extra heat makes alcohol and ripeness feel heavier and obscures the freshness that holds the wine in balance.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Pavie is more than “a powerful Saint-Émilion”. It is a sunlit limestone slope, a historically assembled estate and an emphatic style that has first been intensified and then refined since 1998 — without surrendering its ambition to age.
:::

:::caveat{#kracht-is-geen-formule depth="advanced" variant="simplification" source_refs="source.chateau-pavie-domain source.decanter-chateau-pavie-profile-2025"}
Southern exposure, low yields, long maceration and new oak do not simply add up to quality. Fruit health, ripeness, extraction, blending, maturation and time must form a balance together; more of one element can instead disrupt it.
:::
