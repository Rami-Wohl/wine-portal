Château Léoville Las Cases lies on the northern edge of [[appellation.saint-julien|Saint-Julien]], where the walled Grand Enclos is separated from [[appellation.pauillac|Pauillac]] and [[producer.chateau-latour|Château Latour]]'s vineyards only by the Juillac stream. It is the largest surviving portion of the former Domaine de Léoville and was ranked a second growth in the [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]]. [@source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-profile]

The Delon family has been associated with the estate since 1900. With the 2025 harvest, Arnaud Delon became the fifth generation to assume day-to-day leadership. The [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]] is generally Cabernet-led, deeply structured and built to develop. Its reputation as “almost Pauillac” is understandable from location and style, but legally and historically this is emphatically Saint-Julien. [@source.decanter-saint-julien-2025] [@source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-wines]

## History and ownership — the largest inheritance from Léoville

The old Mont-Moytié passed by marriage to the de Gascq family late in the seventeenth century, and they began using the Léoville name. Following the French Revolution and later inheritances, the extensive property was divided. One portion became Léoville Barton; another nineteenth-century division created Léoville Poyferré. The largest remaining part stayed connected to the Las Cases family. [@source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-profile]

Wine merchant Théophile Skawinski joined the ownership group around 1900. Through his son-in-law André Delon, the Delon family gradually became the defining owner and manager. Following Jean-Hubert Delon, Arnaud Delon has represented the fifth generation in leadership since the 2025 harvest. [@source.decanter-saint-julien-2025]
:::

:::detail{#revolutie-en-verdeling parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.leoville-las-cases-history source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-profile"}
### One historic vineyard became three famous names

The three-way division explains why Saint-Julien now has Léoville Las Cases, Léoville Barton and Léoville Poyferré. They share an origin, but their parcels, buildings, ownership histories and selections subsequently developed separately. A common name does not indicate one modern business or a single wine style.

Las Cases retained the walled northern core. That continuity gives the estate a powerful historical narrative, but plantings, cellar and selection changed across generations here too.
:::

:::detail{#familiebezit-en-generatieovergang parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-profile source.decanter-saint-julien-2025"}
### Family continuity consists of transfers, not stillness

At the beginning of the twentieth century, ownership was still divided among several shareholders. The Delons gradually increased their position and later joined Las Cases to other estates within Domaines Delon. This differs from a château governed without interruption by one owner since the Middle Ages.

The transfer to Arnaud Delon coincided with a substantial new cellar, first used for the 2025 harvest. New infrastructure and a new generation can alter working methods; a stylistic break can only be assessed carefully after several bottled and matured vintages. [@source.decanter-saint-julien-2025]
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — a clos on the boundary of two appellations

The Grand Enclos is a walled core vineyard on the road from Saint-Julien to Pauillac. Gravel of varying depth lies over layers containing sand and clay. Small elevation changes govern drainage and water availability; the nearby Gironde moderates some temperature extremes. This [[concept.terroir|terroir]] has gentle relief and is not hilly. [@source.leoville-las-cases-terroir]

The Juillac forms the administrative boundary to the north. Château Latour's proximity does not change the appellation: Las Cases is Saint-Julien because its parcels lie within that delimited origin. Neighbourhood can help explain landscape similarities, but does not transfer rank or identity.
:::

:::detail{#grand-enclos-als-werkeenheid parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.leoville-las-cases-terroir source.leoville-las-cases-wines"}
### The wall does not enclose uniform soil

A clos looks like one whole but contains different soil depths, exposures, vine ages and varieties. The team consequently reads it parcel by parcel. The wall's great value is not that it makes all vines equal, but that a recognisable historic core survived as a coherent working unit.

The Grand Vin comes largely from this core. “Largely” matters: wine selection does not automatically follow every stone boundary and may shift by vintage. [@source.leoville-las-cases-wines]
:::

:::detail{#grens-is-geen-smaaklijn parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.leoville-las-cases-terroir source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-wines"}
### The stream separates rules more sharply than flavours

The appellation boundary at the Juillac is legally exact, while geology and climate change gradually. A taster may therefore experience similarities with neighbouring Pauillac. It does not follow that Las Cases is “really Pauillac” or that one soil type directly produces a particular aroma.

Blackcurrant, cedar, graphite-like associations and firm tannin are tasting descriptions. They are also shaped by grape variety, ripeness, [[concept.extraction|extraction]], oak and bottle age. The boundary says where the wine comes from; the glass shows how place and decisions combined that year.
:::

## Grape varieties — Cabernet as architecture

[[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] is the principal variety and generally gives the Grand Vin its spine of [[concept.tannin|tannin]] and acidity. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] can contribute volume and earlier fruit; [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] brings aroma and tension in suitable lots, while [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] may play a small supporting role. [@source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-profile]

Planting proportions are not a bottle recipe. Which parcels reach the Grand Vin and how the [[concept.assemblage|blend]] is composed depend on the growing season and the quality of individual lots.
:::

:::detail{#cabernet-en-geduld parent="druivenrassen" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.leoville-las-cases-wines source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-wines"}
### Cabernet explains structure, not quality by itself

A high Cabernet proportion can provide abundant tannin, freshness and ageing potential when the grape is ripe and balanced. Unripe or harshly extracted Cabernet does not improve through its name. Harvest timing, skin ripeness and vat handling are therefore as important as the percentage in the blend.

Las Cases selects for development over time. This may make a young wine seem reserved, but “austere” should not excuse dryness, imbalance or a faulty bottle.
:::

## Grand Vin — precision that asks for time

The full name is Grand Vin de Léoville du Marquis de Las Cases. Across strong vintages it is often described as deep, Cabernet-led and powerfully structured, with dark fruit and aromas that can move towards cedar, tobacco, earth or savoury nuances through bottle age. These are recurring experiences, not a guaranteed checklist. [@source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-wines]

Its reputation rests heavily on ageing potential. That does not mean every [[concept.vintage|vintage]] needs decades or that older is always better. Harvest year, selection, bottle size, cork and storage together determine whether an individual bottle reaches its possible development.
:::

:::detail{#stijl-en-selectie parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.leoville-las-cases-wines source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-wines"}
### Reserve in youth can have several causes

Tannin and Cabernet aroma can make young Las Cases seem closed. Barrel maturation and reductive development may temporarily mute it too. Air and time can then reveal more detail, but decanting does not repair underripeness, cork taint or oxidation.

The style is not static either. More precise parcel work and a greater number of separate vinifications may refine texture without removing the structural character. Whether a new cellar actually achieves that must be demonstrated in the glass over several years.
:::

:::detail{#rang-reputatie-en-fles parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-wines"}
### Second growth is historic market status, not a current tasting score

The 1855 rank reflected contemporary reputation and market price. The later nickname “super second” is informal and has no legal status. Both help explain market position, but neither can decide a vintage, bottle or blind tasting in advance.

With old Las Cases, provenance is therefore practical knowledge. Fill level, capsule, storage and previous trade may reveal more about condition than the fame of the label.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.leoville-las-cases-wines source.clos-du-marquis-history"}
## Other wines — Le Petit Lion is the second wine; Clos du Marquis is not

Le Petit Lion du Marquis de Las Cases was created with the 2007 vintage as Léoville Las Cases' [[concept.second-wine|second wine]]. It generally contains more Merlot and is intended to become approachable sooner, while receiving the same attention to selection, vinification and maturation as its own [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]]. [@source.leoville-las-cases-wines]

Clos du Marquis is different: a separate Saint-Julien wine from its own vineyard, planted and released as a brand from 1902. It is not a repository for lots that fail to reach the Grand Vin. Shared ownership and family resemblance do not turn two wines into first and second labels. [@source.clos-du-marquis-history]
:::

:::detail{#drie-selectielogicas parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.leoville-las-cases-wines source.clos-du-marquis-history"}
### Origin, selection and positioning answer different questions

The Grand Vin is principally the top selection from the historic Las Cases terroirs. Le Petit Lion is a second selection within that producer logic. Clos du Marquis instead begins with another vineyard and has its own first-wine identity. These three models should not be flattened into a single quality ladder.

The label provides the check: cuvée name, producer, appellation and vintage each say something different. The most famous name in the background is not always the origin of the grapes in the bottle.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-opvoeding depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.leoville-las-cases-wines source.decanter-saint-julien-2025"}
## Winemaking and maturation — resolving the Grand Enclos into small lots

Parcel-by-parcel harvest and vinification keep differences separate long enough to assess them. The team can adapt temperature and extraction by vat, follow press wine separately and decide only at blending which lots together form the Grand Vin. During [[concept.elevage|barrel maturation]], tannin, fruit and oak integrate further.

The new winery used for the 2025 harvest greatly increased the number of vats and therefore the scope to work separately even within parcels. [@source.decanter-saint-julien-2025] More vats mean more information and choice, not automatically better wine.
:::

:::detail{#precisie-en-markt parent="wijnmaken-en-opvoeding" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-saint-julien-2025 source.decanter-leoville-las-cases-wines"}
### Technical precision and market expectation pull on the wine together

Finer selection may avoid harshness and improve the purity of lots. At the same time, Las Cases protects a reputation for structure and long development. The cellar therefore does not stand outside style: technical possibilities are used within an idea of what the Grand Vin ought to be.

The wine is offered through the [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] trade and [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]]. Early assessments consequently concern an unfinished blend still in maturation. For a wine known for slow development, that gap between barrel promise and bottle experience is especially large.
:::
