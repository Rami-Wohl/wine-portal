Château Ducru-Beaucaillou lies in [[appellation.saint-julien|Saint-Julien]], on gravel ridges that slope gently towards the Gironde. The estate owes half its name to the large quartz pebbles—the *beaux cailloux*—and the other half to Bertrand Ducru, who bought and greatly developed it in 1797. It was ranked a second growth in the [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]]. [@source.ducru-beaucaillou-history]

The Borie family has been associated with the estate since 1942; Bruno-Eugène Borie has led it since 2003. The [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]] is Cabernet-led and is generally characterised by concentration, fine but evident [[concept.tannin|tannin]] and long development. Elegance is not another word for lightness here: the strongest [[concept.vintage|vintages]] can be precise, powerful and closed for many years at once. [@source.ducru-beaucaillou-family] [@source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical]

## History and ownership — three centuries between trade and vineyard

The documented history begins in 1720, when Jacques de Bergeron married vineyard heiress Marie Dejean. Under the Bergerons, the wine was already traded beyond France. Bertrand Ducru bought the property in 1797, invested in vineyard and cellar, and permanently joined his name to Beaucaillou. His successors saw the estate recognised as a second growth in 1855. [@source.ducru-beaucaillou-history]

After the Johnston and Desbarats families, Francis Borie took charge in 1942. The Borie family developed the estate across three generations; Bruno-Eugène Borie assumed leadership in 2003. This makes Ducru-Beaucaillou a family estate, but not an unchanged time capsule: each ownership phase brought new buildings, techniques, selections and commercial priorities. [@source.ducru-beaucaillou-family]
:::

:::detail{#johnston-en-bordeauxse-pap parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.ducru-beaucaillou-history"}
### The Johnstons joined architecture to viticultural innovation

Nathaniel Johnston bought the estate in 1866. Under his family, château and park gained their striking nineteenth-century appearance. More important to viticulture was the work with manager Ernest David and botanist Alexis Millardet on the copper-and-lime treatment known as Bordeaux mixture. It helped European vineyards defend themselves against downy mildew. [@source.ducru-beaucaillou-history]

The story shows that [[region.medoc|Médoc]] prestige grew from more than classification and architecture. International trade, disease control, capital and practical vineyard management were at least as important to a château's continuity.
:::

:::detail{#breuken-achter-de-continuiteit parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.ducru-beaucaillou-history source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical"}
### Reputation does not remove vulnerability

War, economic crises and Prohibition weakened the Johnston trade; they sold the estate in 1928. A later break was technical: some wines from the late 1980s and early 1990s were affected by TCA, arising not only through cork but also contaminated timber in the cellar. The causes were identified and facilities renewed. [@source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical]

An old name is therefore no guarantee for every old bottle. Vintage, production conditions and bottle provenance remain separate questions, even for a celebrated second growth.
:::

:::section{#wijngaard-en-terroir depth="foundation" source_refs="source.ducru-beaucaillou-terroir source.ducru-beaucaillou-la-croix"}
## Vineyard and terroir — beautiful stones, useful poverty

The name Beaucaillou refers to the large pebbles deposited here by ancient river systems. The gravelly topsoil drains relatively quickly and offers few readily available nutrients. This naturally limits vigour while roots must seek water in deeper layers. The value of this [[concept.terroir|terroir]] does not lie in a magical taste of stone, but in the way soil profile and water behaviour influence vine growth. [@source.ducru-beaucaillou-terroir]

The land slopes only gently. The broad Gironde is close enough to moderate some temperature extremes; it does not prevent bad weather. Exposure, parcel, soil depth, grape variety and the season remain decisive together.
:::

:::detail{#cabernet-en-merlot-op-hun-plek parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.ducru-beaucaillou-terroir source.ducru-beaucaillou-la-croix"}
### Cabernet and Merlot respond differently to the same ridge

[[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet Sauvignon]] forms the structural core of the Grand Vin. On well-drained gravel it can ripen slowly while retaining acidity and tannin. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] generally contributes rounder fruit and volume; [[grape.petit-verdot|Petit Verdot]] can play a small supporting role in some selections.

This is not a fixed recipe. The proportion in the vineyard, the proportion in harvested lots and the final [[concept.assemblage|blend]] are three different figures. A cool, wet or hot season changes which parcels and grapes perform most convincingly.
:::

:::detail{#steen-is-geen-aroma parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.ducru-beaucaillou-terroir source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical"}
### Gravel explains conditions, not the glass word for word

Heat retention, drainage and rooting can influence ripening. It does not follow that quartz tastes directly of graphite, cedar or blackcurrant. Such terms summarise human tasting experience and are also shaped by ripeness, extraction, oak, bottle age and serving context.

Terroir is most useful here as a system: a specific place sets possibilities and limits. Each year, the producer translates them into wine through pruning, harvest timing, selection and cellar work.
:::

## Grand Vin — finesse with a firm spine

Château Ducru-Beaucaillou is the Grand Vin. Across vintages, cassis-like dark fruit, cedar or spice development, fresh tension and fine-grained yet substantial tannin recur regularly. The wine can therefore seem graceful without being quickly accessible. None of these features is equally strong in every vintage or bottle. [@source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical]

The most dependable constant is selection: only lots that together provide the desired length, structure and precision become Grand Vin. The name consequently describes more than the best soil; it is the outcome of annual decisions within the available crop.
:::

:::detail{#stijl-door-de-generaties parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.ducru-beaucaillou-family source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical"}
### House style moves with technique and taste

Under Francis and Jean-Eugène Borie, oenologist Émile Peynaud helped manage ripeness, hygiene and vinification more precisely. Under Bruno Borie, parcel selection tightened further. In strong recent vintages this can yield ripe fruit, polished texture and great concentration together; successful older bottles show how that structure can shift towards leather, tobacco and earthy notes. [@source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical]

This is not a simple march from old-fashioned to modern. Some old years remain exceptionally vivid, some young years need a long time, and no technique erases the differences between growing seasons.
:::

:::detail{#oude-fles-en-herkomst parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical"}
### With old Ducru, provenance is part of wine knowledge

The TCA history makes vintage and bottle provenance especially relevant at Ducru-Beaucaillou. Not every bottle from an affected period is identically compromised, but the château's reputation alone cannot predict condition. Fill level, capsule, cork, storage temperature and documented provenance deserve attention.

Even outside that period, ageing potential is conditional. A great vintage stored warm may be less convincing than a modest year kept perfectly.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.ducru-beaucaillou-la-croix source.ducru-beaucaillou-le-petit"}
## Other wines — not a simple ladder of leftovers

La Croix Ducru-Beaucaillou comes from the estate's own Saint-Julien vineyards and is presented as a distinct terroir expression. Cabernet Sauvignon generally leads the blend, supplemented by Merlot and sometimes Petit Verdot. The [[concept.cuvee|cuvée]] shares the Grand Vin's ambition for precision but draws on a different selection of parcels and lots. [@source.ducru-beaucaillou-la-croix]

Le Petit Ducru is made from a broader selection of Saint-Julien parcels and is intended as an earlier-access introduction to the family's style. From 2019 it succeeded the former Lalande-Borie name. “Petit” signals positioning and drinking horizon; it does not mean that every lot was simply rejected for the two wines above it. [@source.ducru-beaucaillou-le-petit]
:::

:::detail{#selectie-en-merkarchitectuur parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.ducru-beaucaillou-la-croix source.ducru-beaucaillou-le-petit"}
### Second wine, separate cuvée and brand family do not fully coincide

In everyday speech, La Croix is often called the [[concept.second-wine|second wine]] and Le Petit Ducru the third. That helps explain their price and reputation hierarchy, but hides how parcels and intended style help decide where fruit goes. The producer explicitly describes La Croix as its own expression within the vineyard holdings.

Read both cuvée name and appellation and vintage on each bottle. A shared family name tells who made the wine; it does not prove that origin, blend or maturation are the same.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-opvoeding depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical source.ducru-beaucaillou-la-croix"}
## Winemaking and maturation — precision begins before the vat

Hand harvesting and sorting first divide fruit by parcel and quality. Separate vinification lets the team adapt temperature and [[concept.extraction|extraction]] to each lot. The blend then reunites selected components; [[concept.elevage|maturation]] in barrel allows structure and aroma to integrate further.

New cellar technology increases control but does not make the decisions. Ripeness, press wine, barrel choice and the proportion of new oak remain variable by year and cuvée. The Grand Vin is sold through the [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]] trade and [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]]; a barrel sample is therefore always a snapshot before final bottle development.
:::

:::detail{#strengere-selectie-is-geen-neutrale-maat parent="wijnmaken-en-opvoeding" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-ducru-beaucaillou-vertical"}
### Selecting more strictly changes quality and identity

When a smaller share of the crop becomes Grand Vin, average concentration and precision may increase. At the same time, the relationship between cuvées—and potentially the style of the principal wine—changes. “Stricter” is therefore not a purely technical quality control; it is a decision about which expression should carry the Ducru-Beaucaillou name.

The estate's history teaches the same lesson at a larger scale. Terroir remained important, but reputation was repeatedly reshaped by people, investment, mistakes, corrections and the standards of their time.
:::
