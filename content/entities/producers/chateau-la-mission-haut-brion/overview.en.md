Château La Mission Haut-Brion stands in the urban northern sector of [[appellation.pessac-leognan|Pessac-Léognan]], facing its neighbour [[producer.chateau-haut-brion|Château Haut-Brion]]. The two share Dillon family ownership and a broader gravel terrace, but La Mission is neither Haut-Brion's second label nor its vineyard. It is an independent historic cru and a red [[classification.graves|Classified Growth of Graves]]. [@source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths]

The red [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]] is often associated with full dark fruit, powerful yet ripe tannin and smoky, earthy development. Leading vintages can live for a very long time. Such a style portrait helps orientation, but comparison with Haut-Brion must not erase La Mission's own parcels, history and annual blend. [@source.decanter-la-mission-haut-brion]

## History and ownership — wine, trade and a religious legacy

Arnaud de Lestonnac bought land in 1540 that formed the core of La Mission. Marriage connected his family to the Pontacs of Haut-Brion. Lazarist missionaries managed the estate from 1682, improving vineyard and buildings and giving the property its lasting name and chapel identity. It was sold as national property after the French Revolution. [@source.mission-haut-brion-history]

The Chiapella family linked the estate to trade with New Orleans in the nineteenth century. From 1919 the Woltner family modernised vineyard and cellar. Domaine Clarence Dillon, already owner of Haut-Brion, bought La Mission in 1983 and restored buildings and equipment while retaining its separate name. [@source.mission-haut-brion-history]
:::

:::detail{#lazaristen-en-de-naam parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.mission-haut-brion-history"}
### Religious imagery is more than decoration

The Lazarists made chapel, crosses and spiritual references part of the estate. The 1698 chapel survives in the name La Chapelle de La Mission Haut-Brion; great vintages written in gold beneath its vault join wine memory to the space.

This heritage records who organised the property over centuries. It proves no supernatural quality and cannot replace an account of vineyard and winemaking.
:::

:::detail{#woltner-en-temperatuurbeheersing parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.mission-haut-brion-history"}
### A modern cellar began here before stainless steel

Frédéric Woltner installed enamelled steel vats with a glass lining in the 1920s. They allowed cleaner work and better temperature control than many wooden facilities of the day. White wine production began in 1927.

The innovation matters, but not apart from fruit quality and cellar decisions. Equipment can reduce risk and increase control; it cannot make a great wine automatically.
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — the same terrace name, a separate mosaic

La Mission occupies the historic Haut-Brion terrace, with quartz-rich gravel above varying sand and clay. The ground undulates gently and is enclosed by urban development. Gravel aids drainage and warms readily; subsoil and rain determine how much water remains available. These are growing conditions, not direct flavourings. [@source.haut-brion-terroir]

[[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet sauvignon]], [[grape.merlot|merlot]] and [[grape.cabernet-franc|cabernet franc]] work together in red wine. The small white vineyard uses [[grape.semillon|sémillon]] and [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|sauvignon blanc]]. Proportions change with parcel and harvest.
:::

:::detail{#buur-is-geen-dubbelganger parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.haut-brion-terroir source.decanter-la-mission-haut-brion"}
### Facing Haut-Brion does not make it Haut-Brion

The estates are close and managed by the same group. Parcels, grape proportions and historic selections nevertheless differ. Shared technical knowledge makes comparison useful, not conclusive.

La Mission is often described as fuller and more immediate, Haut-Brion as more reserved. This is a useful tasting hypothesis across years, not a natural law for every vintage or bottle.
:::

:::detail{#stedelijke-wijngaard-als-erfgoed parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.mission-haut-brion-history source.haut-brion-terroir"}
### The edge of the city also limits growth

The urban setting preserves a vineyard where building land is valuable. Continuity of parcels therefore carries extra weight; expansion or replacement elsewhere is not straightforward.

This is not a romantic opposition between nature and city. Roads, buildings, drainage and heat are real conditions to which growers must respond.
:::

## Grand Vin — power without volume alone

Château La Mission Haut-Brion Rouge is the Grand Vin and the wine covered by the Graves classification. Its blend changes, but in strong years the wine is often experienced as deep, broad and energetic, with dark fruit, spice, smoky and earthy development and firm tannin. [@source.mission-haut-brion-wines] [@source.decanter-la-mission-haut-brion]

A reputation for richness should not obscure finesse. Bottle age can refine tannin and reveal savoury layers. Poor storage or a lesser year is not corrected by the name.
:::

:::detail{#blend-en-jaargang parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.mission-haut-brion-wines source.decanter-la-mission-haut-brion"}
### The blend moves between merlot and cabernet

Merlot can provide volume and ripe dark fruit; cabernet sauvignon often supplies backbone and length, while cabernet franc can add aromatic nuance. Which role dominates depends on ripeness and selection.

A technical sheet for one year is therefore not a timeless recipe. It shows how the team interpreted that particular season.
:::

:::detail{#la-mission-zonder-1855-fictie parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.pessac-leognan-classified-growths source.decanter-la-mission-haut-brion"}
### High regard does not make La Mission an 1855 first growth

Critics and market analyses have often placed La Mission at first-growth level. That can describe reputation and tasting performance but does not rewrite 1855. The estate is officially a red Classified Growth of Graves.

The precision does not diminish the wine; it prevents critical regard and legal history from merging.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.mission-haut-brion-wines source.mission-haut-brion-history"}
## Other wines — white, second red and a shared cuvée

Château La Mission Haut-Brion Blanc is the dry white Grand Vin, known before the 2009 vintage as Château Laville Haut-Brion. Sémillon normally has a large role and can bring breadth and waxy development; sauvignon blanc adds tension and aromatic clarity. This rare wine sits outside the red Graves classification. [@source.mission-haut-brion-history] [@source.mission-haut-brion-wines]

La Chapelle de La Mission Haut-Brion is the [[concept.second-wine|second red wine]]. La Clarté de Haut-Brion is a shared second white selection from La Mission and Haut-Brion. Reading “Haut-Brion” alone therefore misses which château, colour and selection the bottle contains.
:::

:::detail{#vier-namen-vier-functies parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.mission-haut-brion-wines"}
### Family resemblance is not identical origin

The red Grand Vin and La Chapelle emerge from La Mission's red selection. The white Grand Vin shares the château name but has its own vineyard and grape logic. La Clarté combines white lots from two estates.

The range shows why producer, vineyard, cuvée and classification must remain distinct concepts. One team can make several clearly bounded wines.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-opvoeding depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.mission-haut-brion-wines source.decanter-la-mission-haut-brion"}
## Winemaking and maturation — richness requires restraint

Red grapes are selected and fermented separately by parcel and picking date. Temperature and [[concept.extraction|extraction]] are adapted to each lot; tasting then decides what enters the [[concept.assemblage|blend]] and what goes to La Chapelle. Barrel ageing integrates structure and aroma without erasing differences between years.

White wine requires different decisions about pressing, oxidation, freshness and barrels. Red and white Grand Vin share a name and organisation, not one cellar recipe. Here too, a young barrel sample is a provisional stage rather than a certain forecast of bottle development.
:::
