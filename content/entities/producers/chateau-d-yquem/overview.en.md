Château d'Yquem lies in [[appellation.sauternes|Sauternes]], south of the city of [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. The estate makes sweet white wine mainly from [[grape.semillon|Sémillon]], supplemented by [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|Sauvignon Blanc]]. The key is [[concept.botrytis|noble rot]]: a favourable form of fungal infection that dehydrates ripe grapes, concentrating sugar, acidity and flavour. Pickers therefore pass through the same vineyard several times and take only suitable grapes. [@source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026]

Yquem is the only *premier cru supérieur* in the [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]]. That unique rank explains part of its prestige, but not the taste in the glass. For that, look to the combination of place, botrytis, exceptionally low yields, selection and barrel maturation. The [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]] can be rich and sweet, yet its reputation rests on tension: freshness and sometimes a lightly bitter edge must keep its weight alive. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview] [@source.decanter-yquem-profile]

## History and ownership — from noble estate to wine icon

Yquem's history is older than its classification. In 1593 Jacques Sauvage received tenure over the estate; his family developed the château and assembled the vineyard parcel by parcel. Yquem became the family's outright property in 1711. The 1785 marriage of heiress Françoise-Joséphine de Sauvage d'Yquem to Louis-Amédée de Lur-Saluces connected the Lur-Saluces name to the estate. [@source.yquem-history-archive] [@source.persee-lur-saluces-yquem]

Françoise-Joséphine was widowed in 1788 and led the property through revolution, war and economic uncertainty. Under her and her descendants, Yquem developed from landed estate into a specialised wine business. Its rank as *premier cru supérieur* in 1855 confirmed that the Bordeaux trade already valued the wine exceptionally highly. The Lur-Saluces family remained decisive until the end of the twentieth century; the château has belonged to LVMH since 1999. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history] [@source.lvmh-yquem]
:::

:::detail{#josephine-en-de-tries parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.yquem-history-archive source.lemonde-yquem-2025 source.persee-lur-saluces-yquem"}
### Joséphine and the emergence of selective picking

The estate attributes an important role to Françoise-Joséphine in refining successive picking rounds, known as *tries*. The account fits her long stewardship and investments, including a new cellar built in 1826. A neat origin story is nevertheless too simple. Historical letters suggest that late harvesting already occurred in the area; a method may emerge gradually and only later be organised systematically. [@source.yquem-history-archive] [@source.lemonde-yquem-2025]

The lasting innovation was not one accidentally delayed harvest, but a business model able to sustain patience. Grapes were not brought in all at once. Pickers returned to take only fruit that had reached the right concentration and condition at that moment. The method is labour-intensive and leaves part of the crop exposed to loss, but it permits far more precise selection.
:::

:::detail{#familiebedrijf-en-overgang parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.yquem-history-archive source.lemonde-yquem-2025 source.lvmh-yquem source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
### Continuity, crisis and the transition to LVMH

The family history was no uninterrupted triumph. Wars, economic crises and difficult harvests affected Yquem too. In several twentieth-century years the Grand Vin was not released at all; 2012 was rejected as well. In retrospect, such a decision reinforces the story of quality, but at the time it had a plain consequence: much of the estate's principal revenue disappeared. [@source.lemonde-yquem-2025]

During the twentieth century, Bertrand de Lur-Saluces defended both Yquem and the legal identity of Sauternes. Alexandre de Lur Saluces took charge in 1968. The acquisition by LVMH in 1999 ultimately placed the estate within an international luxury-goods group. That changed the ownership structure, not its legal origin: the wine remains subject to the rules of Sauternes and the risks of each new harvest. [@source.lvmh-yquem] [@source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026]
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — gravel over clay, water under control

Yquem occupies one of the higher sites in Sauternes. Its soil is not a uniform recipe but a mosaic of gravel, sand and clay. Gravel warms readily and drains rainwater; clay deeper down retains water for dry periods. The estate also describes numerous springs and an extensive drainage network. [@source.yquem-estate-archive]

That drainage matters because noble rot requires a precarious balance. Damp mornings help the fungus develop, but persistent wet weather may damage grapes without producing the desired concentration. A well-drained vineyard stops every shower from having the same effect on roots and fruit. Soil, elevation, airflow, weather and human work therefore form the [[concept.terroir|terroir]] together; no single component explains Yquem.
:::

:::detail{#honderd-dertien-hectare parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.yquem-estate-archive source.decanter-yquem-profile"}
### A large holding that never produces in full

The estate reports 113 hectares of vineyard, around 100 of which are in production at any one time. Small areas are uprooted each year and replanted after a fallow period, so part of the holding always remains out of production. The producer gives a planting split of approximately 75% Sémillon and 25% Sauvignon Blanc. Older independent profiles sometimes report different areas or proportions; this may reflect the date of measurement and the distinction between total area, productive area and the composition of a particular wine. [@source.yquem-estate-archive] [@source.decanter-yquem-profile]

Sémillon is the principal variety: its skin and ripening pattern make it particularly suited to botrytis, and it contributes volume and a waxy texture. Sauvignon Blanc generally adds a sharper aromatic profile and more perceptible acidity. The proportions in the bottle need not mirror those in the ground.
:::

:::detail{#waterbeheer-en-perceelkennis parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.yquem-estate-archive"}
### Making the subsoil manageable

According to the estate, approximately one hundred kilometres of drains run beneath the vineyard, some installed as early as the nineteenth century. The figure is producer-reported, but it makes an important point visible: a celebrated “natural” site functions partly through constructed infrastructure. [@source.yquem-estate-archive]

Drainage does not make the vineyard uniform. A gravel crest may dry more quickly than a clay-rich parcel; a hollow may remain damp for longer. With botrytis, such differences are time-sensitive. Parcel knowledge therefore means knowing not only which variety grows where, but where fungus, ripening and dehydration will change fastest after mist, sun or rain.
:::

## Grape varieties and botrytis — a harvest made of countless small decisions

A bunch does not automatically become noble because it carries botrytis. In favourable weather, the fungus makes microscopic openings in the skin, allowing water to evaporate and the berry to shrivel. Sugar and acidity become concentrated while new aromas develop. In unfavourable, persistently wet conditions, the same fungus can produce grey rot and loss of flavour. [[concept.botrytis|Botrytis]] is therefore not an ingredient a winemaker simply adds, but an opportunity judged berry by berry.

That is why Yquem harvests in successive *tries*. A picking team returns several times, leaving unripe, insufficiently concentrated or damaged grapes on the vine. The foundation of the wine is laid before it reaches the cellar: not on one perfect harvest date, but through many small choices spread over weeks. [@source.yquem-expertise-archive]
:::

:::detail{#tries-opbrengst-en-risico parent="druiven-en-botrytis" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.yquem-expertise-archive"}
### Five or six passes for a little juice

Yquem describes an average of five or six picking rounds over roughly six weeks; in extreme years there may be more than ten and the harvest can continue into December. The estate reports a very low average yield of about 9 hectolitres per hectare. [@source.yquem-expertise-archive] That is neither a magical constant nor a legal target for every Sauternes producer. It results from shrivelled berries, severe selection and fruit lost to weather or rejection.

The expense therefore lies in more than barrels and buildings. Many pickers must be available on demand and at precisely the right moment. Rain between two rounds can destroy value; dry weather can halt botrytis development. The same system that allows concentration also makes production unpredictable.
:::

:::detail{#geen-yquem-in-een-zwak-jaar parent="druiven-en-botrytis" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.lemonde-yquem-2025 source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
### When selection ends with no Grand Vin

Yquem has chosen not to make its Grand Vin in a series of weak or unsuitable years. In the twentieth century these included 1910, 1915, 1930, 1951, 1952, 1964, 1972, 1974 and 1992; 2012 was not released either. [@source.lemonde-yquem-2025] This does not mean the appellation could produce no wine in those years, but that the château considered its own selection threshold unmet.

Such a list is easily read as heroic marketing. More critically, it shows how much latitude a wealthy estate has to put reputation ahead of volume. Smaller producers must select too, but are less able to absorb the financial cost of losing an entire principal wine. The decision is simultaneously a quality measure, an economic risk and a form of brand management.
:::

## Grand Vin — sweetness carried by tension

Château d'Yquem is the estate's sweet Grand Vin. When young it may suggest citrus, apricot, peach, pineapple, flowers, honey and spice; with bottle age, dried fruit, marmalade, saffron, wax, toast and nutty tones may emerge. No bottle is required to tick every box. Vintage, age, storage history and serving temperature determine which side speaks most clearly. [@source.decanter-yquem-profile]

Its defining stylistic quality is not maximum sugar but proportion. Sweetness supplies weight; acidity keeps the wine moving; botrytis brings concentration and distinctive aromas; a fine bitterness can sharpen the finish. When those elements support one another, the wine feels intense rather than heavy. That balance also explains why Yquem can work at the table beyond dessert alone.
:::

:::detail{#stijl-en-flesontwikkeling parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-yquem-profile source.yquem-expertise-archive"}
### From bright fruit to amber and spice

A young vintage may still be bright gold and strikingly fresh in aroma, while an old bottle can turn deep amber or brown-orange. Primary fruit does not simply disappear; it changes and becomes interwoven with maturation aromas. The 1973 bottle on this page demonstrates colour development, but is no standard colour chart: oxygen, closure, light and storage can alter the pace dramatically.

Yquem can mature for a very long time when origin, vintage and storage all cooperate. “Can” matters more than an exact number of years. A half bottle generally develops faster than a larger format, a warm cellar faster than a cool one, and a damaged cork can invalidate every prediction. Age is therefore not an automatic ladder of quality; young and old Yquem offer different experiences.
:::

:::detail{#proeven-zonder-het-etiket parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-yquem-profile"}
### Tasting without tasting the label

The name Yquem can shape expectations so powerfully that every feature is read as proof of greatness. A better tasting method begins without relying on rank: assess sweetness, acidity, alcohol warmth, concentration, aromatic purity, texture, bitterness and length separately. Then ask whether they form a coherent whole, and only afterwards what origin and age add.

This also avoids an overly narrow style portrait. Warm years may appear broader and more tropical; cooler years lighter or more citrus-driven; botrytis can be distinctly spicy or relatively restrained. Oak may be visible without dominating the wine. “Yquem tastes of honey” is therefore a useful opening image but a poor final conclusion.
:::

:::section{#wijnmaken-en-opvoeding depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.yquem-expertise-archive"}
## Winemaking and maturation — many lots, one wine only at the end

The selected grapes are pressed in several stages. The estate describes how later pressings can be smaller in volume but richer in sugar. Press wines are combined before fermentation takes place in new oak barrels. Each barrel is monitored because sugar, botrytis and yeast activity do not behave identically in every lot. [@source.yquem-expertise-archive]

After fermentation, lots initially remain separate. A preliminary selection and blend follows in spring; only chosen barrels continue towards the final wine. The wine then undergoes a long [[concept.elevage|maturation]] in wood before bottling. The purpose of all this separation is paradoxical: preserve differences for as long as possible in order to create one convincing whole at the end.
:::

:::detail{#persing-gisting-en-selectie parent="wijnmaken-en-opvoeding" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.yquem-expertise-archive"}
### Why the final pressing is not automatically the least valuable

With dry white wine, firmer pressing is often treated cautiously because of bitterness and coarse material. Shrivelled botrytised berries alter that relationship: the first juice is not necessarily the most concentrated, and later fractions can be exceptionally high in sugar. Yquem reports three to four pressings and rising potential alcohol in the later fractions. [@source.yquem-expertise-archive]

“Gentle pressing” is therefore not a complete quality criterion. What matters more is which fractions are clean, balanced and useful, and how they are combined. New barrels are not an end in themselves either. They provide small, controllable fermentation vessels and influence oxygen and flavour, but overly conspicuous oak could obscure the tension of fruit and botrytis.
:::

:::section{#overige-wijnen depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.yquem-y-archive source.decanter-yquem-profile source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026"}
## Other wines — Y, the dry countervoice

Y — pronounced *Ygrec* — is the estate's dry white wine. Its first vintage was made in 1959; it appeared irregularly for many years before Yquem chose annual production from 2004. Sauvignon Blanc generally forms the majority and is picked earlier; Sémillon is added when the first influence of botrytis appears. [@source.yquem-y-archive]

Y is not a dry version of the same Sauternes. The Sauternes appellation is for sweet wine and imposes legal requirements covering grapes, ripeness and harvest, among other matters; the estate's dry wine therefore bears a different geographical designation. [@source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026] It shares parcels, people and some grape varieties with the Grand Vin, but harvest timing, sugar level, vinification and style give it a separate identity.
:::

:::detail{#y-in-de-kelder parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.yquem-y-archive"}
### Sauvignon for tension, Sémillon for breadth

According to the producer, Y ferments and matures in barrel, with roughly one-third new wood. It remains on fine lees for around ten months and is regularly stirred, a technique called [[concept.batonnage|bâtonnage]]. [[concept.lees-ageing|Lees ageing]] can add texture and savoury complexity, while Sauvignon Blanc guards the aromatic and acidic line. [@source.yquem-y-archive]

The [[concept.assemblage|blend]] varies with the [[concept.vintage|vintage]]. This is essential: Y is not a fixed recipe and the estate's planted proportions do not predict the bottle. The dry wine also uses fruit selected at another stage of ripeness. It must therefore be judged on its own balance, not as a by-product or diluted Grand Vin.
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-reputatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-overview source.gcc1855-classification-history source.lemonde-yquem-2025"}
## Classification and reputation — alone at the top, not beyond debate

For the 1855 Paris Exposition, the Bordeaux trade ranked the best-known red wines of the [[region.medoc|Médoc]] and the sweet white wines of Sauternes and [[appellation.barsac|Barsac]]. Price and trading reputation were important measures. Yquem received a top category of its own: *premier cru supérieur*. Premiers and deuxièmes crus followed beneath it. [@source.gcc1855-classification-history]

That position is historically remarkable and helps explain why Yquem is treated as a reference wine. It does not mean every vintage automatically tastes better than every other Sauternes, that the vineyard sits outside appellation rules, or that the classification is a current independent quality inspection. The rank describes one place in a nineteenth-century system; the château must try to meet the resulting expectation anew with every harvest.
:::

:::detail{#prijs-schaarste-en-prestige parent="classificatie-en-reputatie" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.gcc1855-classification-history source.lemonde-yquem-2025"}
### Why scarcity is structural here

Yquem's price has no single cause. Historical rank and worldwide demand strengthen the brand, while selective picking, low yield, labour, barrel maturation and the risk of a rejected crop constrain production. Scarcity is thus partly biological and labour-intensive, partly the result of a self-imposed quality threshold, and partly market dynamics.

A high price does not in itself prove greater complexity in your glass. It can, however, show what collectors expect and how much confidence they place in origin, vintage and condition. With old bottles, storage history can ultimately matter more than the famous label.
:::

:::section{#etiket-serveren-en-bewaren depth="foundation" source_refs="source.inao-sauternes-specification-2026 source.decanter-yquem-profile"}
## Label, serving and storage — first read which Yquem is in front of you

A bottle of the sweet principal wine bears Château d'Yquem, the vintage and Sauternes. Older labels may carry the Lur-Saluces name; that is part of the ownership history, not another appellation. Also check bottle format and, for old wine, fill level, signs of leakage and the condition of cork and capsule. Y looks recognisably related but is a separate dry wine and does not simply carry the same Sauternes label.

Serve Yquem cool rather than ice-cold: around 10–12 °C is a useful starting point. A generous white-wine glass allows aroma and temperature to develop. Small pours often work better than a large dessert-wine serving. Pair by balance: salt, fat, umami or spice may be more interesting than still more sugar. Blue cheese, foie gras, poultry, lobster or gently spiced dishes can work; with dessert, the dish should preferably not be sweeter than the wine.
:::

:::detail{#openen-en-volgen parent="etiket-serveren-en-bewaren" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-yquem-profile"}
### A bottle need not disappear in one evening

Thanks to sugar, acidity and alcohol, a sound bottle of Yquem can often last longer after opening than a dry white wine. Reseal it cleanly, keep it cool and follow how its aroma changes. This is not a fixed keeping guarantee: age, oxidation and the condition before opening remain decisive.

Do not automatically decant old Yquem. Sediment is seldom the principal issue in white wine; exposure to abundant oxygen may make a fragile old bottle decline faster. Taste first, pour gently and allow time in the glass. A young, powerful vintage may instead benefit from extra air to loosen the impression of oak and concentration.
:::

## Contemporary challenges — precision beneath a changing sky

Yquem's model remains dependent on a narrow weather window. Heat may raise sugar rapidly before aromas and botrytis are ideal; drought may inhibit fungal development; heavy rain can send rot in the wrong direction. Each extra *trie* also requires available pickers and quick decisions. Climate change therefore affects not only ripeness here, but the timing and economic viability of the entire harvesting method.

There is also a changing market. Sweet wine is less routinely drunk at the table than in some earlier periods, while Yquem attracts enormous attention as a luxury object and collectible. The challenge is to make the wine intelligible as a living product without reducing it to a status symbol. Tasting, serving and comparison return attention to what the estate actually makes.
:::

:::detail{#risico-als-kwaliteitsmodel parent="hedendaagse-uitdagingen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.yquem-expertise-archive source.lemonde-yquem-2025"}
### Risk is not romantic scenery

The story of late picking, mist and shrivelled grapes sounds picturesque, but the margins are unforgiving. Delay can bring greater concentration while threatening the entire crop. A château with capital, stocks and a global brand can carry that risk differently from a small family business. Yquem is therefore an exceptionally visible example of Sauternes, not a scale model for every producer.

Nor does strict selection solve everything. It can keep a weak lot out of the Grand Vin, but cannot restore a lost crop or create freshness that was absent from the grape. Yquem's reliability does not lie in guaranteed perfection. It lies in a repeatable system of observing, waiting, selecting and — when necessary — refusing.
:::
