**Château Angélus** stands on the south-facing foot of the slope in [[appellation.saint-emilion-grand-cru|Saint-Émilion Grand Cru]]. Its name refers to the Angelus prayer: family tradition says vineyard workers could hear the bells of three churches here. The bell became the château's visual emblem.

The de Boüard de Laforest family has been connected with the place since 1782. [[grape.merlot|Merlot]] and an unusually large share of [[grape.cabernet-franc|Cabernet Franc]] anchor the [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]]. Angélus rose to Premier Grand Cru Classé A in 2012 but withdrew its application for the [[classification.saint-emilion|2022 classification]]. Its A rank is therefore historic.
:::

## History and ownership — eight generations and a new name

Jean de Boüard de Laforest settled in Saint-Émilion in 1782. Marriage and consolidation brought family plots around Mazerat into one property. Maurice de Boüard de Laforest added the small Clos de l’Angélus around 1920; from 1945, Angélus became the name of the whole estate. [@source.chateau-angelus-property] [@source.decanter-chateau-angelus-profile]

Hubert de Boüard took charge in 1985 and strongly emphasized ripeness, selection and technical renewal. His daughter Stéphanie de Boüard-Rivoal joined management in 2012 and now chairs the estate. The château consequently remained family-owned while its surface, cellars and international visibility expanded considerably.
:::

:::detail{#van-mazerat-naar-angelus parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-angelus-property source.decanter-chateau-angelus-profile"}
### From Mazerat to Angélus

The modern story is not a straight line from one medieval château. Today's identity emerged by combining plots, family ties and names. The three-hectare Clos de l’Angélus eventually supplied the name for a much larger property. A celebrated wine brand can thus grow from both land and deliberate historical positioning.
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — an amphitheatre below the slope

The core vineyard lies less than a kilometre from the village, in a natural south-facing bowl. Calcareous clay and sandier topsoil above denser clay alternate across slope and foot. The latter profile can retain water and allow late-ripening Cabernet Franc to mature when the season cooperates.

The Grand Vin draws on about thirty hectares. Current plantings are around 52% Merlot and 47% Cabernet Franc, with a tiny share of other red varieties. That near equality is striking within [[appellation.saint-emilion|Saint-Émilion]]: Cabernet Franc is not an accent here, but a leading voice. [@source.chateau-angelus-technical-sheet]
:::

:::detail{#bouchet-en-cabernet-franc parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-angelus-profile"}
### Why Cabernet Franc became so important

Maurice de Boüard increased Cabernet Franc in the 1920s. Family history also connects the choice to his wife Elisabeth Bouchet—*bouchet* is an old local name for Cabernet Franc. Whatever the charming prompt, the variety became structurally important: later harvesting and better plot management helped preserve its floral perfume, freshness and spine.
:::

:::detail{#ligging-is-geen-recept parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-angelus-profile"}
### Position is not a recipe

A southern slope can aid ripening, but in hot years it may also increase heat and rapid sugar accumulation. Clay can buffer water, while compaction and root distribution alter the effect. [[concept.terroir|Terroir]] therefore explains opportunities and limits; it does not replace analysis of vintage, vineyard work and harvest decisions.
:::

:::section{#grand-vin depth="foundation" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-angelus-profile source.chateau-angelus-wines"}
## Grand Vin — Merlot breadth with a Cabernet Franc spine

Château Angélus is the principal wine. Merlot can bring ripe dark fruit and broad texture; Cabernet Franc adds perfume, freshness and shaped [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. The recognizable ambition is generous but not shapeless: ripe fruit should be carried by fragrance and structure.

Style changes with [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and cellar choices. Riper periods in its history may appear fuller and more marked by oak and [[concept.extraction|extraction]]; recent work places greater emphasis on precision and balance. Neither description should become a fixed tasting guarantee for every bottle.
:::

:::detail{#rijpheid-en-precisie parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-angelus-profile"}
### Ripeness and precision

From the 1980s, drainage, canopy, sorting tables and separate ripeness decisions were refined. This particularly mattered for Cabernet Franc, which no longer had to be picked automatically with Merlot. In the cellar, grape and lot receive different vessels and adapted [[concept.elevage|maturation]]. Technique serves the blend; it is not itself the source of quality.
:::

## Other wines — Carillon, N°3 and Tempo

Carillon d’Angélus has existed since 1987. Although often called a [[concept.second-wine|second wine]], it now has dedicated plot selection and its own winery. Since the 2019 vintage its label states Saint-Émilion rather than Saint-Émilion Grand Cru because the new vinification site lies outside the Grand Cru production area. [@source.chateau-angelus-carillon-cellar]

N°3 d’Angélus is Merlot-dominant, matured for less time and intended for younger drinking. Tempo d’Angélus is a separate wine from [[region.bordeaux|Bordeaux]]. Their ascending names suggest a simple quality ladder, but origin, appellation and purpose differ; read each label independently. [@source.chateau-angelus-wines]
:::

:::section{#classificatie-en-reputatie depth="foundation" source_refs="source.chateau-angelus-withdrawal-2022 source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022 source.decanter-chateau-angelus-profile"}
## Classification and reputation — ascent, A rank and withdrawal

Angélus began the first classification as a Grand Cru Classé, rose to Premier Grand Cru Classé in 1996 and received distinction A in 2012. Early in 2022 the château withdrew its application for the new edition against the background of years of legal conflict around earlier rankings. It therefore does not appear on the current list. [@source.chateau-angelus-withdrawal-2022]

This trajectory makes Angélus a key example of the revisable system: a château can rise through new assessments and later stand outside the procedure. Its historic A rank remains a fact for 2012–2021, but is not a current title.
:::

:::detail{#reputatie-en-institutie parent="classificatie-en-reputatie" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-angelus-withdrawal-2022 source.inao-saint-emilion-classification-2022"}
### Reputation and institution do not move in lockstep

Angélus's rapid ascent shows that the Saint-Émilion classification can recognize change. Its withdrawal also shows how authority becomes vulnerable when criteria, interests and procedures are contested. This yields no simple judgement on the wine. Formal rank, market status and sensory quality are connected, but not identical.
:::

:::section{#etiket-serveren-en-bewaren depth="foundation" source_refs="source.chateau-angelus-wines source.chateau-angelus-withdrawal-2022"}
## Label, serving and storage — hear the bell, read the small print

The bell motif makes Angélus instantly recognizable. Also check name, appellation, [[concept.vintage|vintage]] and classification wording: these carry legal meaning, especially for Carillon and older Grand Vin bottles. Young Grand Vin may benefit from air and a generous glass; treat mature bottles gently and let condition outweigh a fixed decanting routine.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Angélus joins family continuity to deliberate change. The bell and ripe Merlot attract immediate attention, but abundant Cabernet Franc, the gradual classification ascent and present absence from the list complete the story.
:::
