Château Margaux stands in the village of Margaux, on one of the gently raised gravel ridges of the [[appellation.margaux|appellation that shares its name]]. A first growth since 1855, its name refers at once to a producer, an estate and a famous red [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]]. That overlap makes the label look simple; behind it sits a carefully separated family of red and white wines. [@source.gcc1855-classification-overview]

The Mentzelopoulos family has owned the estate since 1977. The red flagship is normally led by cabernet and, across successful vintages, is associated with perfume, fine texture, length and a structure that can live far longer than its graceful first impression suggests. This is an orientation rather than a flavour guarantee: vintage, bottle age and storage remain decisive. [@source.chateau-margaux-history] [@source.decanter-chateau-margaux-vertical]

## History and ownership — from La Mothe to a family estate

The place was known in the Middle Ages as La Mothe de Margaux, referring to a slight rise in an otherwise flat landscape. Only from the sixteenth century did the Lestonnac family assemble a coherent wine estate. In the seventeenth century, steward Berlon vinified red and white grapes separately and delayed picking until morning dew had lifted: early examples of choices that made an origin wine more distinct. [@source.chateau-margaux-history]

The present neoclassical château was commissioned by the Marquis de la Colonilla from 1810. Margaux received the highest rank in 1855. After changing owners and a difficult twentieth century, André Mentzelopoulos bought the property in 1977 and invested in drainage, replanting and buildings. His daughter Corinne continued that course; the next family generation has taken a more prominent role since 2023. [@source.chateau-margaux-history]
:::

:::detail{#naam-reputatie-en-handel parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.chateau-margaux-history"}
### An estate name became more important than the village alone

Margaux was among the Bordeaux wines traded early under a recognisable origin name. This was more than branding: separate vinification, parcel selection and deliberate ripeness helped turn mixed agricultural holdings into an estate wine. The name therefore carried meaning before the classification confirmed it formally.

That continuity is not a straight quality line. War, economic decline, vine disease and ownership changes caused real breaks. The revival from 1977 matters precisely because prestige alone could not rescue a neglected estate.
:::

:::detail{#architectuur-is-geen-terroir parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-margaux-history"}
### The famous building and the wine tell different stories

The columned façade is Château Margaux's visual emblem, but it was built centuries after the vineyard began to take shape. It records nineteenth-century capital and representation; the viticultural identity lies in parcels, grapes, selection and successive teams.

The distinction applies elsewhere in Bordeaux too. Architecture can make history visible, but it does not prove soil quality or explain an aroma. A Grand Vin is not a monument in liquid form.
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — gravel, clay and small changes in height

The leading red parcels lie largely on well-drained gravel rises. Relief is subtle: a few metres can alter drainage and soil depth. [[grape.cabernet-sauvignon|Cabernet sauvignon]] is concentrated on deeper gravel, while [[grape.merlot|merlot]] plays a larger role where more clay retains water and vigour. Small amounts of [[grape.cabernet-franc|cabernet franc]] and [[grape.petit-verdot|petit verdot]] widen the possibilities for the [[concept.assemblage|blend]]. [@source.decanter-chateau-margaux-vertical]

The dry-white vineyard occupies another part of the estate and is planted with [[grape.sauvignon-blanc|sauvignon blanc]]. It is not a white extension of AOC Margaux: the appellation covers red wine, so the white is released under a broader Bordeaux origin. [@source.chateau-margaux-pavillon-blanc]
:::

:::detail{#druivenrollen-en-jaargang parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-margaux-vertical"}
### The blend follows ripeness rather than a fixed recipe

Cabernet sauvignon usually supplies backbone, freshness and slow development; merlot can add middle-palate volume and roundness. Cabernet franc and petit verdot are smaller but sometimes expressive components. Vineyard proportions are not bottle proportions: selection and season decide which lots enter the final wine.

“Cabernet-led” is therefore more useful than an exact percentage without a vintage. Cool, wet and hot, dry seasons change the balance, while replanting alters the available grapes over decades.
:::

:::detail{#terroir-zonder-parfumformule parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-margaux-vertical"}
### Gravel explains water relations, not violets directly

Drainage, warmth, rooting and available water affect how evenly grapes ripen. Gravel itself does not taste of flowers, cedar or graphite. Those terms summarise human tasting experience shaped also by fruit, extraction, oak, bottle development and serving context.

Margaux shows why terroir is more useful as a system than a flavour code. Place creates possibilities and limits; people decide when to pick, what to select and how long to age the wine.
:::

## Grand Vin — grace with staying power

Château Margaux is the red Grand Vin. Vertical tastings repeatedly find floral perfume, red and black fruit, fine tannin and long, layered development. The wine can feel silky while retaining structure for decades. “Elegant” does not mean thin, sweet or immediately ready to drink. [@source.decanter-chateau-margaux-vertical]

The Grand Vin is an annual selection, not an immutable parcel map. Lots are followed separately and only later assembled through tasting. Both the blend and the share of the crop bearing the main name therefore change.
:::

:::detail{#flesrijping-en-serveercontext parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-chateau-margaux-vertical"}
### Finesse often becomes clearer with time

Young vintages may hide their perfume behind tannin, oak and compact fruit. With sound storage, structure can relax and make room for dried flowers, tobacco, earthy and savoury nuances. Bottles do not follow one clock: vintage, format, closure and storage history matter.

Old Margaux therefore rewards careful handling. Standing it upright, opening gently and tasting before prolonged decanting protects a fragile bottle better than one universal serving rule.
:::

:::detail{#stijl-en-selectie parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-margaux-history source.decanter-chateau-margaux-vertical"}
### A stricter Grand Vin changes the rest of the range too

When fewer lots become Château Margaux, the flagship may appear more consistent and concentrated. Strong fruit also moves into Pavillon Rouge and further selections. A stylistic gain in the Grand Vin cannot be separated from newly drawn boundaries between labels.

Consistency under the Mentzelopoulos family is historically important, but it does not make older bottles worthless or recent ones identical. House style is a moving tradition, not a recipe.
:::

## Other wines — three selections and a white branch

Pavillon Rouge du Château Margaux is the historic [[concept.second-wine|second red wine]]. Its present name has been used since 1906; it is usually cabernet-led and offers an earlier, more direct view of the estate family without copying the Grand Vin. Margaux du Château Margaux, introduced from the 2009 harvest, is a further red selection. [@source.chateau-margaux-pavillon-rouge] [@source.chateau-margaux-history]

Pavillon Blanc is a dry sauvignon blanc with a history reaching back to the seventeenth century and its current name since 1920. Pavillon Blanc Second Vin has recently joined it. Across the range, read colour, cuvée, vintage and appellation separately: the words “Château Margaux” do not make every wine red or a first growth. [@source.chateau-margaux-pavillon-blanc]
:::

:::detail{#wit-buiten-margaux parent="overige-wijnen" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.chateau-margaux-pavillon-blanc source.inao-margaux-specification-2023"}
### Pavillon Blanc is an estate wine but not AOC Margaux

The white parcel, owner and team belong to Château Margaux; the protected Margaux origin does not include this dry white. The distinction between estate and appellation is legally substantial. A château can make wines under different origins without its name overriding their rules.

Selection is dynamic too. Part of the sauvignon becomes Pavillon Blanc, some enters the second white and the rest may leave the estate labels. “Second” describes an editorial and commercial selection, not automatically younger vines or inferior ground.
:::

## Winemaking and maturation — many small decisions, one final name

Red parcels are picked, sorted and fermented separately so ripeness and [[concept.extraction|extraction]] can be managed lot by lot. The blend follows fermentation; barrel [[concept.elevage|maturation]] allows tannin and aroma to integrate before final bottling. The winery completed in 2015 created more scope for separate vinification but did not replace tasting. [@source.decanter-chateau-margaux-vertical]

White wine calls for another process and shorter maturation focused on freshness. Exact oak percentages and production figures vary and belong to a vintage, not an eternal estate definition. The reds pass through the Bordeaux trade and [[concept.en-primeur|en primeur]]; early barrel impressions remain provisional snapshots.
:::
