**Petrus** is a compact wine estate on the plateau of [[appellation.pomerol|Pomerol]], known for a single red wine made from [[grape.merlot|Merlot]]. The property currently covers about 11.4 hectares on a clay-rich site that holds water differently from the surrounding gravel and sand. [@source.lemonde-petrus-2025]

The name appears without “Château” on the label and the appellation has no official cru classification. Petrus is not therefore Pomerol’s statutory highest growth; its position arose from vineyard, selection, family work, distribution, scarcity and decades of reputation. The Moueix family retains control, while the Santo Domingo family has held a minority interest since a transaction disclosed in 2018. [@source.lemonde-petrus-2025]

## History and ownership — a reputation built in the twentieth century

Petrus was already a respected Pomerol in the nineteenth century and won gold at the 1878 Paris Exposition Universelle. The decisive acceleration came in the twentieth century. Hotelier Edmonde Loubat began acquiring shares in the estate from 1925 and owned it outright by the end of the Second World War. She improved the property and treated the wine as a name with its own future, rather than anonymous red Bordeaux. [@source.decanter-petrus-profile]

Jean-Pierre Moueix, who had founded his Libourne merchant house in 1937, became her commercial partner. After Loubat’s death in 1961, his family interest grew; he became sole owner in 1969. [@source.moueix-history] The modern legend rests not on a single discovery or famous dinner, but on the connection between farming, cellar discipline, market knowledge and sustained distribution.
:::

:::detail{#mensen-achter-de-naam parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.lemonde-petrus-2025 source.decanter-petrus-profile"}
### From Loubat and Moueix to the Berrouet family

Edmonde Loubat gave Petrus ambition; Jean-Pierre Moueix helped place it with international buyers. In the cellar, Jean-Claude Berrouet shaped dozens of harvests from the 1960s onwards. His son Olivier took over technical direction in 2009. [@source.lemonde-petrus-2025]

That succession resists a simple hero myth. Ownership, commerce and winemaking are different roles, each with its own continuity. According to reporting published in 2025, Jean-François Moueix manages the family estate and a new generation is involved; such current roles remain time-sensitive and require confirmation at a future review.
:::

:::detail{#marktstatus-zonder-rang parent="geschiedenis-en-eigendom" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.lemonde-petrus-2025 source.decanter-petrus-profile"}
### Market status without official rank

Pomerol has no counterpart to the [[classification.bordeaux-1855|1855 classification]] or Saint-Émilion’s periodic ranking. Petrus can consequently be one of the world’s most expensive wines while legally being simply Pomerol. Price measures scarcity, demand, distribution and reputation; it is not a laboratory result for quality.

Rarity also increases the risk of counterfeiting and uncertain bottle history. For an older bottle, seller, chain of custody, storage, fill level, capsule and label matter at least as much as the harvest year. A famous label makes scrutiny more necessary, not less.
:::

## Vineyard and terroir — Merlot on clay

The vineyard forms an almost contiguous block of about 11.4 hectares on the highest part of Pomerol’s gentle plateau. Merlot is its only current variety. [@source.lemonde-petrus-2025] That is unusual, not mystical: variety, clay-rich subsoil, relief and water behaviour interact with vine age, season and human management.

Clay can hold considerable water and changes volume between wet and dry conditions. In dry periods, that reserve can support the vine for longer; in wet years, drainage and oxygen around the roots become more critical. Soil creates possibilities, but cannot make wine without pruning, crop management, healthy fruit and precisely chosen harvest dates.
:::

:::detail{#klei-is-geen-aroma parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.lemonde-petrus-2025 source.decanter-petrus-profile"}
### Clay is not an aroma

Petrus is often explained through “blue clay”. That is shorthand for a complex soil profile, not a pigment or flavour component that passes directly into the grape. Clay chiefly affects the root environment, water availability, temperature and growth. Aromas arise later from fruit, fermentation, maturation and bottle development.

Nor does one hundred percent Merlot automatically mean softness. On this site the variety can build considerable concentration, acidity and [[concept.tannin|tannin]]. The young wine can consequently seem compact and reticent; the velvety texture associated with mature bottles is partly the work of time.
:::

:::detail{#jaargang-blijft-beslissend parent="wijngaard-en-terroir" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.lemonde-petrus-2025"}
### One variety makes the vintage more visible

A blend of varieties with different ripening times can spread risk. Petrus lacks that option: all principal lots are Merlot. Rain at flowering, summer heat, drought and autumn weather therefore affect the same variety, although parcels and vine ages do not respond identically.

Selection can reject weaker lots, but cannot restore a lost crop. The estate, for example, made no 1991 after severe spring frost. [@source.lemonde-petrus-2025] That exceptional decision shows where reputation management meets farming: sometimes rigorous selection means bottling nothing.
:::

## Grand Vin — one wine, no second label

Petrus makes one [[concept.grand-vin|Grand Vin]] and has no regular [[concept.second-wine|second wine]]. Lots that miss the selection are therefore not offered as a younger Petrus label. This strengthens the simplicity of the brand, but says nothing by itself about how much wine is made in a particular year.

In the glass, Petrus is often associated with ripe plum and black cherry, violets, spice and a dense texture that can move towards tobacco, earth and truffle-like aromas with bottle age. Great bottles combine richness with freshness and length. This is a historical style image, not a checklist: [[concept.vintage|vintage]], age, storage and bottle variation can transform the experience.
:::

:::detail{#oogst-vinificatie-en-opvoeding parent="grand-vin" depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.decanter-petrus-profile"}
### Harvest, vinification and maturation

Merlot is monitored and selected parcel by parcel. After harvest, sorting, fermentation temperature and skin contact govern extraction of colour and tannin. The wine then develops in oak barrels; the share of new wood and exact duration are cellar choices, not appellation rules.

The goal is not to force maximum power from every berry. Excessive extraction can dry the texture, late picking can cost freshness and conspicuous oak can obscure the fruit. Petrus’s reputation depends instead on the coherence of ripeness, concentration and tactile softness, not on one spectacular technique.
:::

:::detail{#ouder-worden-zonder-zekerheid parent="grand-vin" depth="advanced" source_refs="source.decanter-petrus-profile"}
### Ageing without certainty

Structure and concentration give strong vintages a long arc of development, but age is not a quality multiplier. Heat in transit, a dry cork, seepage or excessive oxygen can irreparably damage an expensive bottle. Two bottles from the same year may therefore taste very different after decades.

Decanting a young bottle may open its aroma but cannot make ripe tannin instantly old. An old bottle instead needs rest and careful pouring because of sediment and fragile aromas. The wisest serving rule is less romantic than the name: know the storage, inspect the bottle and taste attentively.
:::

:::section{#etiket-en-betekenis depth="intermediate" source_refs="source.lemonde-petrus-2025 source.decanter-petrus-profile"}
## Label and meaning — Saint Peter, Pomerol and a year

The label says Petrus without an accent and without the word château. Saint Peter holds the keys that refer to his name. The appellation Pomerol, [[concept.vintage|vintage]], volume and bottling information provide the practical reference points. “Grand Vin” is not a classification tier.

The pictured 1973 bottle also shows how a label becomes a historical document: owner’s name, typography and compulsory statements capture a moment. Do not treat an old bottle as a template for every current vintage. Labels, laws and ownership can change while the stable Petrus entity remains.
:::

:::key-idea{#centraal-inzicht depth="foundation"}
Petrus is not an officially ranked super-cru and its clay is not a magical flavouring. It is an exceptionally compact Merlot estate whose global status grew from place, selection, human continuity, distribution and scarcity — and whose every bottle remains subject to farming, time and storage.
:::
