# Content brief — Médoc

## Editorial identity

The Médoc is presented as the broad wine region on the peninsula between the Atlantic and the Gironde estuary, not as a synonym for AOP Médoc, Haut-Médoc or the entire “left bank”. Its fame rests on Cabernet-led red wines and classified châteaux, but the page must also show the less glamorous infrastructure that made them possible: drainage, mixed soils, ports, trade, labour and a living estuary.

## Completeness questions

- Can a first-time reader distinguish the Médoc region, AOP Médoc, AOP Haut-Médoc, the six communal appellations and the informal term left bank?
- Does the history explain settlement and viticulture before the famous estates, the seventeenth-century drainage works, the rise of Bordeaux investors and merchants, the 1855 classification, vineyard crises and the AOC era?
- Are ocean, forest, estuary, jalles, marshes, gravel rises and the wider soil mosaic connected without inventing boundary or terroir geometry?
- Are Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and their blending partners explained as site- and vintage-dependent roles rather than a fixed recipe?
- Is the dominance and diversity of red wine clear, while the recent legal arrival of white AOP Médoc is identified as a specific development rather than projected backwards?
- Can readers navigate all eight appellations and understand why Médoc and Haut-Médoc are not quality rungs?
- Are the 1855, Crus Bourgeois du Médoc and Crus Artisans du Médoc systems distinguished by scope, function and mutability?
- Do trade, courtiers, négociants, château identity and estate bottling explain how regional reputation reaches the market?
- Can readers interpret Médoc, cru and classification language on a label without confusing geography, rank and brand?
- Are contemporary heat, drought, disease pressure, water management and biodiversity treated as interacting questions?
- Do the visuals show both vineyard and estuary, rather than presenting one château as the whole region?
- Does each section answer its own question fully at the selected depth, with specific legal and historical claims cited proportionally?

## Depth calibration

- **Foundation / approximately WSET 2:** orientation, historical arc, landscape, main grapes, red-wine identity, eight-appellation structure, classifications at a glance and broad glass profile.
- **Intermediate / approximately WSET 3:** drainage and trade, soil and water functions, blending and élevage choices, distinctions among Médoc/Haut-Médoc/communal AOPs, label reading and serving.
- **Advanced:** limits of left-bank shorthand, human construction of terroir, scope and market effects of classifications, the new white AOP Médoc category and climate-adaptation trade-offs.

## Source strategy

Use INAO for current legal scopes and especially the 2025–2026 changes to AOP Médoc. Use the CIVB and Conseil des Vins du Médoc for broad orientation, then academic history, geology and landscape sources for claims requiring precision. Treat common regional synthesis as common knowledge; cite legal, dated, quantitative, contested and unusually specific claims. Never derive a boundary from prose or a tourism map.

## Visual plan

Reuse the Pauillac vineyard-and-estuary panorama as one concrete example of the eastern wine landscape, explicitly not as a portrait of all Médoc. Add an open-licensed photograph of fishing huts on the Gironde to make the estuary visible as a working landscape. Defer a regional map until verified Atlas geometry is available.
